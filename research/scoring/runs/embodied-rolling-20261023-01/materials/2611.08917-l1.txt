# PRISM: Primitive Routing via In-context Skill Mixing for Retentive VLA Adaptation

## Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models promise a single language-conditioned policy adaptable to many manipulation behaviors, yet deployment additionally requires skill retention and long-horizon composition: a policy must retain previously acquired skills and reuse them inside composite tasks whose behavior changes across phases. The standard recipe absorbs every new task into one shared update, which provides neither a parameter-level locus for reusable skills nor a mechanism for invoking each skill along the trajectory. We observe that this missing structure is latent rather than absent: per-skill low-rank updates of a pretrained VLA occupy a robustly distinguishable, low-dimensional subspace, and a single composite-task LoRA concentrates roughly half of its Frobenius energy in this subspace, against a near-zero matched random-rotation control. Building on this geometry, we introduce PRISM, which represents reusable skills as parameter-level primitives: each skill family is stored as a dedicated low-rank action-expert adapter in a primitive bank, primitives are augmented with skill-specific low-rank residuals and composed at inference by a per-chunk VLM-conditioned router that reads the prefix representation to track task progress, and the router is supervised by per-chunk labels from a deterministic, task-conditioned, action-derived oracle. During composite training, the vision encoder, the language LoRA, the skill residuals, the router, and the shared action projections and time MLP are updated; every primitive is held frozen, so the base per-skill action-expert adapters are preserved by construction. On RoboCasa365, PRISM achieves 56.43% atomic-skill success and reaches 20.00% on long-horizon composite tasks, while a standard $π_{0.5}$-LoRA two-stage fine-tune reaches 17.86% atomic and 17.50% composite success. We additionally validate PRISM on an AgileX PIPER real-robot setup.

## 1 Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models [ 6 , 5 , 20 , 36 , 4 , 3 , 35 ] are emerging as a general substrate for language-conditioned robot manipulation: a single pretrained policy, steered by natural-language instruction, can in principle be adapted toward a broad range of downstream behaviors. Yet deployment exposes two failure modes of the standard single-policy adaptation paradigm: sequentially fine-tuning the policy on a new behavior degrades performance on previously acquired ones, forcing a choice between retraining from scratch and losing capabilities; and long-horizon tasks that recombine known sub-skills must still be fit end-to-end as if their constituent skills had not already been mastered—an object-washing task, for instance, requires end-to-end training even though it chains a lever-like interaction (to activate a water source) with a pick-and-place-like interaction (to move the object through the stream). We refer to the two requirements that address these failures as skill retention and long-horizon composition , and ask how a VLA should be parameterized to support both. The standard adaptation paradigm is monolithic: a downstream task is absorbed into a shared set of model parameters, with no explicit representation of which part of the update corresponds to which reusable behavior. This parameterization may be adequate for mastering one task in isolation, but it lacks two handles required for deployment. The first is where : where is a previously learned skill localized in the model update? Without such a parameter-level locus, later updates can interfere with the directions that supported earlier behaviors. Common defenses either allocate additional task-specific parameters, which limits reuse, or constrain updates globally, which can slow acquisition without identifying the skill-specific directions that require protection. The second handle is when : when during a long-horizon trajectory should the policy invoke each skill? A composite task cycles through distinct phases of object interaction, motion, and sub-goal. Without a mechanism for switching behavioral regimes, an undifferentiated policy must fit each composite end-to-end as if it were a fresh task. Long-horizon demonstrations are not merely longer action sequences but reflect the selection and recombination of behaviors the policy has already acquired, and a policy with no parameter-level skill identity has no way to interpret such a trajectory in relation to its prior skills. We find that this missing structure is more latent than absent. When each primitive skill family is adapted on its own through a small low-rank update [ 17 ] , the family of per-skill update tensors does not diffuse uniformly throughout parameter space: it occupies a robust, semantically clustered subspace that reproduces across independently trained checkpoints (Section 3.3 )—low-dimensional relative to the full action-expert parameter space, while remaining nearly full-rank across primitive families. Moreover, when a single LoRA is fit on a composite-task dataset, a substantial fraction of its update tensor lies in the linear span of the per-skill update tensors (Section 3.3 ): composite-task behavior is, to a first approximation, partially expressible as a combination of per-skill primitives. However, when atomic and composite behaviors are absorbed into a single shared adapter trained sequentially, the skill-specific directions learned during atomic adaptation are no longer separately addressable, and atomic-skill performance degrades as composite training proceeds. We refer to this loss of per-skill addressability as primitive collapse . This observation suggests a constructive parameterization. If reusable skills occupy distinguishable low-dimensional update directions, then mixture weights over these updates can be trained to serve as skill-level control variables rather than arbitrary adapter coefficients. Later adaptation need not rewrite a shared update; it can preserve skill-localized slots while learning to route over them. Likewise, long-horizon execution need not fit composite behavior as an independent policy; it can select or mix primitives over time as the task progresses. Under this view, retaining and composing skills reduce to routing over preserved primitives : once a skill is represented as a primitive slot, executing a long-horizon behavior activates the appropriate primitive or mixture at each phase. Building on this observation, we introduce PRISM , a VLA adaptation framework that turns reusable skills into first-class parameter-level objects. PRISM has three coupled components: a primitive bank that stores each skill family as its own low-rank adapter over the action expert, augmented with a skill-specific residual during composite training; a prefix-conditioned router that emits a per-chunk mixture over these primitive–residual branches; and a deterministic action-derived labeler that supplies per-chunk supervision for the router. ## 4 Method
Overview. At inference, PRISM takes a language instruction \ell and camera images, and outputs action chunks of H=10 actions. It operationalizes the skill geometry of Section 3 with three components: a primitive bank that stores per-skill LoRA slots augmented with trainable residuals during composite training, a labeling oracle that provides skill supervision, and a VLM-conditioned router that selects mixtures of the primitive–residual branches. At chunk boundary t , the router reads prefix h_{t} and emits \alpha(t)=r_{\theta}(h_{t})\in\Delta^{N-1} . These weights combine adapted attention contributions and complete skill-specific FFN outputs, as defined below. The primitives remain frozen during composite training, while their residuals provide additional adaptation capacity. Skill Primitives and Residual Adaptation. For each atomic skill s_{i} , we learn a rank- 32 action-expert primitive P_{i}=B_{i}A_{i} from demonstrations D_{s_{i}} under one-hot routing, so each sample trains its corresponding primitive. The bank \mathcal{B}=\{(B_{i},A_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N} consists of disjoint parameter blocks: for i\neq j , (B_{i},A_{i}) and (B_{j},A_{j}) share no parameters with each other or with the frozen action-expert backbone W_{A,0} . The visual encoder and shared language LoRA sit upstream of all primitives. After Phase 1, every primitive is frozen, giving a parameter-level answer to the where question: skill s_{i} ’s atomic update remains (B_{i},A_{i}) . Composite training introduces a rank- 10 residual R_{i}=D_{i}C_{i} for every skill, attached to the action expert’s attention and feed-forward projections alongside P_{i} . The residual scaling factor is one; C_{i} is randomly initialized and D_{i} is initialized to zero. Thus, adding the residual initially preserves each branch’s output under the same routing weights.
