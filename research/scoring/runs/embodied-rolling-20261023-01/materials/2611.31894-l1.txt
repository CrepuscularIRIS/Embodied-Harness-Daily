===ABSTRACT===
Large language model (LLM) coding agents can iteratively self-improve robot manipulation skills in simulation benchmarks, yet real-world tasks provide neither ground-truth success scorers nor resettable practice environments. Our key observation is that a single real demonstration simultaneously specifies scene geometry, defines the terminal success criterion, and serves as a physical reference trajectory for verifying world fidelity---all decoupled from the source robot's kinematics. We present {RESET}: we reconstruct a contact-rich world and derive a frozen read-only criterion from one demonstration ({R}ebuild); check that world against the demonstration by open-loop replay and silhouette consistency ({E}xamine); evolve modular white-box task graphs and skill code from per-node failure evidence ({S}elf-improve); deploy frozen code on real robots with zero runtime LLM calls ({E}xecute); and share the verified world and criterion with unseen robots without new demonstrations ({T}ransfer). On 8 manipulation tasks, each rebuilt from one real demonstration, RESET achieves 97% average success on unseen layouts in simulation, whereas zero-shot code reaches 27%, per-task fine-tuned pi0.5 policies 31%, and Diffusion Policy 50%; injecting reconstruction defects shows that practice tolerates many reconstruction errors, that geometric errors can still stall or mislead it, and that the check flags most of them while physical randomization covers the rest. Without any target demonstrations, 3 heterogeneous robots acquire the same task skills in the shared world: a robot with similar arms copies 95% of its skill functions unchanged from the source robot, while robots with different arms and hands write their own. In real-robot experiments, the resulting programs run on 4 robots and succeed in 80% of 280 trials.

===INTRO===
LLM-driven coding agents are emerging as a compelling paradigm for acquiring robot manipulation skills. Whereas hand-crafted or one-shot synthesized control programs [ 10 ] struggle to scale across contact-rich tasks and diverse robots, closed-loop recursive self-improvement (RSI) lets an agent inspect failure evidence, revise control code, and retain verified improvements, steadily advancing performance on simulation benchmarks such as RoboDojo [ 24 , 19 , 9 , 15 , 6 , 2 ] . Yet this paradigm relies on a strong hidden premise: human engineers have already built the simulation scene, reset mechanism, and ground-truth success scorer. Physical tasks supply none of these. Recent attempts to run self-improvement directly on real robots [ 22 ] rely on manual or heuristic self-checks and custom reset fixtures, requiring hours to tens of hours of physical trial-and-error per task without generalizing across embodiments. Scaling code self-improvement to the real world thus hinges on a foundational question: without a hand-engineered benchmark, where do a reliable success criterion and a trustworthy practice world come from? Reconstructing real scenes into simulation, practicing inside them, and transferring back to the real world (Real-to-Sim-to-Real) offers a natural route [ 17 , 5 , 4 ] , but existing pipelines fall short in three ways. First, reconstructed worlds lack pre-training verification : prior methods proceed straight from scene reconstruction to policy learning, leaving world fidelity to be discovered only post hoc via real-world failure [ 12 ] . Second, success criteria rely on manual reward design and invite reward hacking : under multi-round code search, agents readily exploit loose criteria with unintended behaviors [ 17 ] . Third, demonstrations remain entangled with source kinematics : imitating recorded trajectories binds task knowledge to one robot morphology, invalidating the demonstration whenever degrees of freedom (DoF) or end-effectors change [ 17 , 4 ] . Our core insight is that a single successful real-world demonstration simultaneously provides a blueprint of the world, a success criterion, and a physical reference for verifying that world—all independent of the source robot’s kinematics. The initial RGB-D frame captures scene geometry, while the terminal state specifies exact geometric completion relations. Crucially, because the demonstration is a known-successful physical trajectory, an accurate world should reproduce object grasps and motion under open-loop contact replay of the recorded joint commands and align rendered object silhouettes with observed masks. Moreover, by distilling the demonstration into an embodiment-agnostic specification ( world + frozen criterion + local contact priors ) rather than imitating its trajectory, an agent can write modular control code on its own. This both resolves the scalability bottleneck of manual code-skill authoring and exploits the white-box nature of code skills—explicit geometric guards, stage-level verification, and deterministic execution—for long-horizon stability. Guided by this insight, we propose RESET ( R ebuild, E xamine, S elf-improve, E xecute, T ransfer; Fig. 1 ). The name reflects what the method restores to a real task: a world that can be reset and practiced in at will. Rebuild reconstructs an interactive MuJoCo scene from one RGB-D demonstration and extracts a read-only terminal scorer. Examine checks the rebuilt world against the demonstration by dynamic replay and silhouette alignment before practice, leaving unidentifiable mass and friction to randomized practice. Self-improve evolves an object-centric task graph and white-box skill library from per-node failure evidence under diagnostic/hold-out gating. Execute runs frozen code online with zero LLM calls, low cost, and node-level debuggability. Transfer hands the verified world and criterion unchanged to target robots that adapt only a thin hardware layer. We evaluate RESET across 4 diverse embodiments and 8 real manipulation tasks. From one demonstration on the source robot, RESET achieves 97% success on unseen layouts in simulation, where zero-shot code and per-task fine-tuned \pi_{0.5} policies stay near 30% and Diffusion Policy reaches 50%. Controlled defect injection shows that practice tolerates many reconstruction errors. A geometric error, however, can make a task unsolvable or hide a failure during practice, and it changes what a simulated success rate means; Examine flags most such errors, and physical randomization covers mass and friction. Without any target demonstration, Dual-arm Franka, ARX LIFT2, and Unitree G1 acquire their own skills in the shared world. The final programs succeed in 80% of 280 real-robot trials on 4 physical platforms. Our main contributions are threefold: • A framework from one demonstration to skills across embodiments.
