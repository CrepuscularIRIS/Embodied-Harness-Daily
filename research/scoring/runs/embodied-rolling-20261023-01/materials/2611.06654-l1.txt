# Compounding Physical RSI: Turning Execution Failure into Inherited Capability

## Abstract
Generalist robot policies are trained on static offline datasets, yet physical manipulation unfolds sequentially across stages. Because a weakness in a downstream stage remains unobservable until the policy passes earlier ones, each resolved bottleneck exposes the next hidden failure mode. This staged structure calls for Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI), in which a policy iteratively executes, diagnoses failures, and updates itself. Moreover, because task completion behaves like a product of stage-level pass rates, recursive gains can multiply across generations. In physical environments, three bottlenecks obstruct compounding: terminal execution feedback rarely localizes the stage that caused a failure, failed trajectories do not supply the corrective action, and updating shared parameters can degrade previously mastered skills. We propose Compounding Physical RSI, a self-evolving policy framework that turns execution failures into heritable capability. First, automated capability-gap assessment traces failed rollouts to the task stage where progression breaks down and formulates a structured improvement objective. Second, conditioned on this objective, our remedy stage curates validated, stage-relevant experience and refines the policy via behavior-regularized offline reinforcement learning, without requiring costly online rollouts at each optimization step. Third, to safeguard existing skills, our retention stage constructs weight-space candidates between the inherited reference policy and the refined policy, evaluates them across the task suite, and promotes the best candidate as the reference state for the next generation. Evaluated across all 42 tasks of the RoboDojo-Sim benchmark, CSU-AI-07 ranks first on the leaderboard with a three-seed mean Overall Score of 26.15 and Success Rate of 20.70%, outperforming the next-best entry by 5.92 points and 5.82 percentage points.

## 1 Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models have substantially advanced generalist robot manipulation by unifying visual perception, language grounding, and action generation within a single policy [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ] . Recent efforts have extended this paradigm across diverse embodiments [ 6 , 7 , 8 ] , refined spatial representations and fine-tuning efficiency [ 9 , 10 ] , and scaled pre-training to over 100K hours of real-world trajectories [ 11 ] . Yet despite these advances in pre-training scale, a generalist policy still encounters many of its fundamental capability limits only after static training has ended and closed-loop physical execution begins. This limitation stems from the sequential, stage-gated nature of manipulation. A robot must approach an object before grasping it, and must establish a stable grasp before transporting or placing it. Consequently, a deficiency in a downstream stage remains masked until the policy reliably passes all preceding stages: a policy that fails during grasping never reveals how it would fail during transport, because its rollouts never reach that state. Moreover, the failure modes encountered during transport depend directly on the contact configuration produced by the grasp, as each policy induces its own closed-loop state distribution [ 12 ] . We define the earliest task stage that a policy cannot yet pass reliably as its capability frontier . Because behavior beyond this frontier remains unobserved during execution, a fixed dataset cannot anticipate how the evolving policy will fail once it crosses the current barrier. Each successful fix advances the capability frontier and exposes the next latent failure mode. A moving capability frontier naturally motivates Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) for embodied intelligence. Rooted in classical formulations of self-referential learning [ 13 , 14 ] and renewed in modern foundation-model research [ 15 ] , RSI recognizes that when a policy’s next bottleneck becomes visible only after the current one is resolved, improvement cannot be reduced to a single static training run. Instead, the robot must iteratively execute, diagnose its own failures, learn from them, and execute again so that each generation uncovers the empirical evidence required by the next. Physical RSI formalizes this paradigm for embodied systems, where contact-rich physical experience drives persistent policy evolution [ 16 , 17 ] . Recent studies have explored key facets of embodied self-improvement, including inference-time self-correction and subtask backtracking [ 18 ] , deployment-time adaptation and memory consolidation [ 19 , 20 ] , and iterative policy updates via autonomous data collection or residual reinforcement learning [ 8 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] . While these efforts show that a robot policy can improve over a single update or within narrow task settings, the central open question is whether such improvements can compound across generations. The potential for compounding follows directly from the staged structure of manipulation. Because a task succeeds only when every constituent stage succeeds, overall task completion behaves like a product of stage-level pass rates. In such a product, stage-level gains multiply: stabilizing a grasp not only lifts the completion rate of every subsequent stage, but also sends a larger fraction of rollouts across the capability frontier, generating richer downstream evidence for the next generation. A recursive loop can therefore achieve super-additive progress across rounds, provided that each generation builds reliably upon the verified gains of its predecessor. However, making Physical RSI compound in practice is hindered by three fundamental bottlenecks that mirror the lifecycle of a policy update. First, execution feedback suffers from opaque temporal credit assignment [ 24 ] : a terminal failure flag indicates that a trial failed, but rarely localizes which stage broke, as visible failure often manifests long after the erroneous action that caused it. Second, failed rollouts lack corrective supervision: even a precisely localized failure shows what went wrong without specifying the counterfactual action the policy should have executed. Third, multi-task gains are fragile under shared weights: because a generalist policy encodes all skills in one parameter set, an unconstrained update that repairs one failure stage can silently degrade previously mastered tasks [ 25 ] . Under the same multiplicative structure that amplifies stage-level gains, such cross-task regressions multiply losses, causing naive recursive loops to accumulate policy churn rather than lasting capability. We argue that the core challenge of Physical RSI is therefore achieving compounding —ensuring that every generation diagnoses its active frontier, resolves it effectively, and inherits verified capabilities without regression. ## 2 Compounding Physical RSI
2.1 From improvement to compounding The stage-gated structure of manipulation imposes three strict requirements on a recursive improvement loop. First, because downstream weaknesses remain hidden behind earlier bottlenecks, improvement is most consequential where execution currently stalls; the loop must therefore accurately localize the active capability frontier. Second, because failed rollouts carry no explicit corrective labels, the loop must synthesize targeted supervision to cross that frontier. Third, because stage-level regressions multiply just as readily as stage-level gains, the loop must preserve previously mastered capabilities when absorbing new updates. Checking these three requirements across successive generations yields a structural definition of compounding: Compounding Physical RSI. A recursive improvement loop is compounding when each generation is diagnosed from its own closed-loop execution, refined specifically against that diagnosis, and promoted only as a behaviorally verified state that the next generation can inherit and build upon. The three clauses of this definition map one-to-one to the three stages of our framework: Diagnosis (Section 3 ), Remedy (Section 4 ), and Retention (Section 5 ). Consistent with recent formulations of Physical RSI [ 16 ] , the term physical denotes that the loop is driven by closed-loop, contact-rich execution governed by physical dynamics—whether in physics simulation or on physical hardware.
