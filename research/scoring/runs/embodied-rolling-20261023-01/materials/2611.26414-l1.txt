===ABSTRACT===
Representative vision-language navigation (VLN) methods typically couple a vision-language model (VLM) brain with a learnable action head and fine-tune the VLM brain on navigation-domain data to predict geometric goals. However, such adaptation can substantially alter base VLM weights and degrade general visual capabilities. We introduce ZeroVLN, a slow-fast navigation framework that achieves effective navigation with zero navigation-domain SFT of the VLM brain. Rather than teaching the brain to predict geometric goals, we equip it with a semantic tool-calling interface for conveying movement decisions to an action expert in natural language. We first adapt the action expert to carry out these movement requests, then jointly improve the brain's movement decisions and the action expert's continuous execution through closed-loop reinforcement learning (RL). Trained on the same navigation episodes, ZeroVLN substantially outperforms representative SFT-based hierarchical VLN methods on standard VLN-CE (continuous environments) benchmarks, while inducing only minor changes to the brain's weights and largely preserving its general capabilities. To our knowledge, ZeroVLN is the first slow-fast VLN framework with zero navigation-domain SFT of the VLM brain, and the first to jointly optimize its slow and fast policies through closed-loop agentic RL in VLN-CE.

## 2 Architecture
Semantic Tool Calls and Contextual Queries. As illustrated in Figure 2 , we separate movement decisions from continuous motion execution through a semantic tool-calling interface. Rather than predicting geometric goals, the VLM brain makes movement decisions via run_action_head(target, where, plan) , specifying a target, its relative location, and a local plan for the action expert. The brain can also inspect the scene through turn(direction, degrees) or terminate navigation through stop(reason) , with prompts and tool schemas detailed in Appendix G.1 . At slow decision round t , the brain policy \pi_{\theta} generates a tool call y_{t} from multimodal context x_{t} , including the navigation instruction, RGB observations, tool history, and trajectory trace. For movement calls, q_{t} denotes the natural-language arguments of y_{t} . To convey the request and its navigation context to the action expert, we concatenate learnable query embeddings Q with the embedded context and tool call. The VLM contextualizes these queries, whose final-layer hidden states Q_{t} provide the action expert with semantic conditioning Wei et al. (2025a) : Q_{t}=\left[\operatorname{VLM}_{\theta}\left(\operatorname{Embed}(x_{t},y_{t})\oplus Q\right)\right]_{Q}. (1) Visual Execution at a Faster Timescale. While the brain specifies the intended movement, the action expert executes the call over multiple fast cycles. Throughout each call, the semantic condition Q_{t} and reference image I_{t}^{\mathrm{ref}} remain fixed, while RGB observations I_{t,k} are refreshed at each cycle Wei et al. (2025a) . A frozen visual encoder and trainable fusion module combine both images into visual features F_{t,k} . The semantic adapter P_{1} projects Q_{t} into the visual conditioning space and adds a semantic-channel embedding, while P_{2} adds a visual-channel embedding to F_{t,k} . Concatenating the two streams forms the action expert’s conditioning input: C_{t,k}=P_{1}(Q_{t})\oplus P_{2}(F_{t,k}). (2) Conditioned on C_{t,k} , the continuous-action expert \pi_{\phi} integrates the flow velocities predicted by f_{\phi} to generate an H -step action sequence. After inverse normalization, each action specifies relative planar displacements and a heading change in the local frame defined by the agent’s pose at the start of each cycle. At each cycle, we execute only the first h\leq H steps, then refresh the observation and predict the next sequence, adapting motion while preserving the brain’s intent. After each movement call, visual and trajectory feedback, including blocked motion or collisions, allows the brain to continue, inspect the scene, revise its request, or stop. Figure 3 details this execution path: N_{q} semantic queries and N_{v} visual tokens condition an L_{d} -layer decoder, with an L_{m} -layer flow-time MLP and N_{f} flow integration steps. Appendix B.1 provides further details on the action expert’s architecture and execution.

## 3 Learning to Execute Semantic Requests
Request-Grounded Anchoring. Our semantic interface also changes how action expert supervision must be constructed. Geometric-interface VLN frameworks can derive training conditions directly from scene meshes or ground-truth trajectories Wei et al. (2025a) ; Gong et al. (2026) ; An et al. (2024) , whereas our action expert follows natural-language requests issued online by the brain itself. Directly constructing semantic requests may not match the brain’s actual request distribution, leaving the action expert exposed to out-of-distribution instructions at inference. Generating requests online along fixed trajectories also leaves the action labels independent of the requested movement. We report results for these alternatives, including CAST-inspired augmentation Glossop et al. (2025) and request transfer in Appendix D.2 . Built upon these insights, we propose a request-grounded data construction strategy that generates trajectories from the brain’s actual requests . To realize this, we design a privileged semantic planner that uses a mesh-derived semantic map Miao et al. (2026) and training-only geometry to ground each request q_{t} at the agent’s current state s_{t} into a reachable local goal g_{t}^{\star} , from which a reference trajectory \rho_{t} is planned. The resulting execution-verified trajectories are paired with their originating requests to provide semantically aligned supervision for action expert adaptation. Further details of the semantic planner are provided in Appendix B.2 . Execution-Prefix Supervision. These request-grounded trajectories provide aligned training targets, but supervision must also match the action expert’s execution criteria. For each verified trajectory \rho_{t} , we construct H -step reference action chunks U_{t,k}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times d_{a}} across K fast execution cycles, with d_{a} action components per step, and pair them with the corresponding conditions C_{t,k} . Additional details on action construction are provided in Appendix B.3 . Once new observations arrive, the remaining predictions are discarded, creating a mismatch between training and execution.
