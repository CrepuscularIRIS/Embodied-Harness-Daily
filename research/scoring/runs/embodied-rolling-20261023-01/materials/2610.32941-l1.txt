# If at First You Don't Succeed, Backtrack: Lightweight Recovery for Frozen VLAs

## Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models perform well on robotic manipulation, yet remain brittle once their own actions carry them into states their training data barely covers. Recovering from such failures usually requires recovery demonstrations, policy retraining, or a separate model at inference time. We introduce Backtrack, a lightweight failure detection and recovery mechanism for frozen VLAs that needs none of these. A small failure prediction head, trained only on outcome-labeled rollouts of the frozen policy, reads the VLA's existing internal representations to estimate whether the current episode will fail. When the predicted failure probability exceeds a threshold, a controller pauses the policy, retraces the arm's recently achieved motion, and hands control back to the unchanged policy so it can retry from an earlier configuration. The head adds only 0.48-0.88% to the policy's parameter count. We evaluate Backtrack on LIBERO with three VLAs spanning two action-generation families: SmolVLA and $π$0.5, which use flow-matching action experts, and the autoregressive $π$0-FAST. On LIBERO-Long, the suite of long-horizon multi-step tasks and the one on which all three policies fail most often, Backtrack reduces each policy's failure rate by 19-48%, raising success by up to 7.4 percentage points; on the three shorter suites, where control success is already 85-99%, its effect is smaller. On MetaWorld, all twelve controller configurations tested with SmolVLA improve on the unassisted policy.

## 1 Introduction
Recent advances in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have spurred the development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models ( Black et al., 2025a ; Pertsch et al., 2025 ; Shukor et al., 2025 ) . These models typically use pretrained VLMs as a backbone, leveraging their visual and language representations to condition the generation of robot actions. Yet even large VLAs trained on diverse, multi-embodiment robotics datasets remain brittle outside their training distribution: modest changes to camera viewpoint, robot initialization, or scene composition can sharply reduce success rates ( Fei et al., 2026 ) . This challenge is particularly relevant for compact VLAs such as SmolVLA, whose VLM backbone benefits from broad vision-language pretraining, but whose robotics training consists of fewer than 30,000 episodes of community-contributed demonstrations collected primarily on a single robot embodiment ( Shukor et al., 2025 ) . Out-of-distribution states are not only something a VLA encounters; they are also something it can create. Robot imitation datasets are built primarily from successful demonstrations and contain comparatively little data showing how to recover once something has gone wrong ( Dai et al., 2025 ; Shukor et al., 2025 ) . For instance, when a VLA drops an object, knocks something over, or otherwise makes a mistake, it can therefore place itself in a state poorly represented in its training data. Simply allowing more execution time leaves most failures unresolved: on LIBERO-Long, doubling the episode step budget increases success by only 2.3–3.7 percentage points across the three evaluated VLAs (Appendix A.3 ). One remedy is to give the policy the data it is missing: collect demonstrations of failures and their recoveries, then train on them ( Dai et al., 2025 ) . But collecting recovery data is expensive, and the cost may need to be paid again for every new embodiment and task. Instead, we ask whether part of the distribution shift can be reversed directly. After a mistake, both the scene and the robot may have changed: objects may have been displaced, while the arm has moved into an unfamiliar configuration. Changes to the scene are difficult to undo, since restoring a displaced object is itself a manipulation problem. The arm configuration, however, is under our control. This suggests a simple recovery strategy: retrace the arm’s motion along the path that led to failure, in the hope of returning toward an earlier configuration from which the unchanged policy can successfully retry. In this work, we introduce Backtrack, a lightweight failure detection and recovery mechanism for frozen VLAs (Figure 1 ). Similar to SAFE ( Gu et al., 2025 ) , a small failure prediction head reads features the policy has already computed and estimates the probability that the current episode will end in failure; when that probability exceeds a threshold, a controller retraces the arm’s recent motion and returns control to the policy. The only supervision Backtrack needs is whether autonomous rollouts of the frozen policy succeed or fail: no teleoperated recovery demonstrations, no policy retraining, and no second model at inference. The head adds just 0.48–0.88% to the policy’s parameters. We evaluate Backtrack on three policies with two different action-generation mechanisms: SmolVLA ( Shukor et al., 2025 ) and \pi 0.5 ( Black et al., 2025a ) use flow-matching action experts, while \pi 0-FAST ( Pertsch et al., 2025 ) generates discrete action tokens autoregressively. On LIBERO-Long ( Liu et al., 2023a ) , the suite on which all three policies fail most often, Backtrack reduces failure rates by 19% to 48%; averaged over all four LIBERO suites, the reduction is 20–26%. On MetaWorld ( Yu et al., 2020 ) , where we evaluate SmolVLA on a second benchmark with a different embodiment, all twelve controller configurations we test improve on the unassisted policy.

## 3 Method
3.1 Failure Prediction Head We attach a Failure Prediction Head (FPH) to the frozen policy. The FPH is a multi-head attention pooling (MAP) layer over the policy’s own token features, followed by a three-layer feed-forward network (FFN) that outputs a single scalar: the predicted probability that the current episode will end in failure. Where the features come from. The FPH consumes activations the policy already computes during normal action generation, so it requires no additional forward pass through the backbone. We consider three feature taps: the prefix embeddings the VLM takes as input ( pre ), the VLM’s output representation of that same prefix ( post ), and post concatenated with the action expert’s own hidden states ( post_expert ). Unless stated otherwise, all reported results use post . \pi 0-FAST has no separate action expert: the VLM emits the action tokens itself. Its pre tap is the same as for the other two policies, the prefix embeddings the VLM takes as input. Its post tap is the VLM’s representation of that prefix after the prefill, just before the first action token is generated. The head is evaluated once per action chunk and reuses activations already present in the forward pass, so its only marginal cost is the MAP layer and the FFN. Its size depends on the width of the features it reads rather than on the size of the policy, so the relative overhead is largest on the smallest policy: it adds at most 0.88% of the policy’s parameters, on the 450M SmolVLA, and as little as 0.48% on the 3.6B \pi 0.5 (Table 1 ). Training. The head is trained on rollouts of the frozen policy, with each frame labeled by the outcome of the episode it belongs to. Rollouts are collected across all four LIBERO suites, and we sample with equal weight over the eight (suite, label) cells rather than from the natural distribution. This matters because failures are distributed very unevenly across suites: for \pi 0-FAST, LIBERO-Long supplies 56% of all failure frames and LIBERO-Object only 7%, and the imbalance is larger still for SmolVLA. Sampling from the raw distribution would give one head per policy that is effectively specialized to the suite its policy fails on most, whereas we want a single head that serves all four.
