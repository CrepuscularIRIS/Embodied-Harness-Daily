# TILDE: Teacher-in-the-Loop Distillation from Embodied Foundation Models

## Abstract
Large-scale action pretraining equips Embodied Foundation Models (EFMs) with strong manipulation capabilities, with particular strengths in generalization and closed-loop robustness, but reproducing such pretraining requires substantial robot data and computation. To transfer these capabilities from pretrained EFMs to policies without action pretraining, we introduce TILDE (Teacher-In-the-Loop Distillation from EFMs), which synergizes on-policy distillation at student-induced states with learning from teacher-executed recovery trajectories. We further propose AutoDAgger, an autonomous data aggregation pipeline in which a robotic reward model is utilized to monitor task progress and trigger teacher intervention, thereby collecting recovery experience without human monitoring or manual control. AutoDAgger accelerates recovery-trajectory collection by more than $2\times$ compared with the human-in-the-loop method. By unifying learning from demonstrations, on-policy teacher supervision, and teacher's corrective behavior, TILDE improves task performance by up to $4.1\times$ over behavior cloning across three real-world tasks and supports effective cross-architecture distillation.

## 1 Introduction
Embodied Foundation Models (EFMs), including Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models ( Zhang et al., 2025a ; Shao et al., 2025 ; Ma et al., 2024 ; Sapkota et al., 2025 ) , have demonstrated remarkable manipulation capabilities and generalization. However, these capabilities often rely on action pretraining on large-scale robot datasets ( Bu et al., 2025 ; Padalkar et al., 2024 ; Walke et al., 2023 ; Khazatsky et al., 2024 ) , making it costly to endow new policy architectures, particularly efficient models, with comparable capabilities ( Yu et al., 2025 ; Guan et al., 2025 ) . Reproducing foundation-scale pretraining requires access to sufficiently diverse, high-quality robot data and substantial computational resources, which is prohibitively expensive in many research and deployment settings. Policies without large-scale action pretraining ( Lin et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026a ; Community, 2026 ) can still achieve competitive performance on standard simulation benchmarks such as LIBERO-Spatial and LIBERO-Object ( Liu et al., 2023 ) . However, our pilot study suggests that this apparent parity does not consistently extend to more challenging settings. As shown in Figure 1 , substantially larger gaps emerge on long-horizon tasks in LIBERO-10, under visual and robot-initial-state perturbations ( Fei et al., 2025 ) , and in real-world tasks. These observations are consistent with action pretraining providing benefits beyond nominal action prediction, particularly improved closed-loop robustness under challenging and out-of-distribution conditions. This motivates a natural question: Can such capabilities be transferred from a pretrained EFM to a student policy without repeating expensive action pretraining? Policy distillation offers an intuitive route by transferring teacher knowledge in a task-specific domain ( Rusu et al., 2015 ; Dong et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025b ) . However, for closed-loop policies, effective distillation depends not only on what actions are supervised, but also on where supervision is provided. When distillation is restricted to a fixed demonstration dataset ( Figure 1 (a)), supervision remains concentrated on the nominal distribution. During closed-loop deployment, compounding student errors and environmental stochasticity can drive the policy into out-of-distribution states. This distribution mismatch between training and inference leaves the student insufficiently prepared to handle execution deviations ( Yan et al., 2026 ; Hu et al., 2026 ) . Interactive imitation learning mitigates this mismatch by querying expert supervision at states induced by the learner ( Ross et al., 2011 ) . In On-Policy Distillation (OPD) ( Agarwal et al., 2024 ; Agarwal et al., 2023 ) , the student interacts with the environment while the teacher provides action targets at student-visited states ( Figure 1 (b)). This expands supervision to student-induced rollout states, but the queried teacher actions are not executed ( Zhong et al., 2026 ; Wan et al., 2026 ) . Consequently, OPD does not observe the subsequent states that would arise following the teacher’s correction. Human-in-the-loop approaches such as HG-DAgger ( Kelly et al., 2019 ) ( Figure 1 (c)) can collect recovery trajectories but require continuous human monitoring and teleoperated takeover ( Murray et al., 2026 ; Xu et al., 2025 ) , severely limiting data scalability. To address these limitations, we introduce TILDE ( T eacher- I n-the- L oop D istillation from E FMs), a policy distillation framework that transfers teacher knowledge over three complementary data distributions: human demonstration, student-induced rollouts, and recovery trajectories ( Figure 1 (d)). Central to TILDE is AutoDAgger, an autonomous teacher-in-the-loop data aggregation pipeline. An off-the-shelf robotic reward model monitors student execution, while the execution manager uses its feedback to trigger intervention, after which the EFM teacher directly takes control and executes a recovery trajectory toward task completion. Automated progress monitoring removes the need for expensive human oversight, while teacher policy takeover avoids the reaction and teleoperation overhead of human intervention, substantially enhancing recovery-data collection throughput. Given the aggregated data, TILDE applies supervision according to the sample source. Offline demonstrations and teacher recovery trajectories provide both behavior-cloning targets from executed actions and teacher distillation targets, while student rollout states use on-policy teacher supervision only. Importantly, all distillation is performed directly in the common action space and requires no alignment of internal representations, allowing the framework to accommodate heterogeneous teacher and student architectures. During closed-loop execution, the policy is repeatedly queried using updated observations. DAgger. Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) ( Ross et al., 2011 ) is an interactive imitation learning algorithm that addresses the distribution shift between offline demonstrations \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{demo}} and learner rollouts \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{roll}} by collecting expert action labels at learner-induced states. In the representative human-in-the-loop variants, the human expert monitors the execution progress and takes control when the learner’s behavior requires correction by providing recovery actions {\bm{a}}^{\star} at the encountered observation {\bm{s}} . The resulting labeled samples ({\bm{s}},{\bm{l}},{\bm{a}}^{\star}) are added to an aggregated dataset \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{rec}} , which is used to update the learner through behavior cloning. 3.2 Overview TILDE transfers task-relevant capabilities from a capable and robust teacher policy \pi_{T} to a student policy \pi_{S} . With a base human demonstration dataset \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{demo}} , the teacher policy is finetuned from the pretrained EFM on the dataset to adapt to specific embodiment and target task, and remains frozen in the framework. The student policy is initialized from a pretrained backbone ( e.g. , VLM in a VLA model) but receives no robot action pretraining, and is trained on \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{demo}} using standard behavior cloning. TILDE consists of two stages: (1) In the AutoDAgger data collection, the student executes the task while a task monitor estimates progress and an execution manager triggers teacher intervention when needed. The teacher takes over to perform recovery, and only episodes with confirmed teacher recovery are combined with the student rollout and added to the aggregated dataset.
