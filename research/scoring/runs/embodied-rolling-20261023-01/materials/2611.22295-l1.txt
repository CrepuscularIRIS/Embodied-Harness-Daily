===ABSTRACT===
Retrieval-augmented vision-language-action (VLA) policies fill their context with the demonstrations whose frames look most like the current observation under frozen DINOv2 embeddings. We ask whether visual similarity selects the demonstrations that make the policy succeed. For a frozen retrieval-augmented VLA (RICL on $π_0$-FAST, LIBERO), we make the demonstration pool swappable at run time and score each demonstration's utility by regressing the closed-loop success of 60 fixed-size pools on their membership. On initial states disjoint from those used for scoring, pools of the five highest-utility demonstrations succeed on 66.4% of 760 paired episodes across 19 tasks, pools of the five most similar on 55.7%, and random pools on 55.5%. Utility beats similarity by 10.8 points ($p=3.2\times10^{-6}$; positive on 15 of 18 untied tasks), including on 12 tasks the policy was never primed on, and similarity-selected pools do no better than random ($+0.1$ points). Within a task, a pool's visual similarity to the test scenes tracks its success at Spearman 0.10; across tasks, similarity separates tasks whose scenes differ and falls to chance when two tasks share a scene. The gain comes from on-task closed-loop evidence: best-of-ten direct search over pools recovers two-thirds of it with 100 rollouts. Against the shipped 20-demonstration pool, utility-curated pools of five lead by 7.0 points over 560 paired episodes from two evaluation runs ($p=0.006$), and utility-ordered retrieval inside the full pool leads by 4.5 points ($p=0.055$).

## 1 Introduction
In-context imitation policies 
===INTRO===

In-context imitation policies place demonstrations in the model’s context at inference time and adapt to a task without a gradient step [ 35 , 37 , 9 , 5 ] . In retrieval-augmented systems such as RICL, a retriever decides which demonstrations enter the context by ranking candidates on visual similarity to the current observation, the distance between frozen vision-encoder embeddings [ 29 ] . The prior is sensible: a demonstration that looks like the present scene is likely to be about the present task. Whether the retrieved demonstrations are the ones that make the policy succeed is a closed-loop question, and it is the question this paper answers. We take a published in-context vision-language-action (VLA) policy, a third-party LIBERO port of RICL on \pi_{0} -FAST [ 35 , 31 , 21 ] , and hold it frozen. The policy indexes every frame of every demonstration in its pool with DINOv2 features and, at each control step, retrieves the four nearest frames as context. We add one control message to the policy server that swaps the demonstration pool without reloading weights. With that, the expected success of the policy under any pool S , V(S) , can be estimated by rolling out. Sampling pools of fixed size and regressing the estimated success on membership gives each demonstration a relative utility score \hat{\varphi}_{i} . The question is then whether choosing the pool by \hat{\varphi} beats choosing it by similarity. illustrates how the two rankings diverge: on one task the demonstration the similarity rule ranks first is the second-worst of twenty by measured utility; on another, its top choice is in the bottom five while a demonstration it never selects is in the top five. The answer is that similarity identifies the task and not the useful demonstration within it. Across 19 tasks, pools chosen by measured utility beat pools chosen by similarity by about 11 points, and similarity-chosen pools do no better than random ones. Pools are fixed at five demonstrations, isolating which demonstrations enter the context, and every condition keeps the policy’s per-step similarity retrieval inside its pool; the shipped configuration searches all 20 demonstrations. Against that pool, the five highest-utility demonstrations lead by 7.0 points over 560 paired episodes ( p=0.006 ), and ordering retrieval inside the full pool by utility leads by 4.5 points ( p=0.055 ). Contributions. 1. An evaluation protocol for a frozen in-context VLA: a live pool swap that makes V(S) estimable, a fixed-cardinality coalition regression for per-demonstration utility, and a prespecified held-out design that separates the initial states used to fit utility from those used to test selection ( Sec. 3 ). 2. A selection effect that replicates on unseen tasks. At pool size five, utility-selected pools beat similarity-selected pools by 10.8\,\mathrm{pp} over 19 tasks and 760 paired episodes per cell, with no detectable heterogeneity across tasks; on 12 tasks the policy was never primed on the effect is +10.2\,\mathrm{pp} , against +11.8\,\mathrm{pp} on the original seven. Similarity-selected pools do no better than random ones ( Sec. 4.2 ). 3. Why similarity fails. Within a task, a pool’s similarity to the test scenes barely tracks its success (mean Spearman 0.10). Across tasks, similarity separates tasks that differ visually and falls to chance when two tasks share a scene ( Figs. 9 and 4.6 ). 4. What buys the gain. The gain comes from closed-loop evidence on the task: best-of-ten direct search reaches two-thirds of it at 100 rollouts, while removing the worst demonstrations alone did not detectably raise success over random. The effect holds at pool sizes three and five and vanishes at ten ( Secs. 4.3 , 4.4 and 4.7 ).

## 3 Setting and instrument
Policy. The checkpoint is step 10 000 of a third-party port of RICL to LIBERO published on Hugging Face ( Appendix S1 ), a 2.9B-parameter \pi_{0} -FAST model post-trained so that four retrieved demonstration frames, each with its own action, precede the query observation. The retriever loads every demonstration directory in a pool, builds one FAISS index over DINOv2 per-patch embeddings of every frame, and at each control step retrieves the four frames nearest to the current agent-view image. Priming used 12 LIBERO tasks with 20 demonstrations each; we evaluate on both primed and held-out tasks. Each task has a pool of 20 processed demonstrations. The instrument. We added four control messages to the policy server: a pool swap that rebuilds the FAISS index from a chosen set of demonstrations without reloading weights, an embedding endpoint exposing the server’s own DINOv2 features (so the similarity baseline uses the retriever’s metric), a retrieval-trace counter, and a per-episode RNG reset. Rollouts remain stochastic after the reset (non-deterministic GPU reductions perturb the sampled action tokens), so V(S) , the expected success under pool S , is estimated by \hat{V}(S) , the success rate over repeated rollouts. Per-demonstration utility. We score demonstrations by sampling 60 pools of fixed size |S|=5 from the 20 demonstrations, estimating each \hat{V}(S) on the same 10 initial states (0–9), and fitting a ridge regression of \hat{V} on centred membership indicators, \hat{\varphi}=(X_{c}^{\top}X_{c}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{c}^{\top}y_{c} with \lambda=1 . Fixing pool size separates demonstration content from cardinality, since the retriever takes the four nearest frames from a single pooled index and a larger pool mechanically supplies nearer neighbours. Because pool size is fixed and the indicators are centred, \hat{\varphi}_{i} is a relative score: adding one demonstration displaces another, and the coefficients sum to zero. Selection uses the two ends of the ranking. Pool-selection rules and test. Four pools of five are compared on every task: top (five highest \hat{\varphi} ), bottom (five lowest), dino (the five demonstrations with the smallest L_{2} distance, in the retriever’s own DINOv2 embedding, between any of their frames and the test scene’s initial observation), and random (seeded per task and scene). All four conditions keep the policy’s per-step frame retrieval inside the selected pool; they differ only in which five demonstrations the pool contains. The shipped configuration, the full pool of 20 ( full ), is evaluated as a separate baseline ( Sec. 4.4 ). Cells are evaluated on initial states 10–49, disjoint from the 0–9 used to fit \hat{\varphi} , with 40 paired episodes per cell per task; paired cells are compared by the exact McNemar test [ 25 ] and pooled rates carry Wilson intervals [ 41 ] . A task entered if its full-pool success lay in [0.25,0.85] on a screen of 10 (LIBERO-90) or 20 (standard suites) trials and passed a prespecified screen on the pool estimates ( Appendix S1 ), which excluded one task ( spatial:7 ). The 12 LIBERO-90 tasks receive their own utility fits, so they replicate the whole procedure on unprimed tasks.

## 4 Experiments
4.1 Context content is necessary, and the eligible band is narrow We first verify that the policy depends on the demonstrations in its pool. On 8 held-out tasks, swapping a task’s own 20 demonstrations for those of a different task (a fixed cyclic shift) moved success from 26.2% to 0.6% over 160 paired episodes (matched won 41 discordant pairs, mismatched none, p<10^{-5} ); on the 12 primed tasks, from 87.9% to 1.7% ( b=207 , c=0 ).
