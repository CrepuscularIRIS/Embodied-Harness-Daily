# VLA-Stem: A Lightweight Safety Mechanism for Vision-Language-Action Models via Reinforcement Learning

## Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models integrate high-level reasoning with low-level action execution for end-to-end robotic control. However, most VLA models target static manipulation in unconstrained environments, overlooking spatial constraints and dynamic obstacles. Fine-tuning VLAs on obstacle-avoidance data is a straightforward solution, but collecting such data is costly, especially for dynamic scenarios, and may degrade their original task capabilities. To address these limitations, we propose VLA-Stem, a lightweight, reinforcement-learning-based safety mechanism for VLA systems. Without fine-tuning the underlying VLA policy, VLA-Stem converts its action chunks into motion references and uses trajectory-level guidance to preserve their spatial intent while allowing flexible motion and timing adjustments. Conditioned on these references, robot proprioception, and local obstacle information, the Stem policy adaptively modifies execution at inference time, preserving the original task intent while enabling responsive adaptation to spatial constraints and dynamic obstacles. For robust and sample-efficient training, we combine teacher-student distillation with curriculum learning. We evaluate VLA-Stem on reference following, static spatial constraints, and dynamic obstacle avoidance. Across six constrained simulation settings, VLA-Stem achieves 52.8% average collision-free task success versus 23.7% for the strongest safety baseline, while preserving base-policy performance in obstacle-free settings; real-world experiments further show consistent collision reductions under static and dynamic obstacles. These results demonstrate that VLA-Stem couples high-level VLA task intent with rapid, reflex-like responses to immediate physical risks, providing a brainstem-inspired mechanism for robust robot execution.

## I Introduction
Embodied intelligence aims to integrate robotics with artificial intelligence for general real-world interaction, with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models emerging as a prominent direction. State-of-the-art VLA models [ 1 ] adopt end-to-end architectures that combine high-level capabilities with low-level action execution. This resembles human behavior, where deliberate reasoning guides bodily actions to accomplish diverse real-world tasks. However, we argue that this paradigm overlooks another fundamental aspect of biological intelligence: basic physiological and sensorimotor functions, such as postural regulation and rapid reflexive responses, which operate through lower-level neural circuits including the brainstem. In contrast, most existing VLA policies lack an explicit counterpart to such lower-level responsive regulation. Therefore, VLA policies should not only learn what to do and how to do it, but also remain responsive to the safety demands of physical interaction. Building on this perspective, we aim to endow VLA systems with the ability to react to immediate physical risks while remaining coordinated with their task-directed behavior (Fig. 1 ). Existing approaches mainly follow two directions. One is to learn collision-aware manipulation behaviors from large-scale avoidance data [ 2 ] ; however, collecting such data is labor-intensive and costly, especially in dynamic environments, and adapting a pretrained VLA requires additional training of the task policy itself. Another direction is to augment a frozen VLA with a conventional safety module. For example, VLSA [ 3 ] introduces control-theoretic constraints to modify unsafe VLA actions during execution. While this modular design provides an explicit mechanism for satisfying local geometric constraints, it primarily focuses on action-space safety and ignores the coordination between safety responses and the task-directed behavior of the VLA, leading to restricted corrective behaviors, reduced task success, and limited adaptability in complex dynamic environments. This motivates a mechanism that does not treat safety adaptation independently of VLA task intent, but instead tightly integrates safety responses with VLA task execution. To address these limitations, we propose VLA-Stem , a lightweight, brainstem-inspired RL-based safety mechanism that couples VLA task intent with rapid responses to physical risks. Specifically, VLA-Stem uses the short-horizon trajectory generated by the base VLA as a reference for its intended motion, and jointly conditions on this reference trajectory, robot proprioception, and local obstacle information to generate executable actions. To support such flexible execution, we introduce a trajectory-level guidance objective that preserves the spatial intent of the motion reference while relaxing strict temporal alignment. We further adopt teacher–student distillation with curriculum learning to facilitate structured exploration and improve the learned policy’s recovery capability under safety-induced deviations. As a result, VLA-Stem improves execution safety and robustness without sacrificing task performance or incurring additional training cost for the base policy. We evaluate VLA-Stem across multiple manipulation tasks and scenarios, including obstacle-free execution, static spatial constraints, and dynamic obstacle avoidance. Results show that VLA-Stem reduces safety violations while maintaining base-policy task performance, demonstrating its potential as an inference-time safety mechanism for VLA policies. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose VLA-Stem , a lightweight, reinforcement-learning-based safety mechanism for VLA models that directly integrates VLA task intent with runtime safety adaptation to physical constraints, without fine-tuning the underlying policy. • We design a trajectory-level guidance objective that uses the base VLA trajectory as a reference motion, allowing flexible local motion and timing adjustments while preserving task behavior. We further introduce a teacher–student distillation framework with curriculum learning to improve recovery from obstacle-avoidance-induced deviations. • We conduct extensive experiments across multiple manipulation scenarios in both simulation and the real world, demonstrating improved collision-free task success and direct sim-to-real transfer of the Stem policy without real-world training or fine-tuning.

## III Method
An overview of the proposed framework is illustrated in Fig. 2 . We first formulate VLA-conditioned execution, where the frozen VLA provides a task-directed motion reference that conditions the Stem policy (§ III-A ). We then introduce trajectory-level guidance to preserve the spatial intent of this reference while allowing flexible motion and timing adjustments (§ III-B ). Next, we present teacher–student distillation for learning recovery from execution deviations using privileged task information during training (§ III-C ). Finally, we describe the RL objective and curriculum that progressively train the Stem policy from trajectory following to static and dynamic obstacle interactions (§ III-D ). III-A VLA-Conditioned Execution We formulate VLA-Stem as a coupled execution architecture in which the frozen VLA provides a short-horizon task-directed motion reference, while the Stem policy conditions jointly on this reference and the current physical context to generate executable actions. Execution is organized into intervals of h control steps, each associated with one VLA action chunk. Let c denote such an execution interval and t_{c} its first control step. At t_{c} , the frozen VLA policy \pi_{\mathrm{VLA}} is queried with its observation \mathbf{o}_{t_{c}}^{\mathrm{VLA}} and produces an h -step action chunk \mathcal{A}_{c}^{\mathrm{VLA}}=\pi_{\mathrm{VLA}}\left(\mathbf{o}_{t_{c}}^{\mathrm{VLA}}\right). (1) Rather than being executed directly, this action chunk is converted into a motion reference that conditions Stem execution over the current interval. Let T_{t}^{w}\in SE(3) denote the end-effector pose in the world frame. We map the VLA action chunk to the corresponding future end-effector poses \{T_{t_{c}+1}^{w},\dots,T_{t_{c}+h}^{w}\} and express them relative to the end-effector pose T_{t_{c}}^{w} at the beginning of the interval: \Delta T_{c,k}=\left(T_{t_{c}}^{w}\right)^{-1}T_{t_{c}+k}^{w},\qquad\tau_{c}^{\mathrm{rel}}=\left\{\Delta T_{c,k}\right\}_{k=1}^{h}. (2) The resulting trajectory \tau_{c}^{\mathrm{rel}} serves as the task-directed motion reference until the next VLA query. At each control step t within the interval, the Stem policy also receives the current physical context, consisting of robot proprioception and local obstacle information.
