# Review the Episodes, Refine the Lessons: Simple Memory Evolution for Embodied Agents

## Abstract
Recent studies of embodied agents based on frontier multimodal large language models (MLLMs) develop harness engineering around the MLLM, such as harnesses with post-trained policies and recursive self-improvement loops. Yet whether these additions exceed the native capability of the MLLM remains unexplored. We introduce SimplEvo to explore how far an MLLM can improve under direct control when it retains its execution experience in self-written text memories. It requires no weight updates, post-trained policies, or privileged information. The MLLM writes a coordinate-free task memory once from a single demonstration. Under a strict train-test protocol, it then creates a bounded working memory on training episodes only and refines it after each episode with lessons on procedure, completion, and recovery. Both memories stay fixed at test time. On EBench, SimplEvo lifts the success rate (SR) of GPT-6-Astra under direct control from 46.7% to 81.7%, and tops the RoboDojo leaderboard with 69.63% SR and a score of 75.32. Compared with memories that also store past visual frames, SimplEvo reaches a similar SR with text memory alone while using fewer tokens. With its memories fixed, SimplEvo also generalizes to unseen layouts and randomized scene configurations. Moreover, the working memory transfers across models: written by GPT-6-Astra, it can be reused as is by weaker MLLMs and improve their performance without any adaptation of their own.

## 1 Introduction
Generalist robotic manipulation is a central problem of embodied intelligence. Vision-language-action models (VLAs) [ Kim et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2025b ; Black et al., 2025a ] and world action models (WAMs) [ Ye et al., 2026 ; Yuan et al., 2026b ; Wang et al., 2026e ] align their manipulation skills through post-training on benchmark-specific trajectories [ Liu et al., 2023 ; Mu et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025 ] . In contrast, recent studies show that frontier multimodal large language models (MLLMs) directly operate robots to complete tasks without parameter updates. They command the end effector from camera views and language instructions [ OpenAI, 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026b ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Jia et al., 2026 ; Lou et al., 2026 ; Hu et al., 2026a ] , explore and adapt their behaviors to the observed results [ Guo et al., 2026a ; Cheng et al., 2026b ] , and recover from errors within an episode [ Fu et al., 2026 ; Yao et al., 2026 ] . These findings have motivated a growing range of embodied agents built on frontier MLLMs. These agents fall into two lines of work. The first is code-as-policies , which began with language models writing robot code [ Liang et al., 2023 ] and composes perception modules and motion primitives into executable programs. In systems such as Code as Policies [ Liang et al., 2023 ] , COAP [ Hu et al., 2026b ] , and RAX on RoboDojo [ Huang et al., 2026 ] , the synthesized programs run at test time without the MLLM in the loop, so they cover only anticipated scene variations and failure modes. Recent works such as CaP-X [ Fu et al., 2026 ] and PyRUA-Lean [ Si et al., 2026 ] therefore execute code online with the MLLM in the loop. Even so, code-as-policies systems typically rely on privileged information beyond RGB views and proprioception, such as metric depth, camera parameters, and object sizes, or on estimates from calibrated depth sensors and vision foundation models [ Kirillov et al., 2023 ; Ravi et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2024a ; Yang et al., 2024b ] , to which the resulting programs are sensitive. Frontier MLLMs, by contrast, already perceive and reason about space from RGB views and proprioception, which raises the question of whether routing perception through intermediate models is necessary at all. The second is harness engineering , which extends the harness around the MLLM with post-trained policies or external improvement loops. Some systems build harnesses with policy that expose post-trained policies as callable tools [ Zhang et al., 2026c ; Wang et al., 2026c ] , so the MLLM plans while the policy executes low-level actions. Each delegated call returns only its final observation and status, so the actions of the policy never enter the context of the MLLM, and the harness can constrain the capability of the underlying MLLM. Other systems adapt self-evolving language agents into embodied recursive self-improvement (RSI) frameworks that revise context, tools, or control logic through revision loops around the agent [ Tian et al., 2026 ; MMLab & PhysicalRSI contributors, 2026 ; Lu et al., 2026 ; Cheng et al., 2026a ] . These frameworks iterate on external harnesses and rarely measure the base model under direct control first. Some self-evolving designs adapt on the test scenes themselves [ Wang et al., 2026d ] . Because the evaluator then returns feedback on the scenes used for evaluation, test-scene information can leak into the evolved system, and its gains no longer show whether the acquired experience generalizes to scenes the agent has never attempted. It thus remains unclear how far a frontier MLLM can go under direct control on its own, and hence how much of this added machinery is necessary. Both lines of work focus on either exploiting the coding ability of the MLLM or wrapping it with post-trained policies and improvement loops, thereby overlooking the rising capability of the MLLM itself to act directly as a general-purpose robot controller. We refer to this setting as direct control , where the MLLM commands the end effector directly using RGB views and proprioception, without external perception modules or a post-trained policy. Direct control returns the result of every executed command to the MLLM, so the closed-loop feedback and within-episode recovery are explicit. After a missed grasp or a failed insertion, the MLLM compares the commanded end-effector coordinates with the resulting camera views, diagnoses the error, and adjusts its next command [ Yao et al., 2026 ] . These adjustments and the experience of each trial, however, vanish when the episode ends, so each episode starts anew. Can a frontier MLLM unlock strong manipulation under direct control simply by inheriting and evolving the in-episode adjustments and trial experience across episodes, relying only on its native multimodal capabilities? ## 3 Method
As shown in Figure 2 , SimplEvo equips direct control with two memories written by the MLLM, each overcoming a limitation of isolated episodes. The task memory supplies the order of stages and the final state of a task before the first attempt, and the working memory preserves the corrections that would otherwise vanish when an episode ends. 3.1 Direct Control under a Strict Information Protocol Agent under direct control. The agent pairs a fixed-weight MLLM core F with an agent harness that specifies its observation and action space. Under direct control , the MLLM issues every robot command itself instead of calling a post-trained policy. At decision step t of the k -th episode on task \tau , the MLLM receives the observation o_{t}=(I_{t},q_{t}) , which consists of RGB images I_{t} from an overview camera and two wrist cameras, or from any subset of them that the MLLM requests, and the proprioceptive state q_{t} of the robot. Given the observations o_{\leq t} and the earlier actions a_{<t} of the episode, the MLLM issues a_{t}=F\big(\ell_{\tau},\,M^{\mathrm{task}},\,M^{\mathrm{work}}_{k-1},\,o_{\leq t},\,a_{<t}\big), (1) where \ell_{\tau} is the task instruction with basic specifications of the robot embodiment, and M^{\mathrm{task}} and M^{\mathrm{work}}_{k-1} are the task and working memories of Sections 3.2 and 3.3 . Each action a_{t} is a chunk of 1 to 6 absolute waypoints, each setting an end-effector pose and a gripper target for each arm and, on the mobile robot of EBench, a base pose. Inverse kinematics converts the end-effector poses into joint commands. An episode ends when the evaluator registers success, when the step limit of the task is reached, or earlier when the MLLM ends its turn. The observations and actions of episode k form its trajectory \xi_{k}=(o_{1},a_{1},o_{2},a_{2},\dots) . By comparison, a harness with policy hands control to a post-trained policy for a bounded number of physics steps and receives only the end state, so \xi_{k} omits the actions that the policy executes. Information protocol. On both benchmarks, we restrict the agent to the information that a physical deployment provides. During execution, the agent observes the scene solely through RGB images and robot proprioception o_{t}=(I_{t},q_{t}) , matching the inputs of a post-trained VLA or WAM.
