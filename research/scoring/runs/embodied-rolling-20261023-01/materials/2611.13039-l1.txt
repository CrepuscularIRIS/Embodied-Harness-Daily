# Robo-MLT: Dual-System Robotic Manipulation for Long-Horizon Medical Laboratory Tasks

## Abstract
Reliable medical laboratory automation requires robots to complete entire workflows in protocol order while handling changes during execution. A robot may execute each motion correctly yet fail the workflow by confusing visually similar stages or stalling after human intervention. We present Robo-MLT, a dual-system framework that couples explicit workflow monitoring with learned action generation. A vision--language planner composes an ordered atomic-skill sequence from the protocol and the initial workspace observation. A multiframe execution monitor detects when the active skill has completed or become infeasible, then advances the workflow or triggers replanning. A skill-conditioned vision--language--action policy generates the manipulation trajectories, and an asynchronous runtime coordinates it with the monitor. We evaluate Robo-MLT against four policy baselines and a VLM-based monitor baseline on blood gas analysis and blood sample tube sorting, with 50 nominal configurations per method and 20 sample-removal configurations. Robo-MLT achieves task success rates of 82\% and 70\%, exceeding $π_{0.5}$ by 30 and 22 percentage points, respectively. After an operator removes the sample being handled, the system revises the remaining plan and continues processing the other samples. These results indicate that, under the evaluated conditions, explicit workflow monitoring makes long-horizon laboratory manipulation more reliable and recoverable.

## I Introduction
Medical laboratory automation aims to reduce repetitive manual work, increase testing throughput, and limit personnel exposure to clinical samples. These benefits require robots to complete entire workflows reliably, not merely to demonstrate isolated manipulation skills. In blood gas analysis, for example, a robot must handle a sample, prepare it for testing, interact precisely with the analyzer, and then withdraw and discard it in the prescribed order. These operations depend on one another: a successful grasp does not ensure a successful test, and a physically feasible action may still be wrong for the current workflow stage. Reliable automation therefore requires both accurate manipulation and consistent execution of the operating protocol [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . Meeting these requirements is difficult in a laboratory workspace shared with people. Sample positions vary, instrument interfaces demand precise alignment, and operators may intervene while a task is running. Different workflow stages can also produce nearly identical observations: a sample at an analyzer inlet must be inserted before testing but withdrawn afterward, yet the local images look alike (Fig. 1 ). Choosing the correct action therefore requires knowing what has already been completed. Disturbances add a further difficulty. A displaced target may only require the ongoing motion to be adjusted, whereas removing the sample from the gripper makes the current step impossible to complete and forces the remaining workflow to be revised. Existing approaches relevant to these requirements fall into three categories. The first is fixed-program and task-specific laboratory automation, which implements procedures through predefined control logic, calibrated motions, dedicated fixtures, and purpose-built mechanisms [ 4 , 5 ] . In clinical laboratories, total laboratory automation lines handle tube transport and sample preparation while human operators oversee the workflow and make non-routine decisions [ 6 ] . The same principle underlies task-specific devices such as microtube cappers [ 7 ] , the KANKA tube sorter [ 8 ] , and swab-sampling robots [ 9 ] , while research architectures encode the workflow as state machines [ 10 ] , behavior trees [ 11 ] , or modular integration frameworks [ 12 ] . Within their intended operating conditions, these designs provide procedural consistency and predictable cycle times by construction; because every transition is enumerated in advance, however, a new workspace configuration or an unanticipated contingency requires the sensing, control logic, and recovery branches to be authored by hand for each task. Demonstration-trained manipulation skills offer a complementary route: they generate actions from visual observations and can be reused across supported variations in object pose without reprogramming individual trajectories [ 13 ] . The second category comprises end-to-end imitation-learning and vision–language–action (VLA) policies. Methods such as ACT [ 14 ] , Diffusion Policy [ 15 ] , \pi_{0} [ 16 ] , and SmolVLA [ 17 ] learn actions from demonstrations, and VLA models add language conditioning and pretrained visual–semantic representations. Because they act on the current observation, they suit the manipulation studied here: tube poses differ between runs, and seating a tube in an analyzer inlet with roughly 5 mm of clearance requires alignment from the live view rather than a calibrated waypoint. Their language conditioning also offers a natural interface to a higher-level component. A policy that does not explicitly maintain workflow progress, however, must infer the current stage from its observations and conditioning inputs. When stages look alike, insufficient temporal context can lead to repeated or stage-inconsistent actions. Moreover, adapting a trajectory to a displaced target does not determine how the workflow should proceed once the current operation is no longer feasible. The third category separates high-level planning or semantic reasoning from low-level execution. In laboratory robotics, ORGANA [ 18 ] combines language-guided planning with visual feedback, and RoboChemist [ 1 ] couples vision–language model (VLM) planning and monitoring with VLA control, advancing once a subtask is verified and repeating it otherwise. General hierarchical systems such as RoboDual [ 19 ] , Fast-in-Slow [ 20 ] , and \pi_{0.5} [ 21 ] connect semantic decisions to continuous manipulation. Recent VLA frameworks also bring progress and recovery inside the policy: PALM [ 22 ] predicts within-subtask progress jointly with actions, and TCoT [ 23 ] couples success assessment with trajectory-level recovery. Progress representations and recovery mechanisms therefore already exist; systems differ in the level at which they act. ## III Methodology
Determining whether to advance or revise the current protocol step requires discrete decisions based on temporal observation history, whereas executing the active step requires a continuous stream of actions at the robot control rate. Inspired by the fast–slow separation of dual-process theory, Robo-MLT assigns these responsibilities to two complementary systems. As shown in Fig. 2 , System 2 is a low-frequency execution monitor that tracks the plan and maintains the active atomic skill, and System 1 is a high-frequency generative executor that maps the active skill to continuous actions. A top-level planner initializes the ordered skill sequence, while an asynchronous runtime coordinates the two systems. III-A Top-Level Task Planning over an Atomic-Skill Library Rather than issuing unrestricted free-form commands during execution, Robo-MLT grounds high-level planning in a declarative atomic-skill library: \mathcal{S}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{|\mathcal{S}|}\} (1) where |\mathcal{S}| is the number of atomic skills in the library and each skill s_{j} is specified by a canonical natural-language instruction l_{j} ; here |\mathcal{S}|=7 for blood gas analysis and |\mathcal{S}|=5 for tube sorting. Given a task instruction, the corresponding instrument protocol, and an initial observation I_{0} of the workspace, a frozen vision–language planner \Phi_{\mathrm{plan}} composes an ordered skill sequence over the library: \mathcal{P}=\Phi_{\mathrm{plan}}(\text{task},\,\text{protocol},\,\mathcal{S},\,I_{0})=\big(s_{\kappa(1)},\,\ldots,\,s_{\kappa(|\mathcal{P}|)}\big) (2) where |\mathcal{P}| is the plan length and \kappa(i) maps the i -th step to a skill in \mathcal{S} ; \kappa need not be injective, since a skill may recur at several steps of the same plan. We instantiate \Phi_{\mathrm{plan}} with the Qwen3.7-Max API [ 33 ] , queried zero-shot and never fine-tuned; the same model serves as the replanner \Phi_{\mathrm{replan}} below. Conditioning on I_{0} lets the planner instantiate the plan from the observed scene rather than a fixed layout: it reads the number, positions, and categories of the tubes on the rack and expands the sequence accordingly—repeating the assay for each detected tube in blood gas analysis, and committing to a feasible pick-and-place order in tube sorting.
