# Objectives as Policies: Vision-Language Model Cost Programs for Contact-Rich Manipulation

## Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) have shown strong semantic and spatial reasoning capabilities in 3D environments. Recently, connecting VLMs with model-predictive controllers (MPC) has emerged as a paradigm for combining strong semantic reasoning with physics-based planning. However, connecting these two for contact-rich robotic manipulation requires a task representation that bridges the two and captures how physical interactions can be reasoned by VLMs. In this work, we introduce Objectives as Policies (OaP), a framework that connects a pretrained VLM to a shared sampling-based MPC through an editable objective program. Each program specifies a sequence of stages for completing a task, combining running and terminal costs from a cost library with stage completion conditions. Given a natural-language task instruction and scene observation, we build a simulation-based digital twin through real-to-sim, and the VLM generates an objective program that is executed by the MPC in the simulation. The program is iteratively updated by the VLM based on MPC rollouts, before being deployed in the real world. We evaluate our framework in both simulation and the real world on four tabletop manipulation tasks, and show that it outperforms strong baselines. Additional videos and examples are available on the project website: https://zihaolu001.github.io/oap-project/

## I INTRODUCTION
Recently, contact-implicit model-predictive control (MPC) has enabled robots to achieve real-time dexterous manipulations on various tasks [ 1 , 2 ] . Compared to end-to-end learned policies, these physics-based methods naturally generalize to new objects and scenes through online re-planning and ensure safety by directly embedding safety constraints during planning. However, they require careful engineering of the rewards and constraints with the tasks and the scenes known a priori, making deployment into the open world challenging. Meanwhile, end-to-end learned policies, such as vision-language-action models, leverage the semantic understanding of vision-language models (VLMs) trained on internet-scale vision-language data and finetune with robot data to acquire robotic manipulation skills. For example, OpenVLA directly predicts actions from visual observations and language instructions, without an explicit physics model for online planning [ 3 ] . We observe that manipulation skills typically can be decomposed into two key components: what the robot should be doing, and how the robot can execute it , where the former requires semantic understanding of the environment and task, and the latter necessitates understanding of the physics and contact dynamics. Hence, in this paper, we ask the following research question: How can we integrate the semantic understanding of VLMs with physics-based MPC to achieve open-world manipulation skills, without any training data? Previous methods have investigated the use of VLMs for robot manipulation in different ways. VoxPoser and ReKep use 3D value maps and relational keypoint constraints, respectively, for closed-loop planning [ 4 , 5 ] . ReKep’s local forward model assumes grasped keypoints move rigidly with the end-effector [ 5 ] , whereas our MPC explicitly simulates robot-object contact dynamics. Another method uses VLMs only for estimating the physical parameters of a world model and employs large-language models (LLMs) that script the MPC and go through iterative real-world refinement [ 6 ] . Beyond using these foundation models to generate objectives and constraints for MPCs, some works use VLMs to directly propose robot end-effector paths [ 7 , 8 ] . Another class of methods trains VLMs to predict execution progress [ 9 ] , which can be used as a noisy reward function during offline reinforcement learning training. Different from these methods, we propose a framework where we enhance a VLM with a digital twin obtained through real-to-sim and a library of objective terms, and allow the VLM to iteratively refine a composite objective function from simulated MPC rollouts, before real-world execution. We introduce Objectives as Policies (OaP), a framework that expresses multi-stage tasks through an editable objective program for a shared sampling-based MPC controller. Each program combines running and terminal objective terms from a fixed library into a sequence of stages, along with completion conditions for every stage. A pretrained VLM generates the program and iteratively refines the program through rollouts in a digital twin obtained from real-to-sim, before final closed-loop execution in the real world. We leverage two refinement strategies: the first uses the VLM to self-review against the task language instruction and the scene before any rollouts, and the second updates the objective program based on the statistics of a failed rollout, which can be done iteratively over many rounds. In simulation experiments, our method achieves 90% cumulative success across four tasks with at most two feedback rounds and 97.5% with up to five rounds. Real-world experiments on the same four tasks achieve an overall success rate of 80% with deliberate scene perturbations.

## III Method
Summarized by Fig. 2 , our method first constructs a digital twin of the observed scene. The VLM uses the task instruction, scene observation, digital-twin information, and cost library to generate and self-review a multi-stage objective program. Each stage specifies running costs, terminal costs, and completion thresholds. MPC executes the task in simulation under these objectives, and failed executions provide feedback for program refinement. The resulting program then guides real-world execution with the synchronized digital twin. MPC replans controls from updated states, while the stage verifier advances the program when all completion conditions hold. In this section, we will first introduce the objective program in Sec. III-A , and discuss real-to-sim and MPC in Sec. III-B and Sec. III-C . We will then detail the objective functions and the cost library in Sec. III-D , and finish by describing the overall VLM workflow in Sec. III-E . III-A Objective Program An objective program for a multi-stage task specifies both the costs used for planning and the conditions used to advance stages. A stage S=(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{T},w,\tau) is defined by the running cost terms \mathcal{R} , the terminal cost terms \mathcal{T} , the cost weights w , and the stage completion thresholds \tau . To reduce the large search space of possible cost terms, we pre-define a cost library L of commonly used parametrized cost terms, and define the running and terminal cost terms \mathcal{R},\mathcal{T} as a combination of terms from the library with task-specific parameters, whose weighted sum according to weights w becomes the objective function of the MPC controller. The conditions for completing stage m are defined by the terminal terms \mathcal{T}_{m} and the corresponding completion thresholds \tau . Then an objective program \mathcal{P}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{M}) is defined by the costs and stage completion conditions of the M stages. III-B Real-to-Sim We build a digital twin of the scene to serve as the computation model of the MPC, both in simulation and real-world experiments, and as the rollout simulators in the objective program finetuning by VLM. For reconstructed objects, we obtain meshes from RGB observations and masks using SAM 3D [ 17 ] , then refine depth-based metric scale and object pose with Any6D [ 18 ] . A VLM then estimates physical parameters of the objects, including density and friction coefficients, from object appearance [ 19 ] . On hardware, FoundationPose updates object poses from camera observations [ 20 ] . MuJoCo-warp is used as the computation model during MPC rollouts [ 21 , 22 ] , and the original CPU-based MuJoCo is used as the rollout simulator since their contact dynamics are slightly more accurate than the MuJoCo-warp. While this simple pipeline works well for the tasks in our setup, in the future, we aim to perform explicit system identification of all shape, texture, and geometry parameters using differentiable simulation and rendering [ 23 ] to minimize the sim-to-real gap.
