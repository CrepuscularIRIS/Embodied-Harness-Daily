# Teaching a Tree to Recover: Online Distillation of an LLM Supervisor with Counterfactual Replay

## Abstract
Autonomous mobile robots commonly pair a global planner with a local model predictive controller (MPC), but nonconvex obstacle avoidance and a limited horizon can make the robot freeze in complex layouts. Deciding whether to replan, wait, or call for help is a supervisory decision. Recent work queries a large language model (LLM) supervisor on every anomaly, which is slow and costly. We instead distill the LLM online into a decision tree. After each training episode, counterfactual replay labels recoveries as needed, unnecessary, or harmful. These labels retire unhelpful rules. In simulation, on 20 unseen layouts where the MPC alone freezes, the tree handles at least 18 correctly across all seeds, either by reaching the goal or by calling for help when no feasible path exists. The tree matches both an always-query LLM supervisor and expert-designed rules, while issuing no LLM queries at test time. Handwritten rules cannot anticipate the long tail of situations a physical robot will encounter. Online distillation is a promising remedy: the tree handles familiar cases, and the LLM is consulted only for new ones, whose validated recoveries become new rules. Language models can thus also serve as designers of supervisors rather than only as runtime supervisors.

## 1 Introduction
Autonomous Mobile Robot (AMR) navigation is commonly split into two layers. A global planner computes a path over a map, typically with Dijkstra’s algorithm or A*, treating the robot as a point and ignoring its kinematic and dynamic constraints. A local controller, often a model predictive controller (MPC), tracks this path while respecting those constraints, and may deviate from it temporarily to avoid static and dynamic obstacles. Such deviations must stay small, because obstacle avoidance makes the optimization problem nonconvex: with a finite horizon, the solver is prone to local optima, slow or failed convergence, and exceeding its computation budget. When the path is blocked by a large or non-convex obstacle that is missing from the map, such as a blocking object across an aisle or a U-shaped arrangement of pallets, a way around it lies beyond what the controller can find, and the robot comes to a standstill. Whether it should then replan, wait, or call for help depends on the cause. This decision belongs to a supervisory layer above the controller. Navigation stacks typically implement this layer as hand-written recovery behaviors triggered when the planner or controller fails [ 9 ] . In this work, we investigate how to learn this recovery behavior. Recent work places a large language model (LLM) in the loop as a supervisor [ 6 , 14 , 7 ] and queries it on every anomaly, at a latency of seconds. This paper studies a similar approach where an LLM is used to find effective failure recoveries for an MPC-controlled mobile robot based on our previous work [ 19 , 3 ] . Unlike other approaches, we study where a decision should reside once it has been made a few times by abstracting and refining recurring events into a decision tree. Further, in subsequent loops, the decision tree is first queried, and only when no fitting decision is present the LLM is queried again for additional labels. Therefore, the cost of decisions drastically decreases over time. To refine and verify decisions in the tree, we use counterfactual replay of each action after an episode finishes, which enables in-context learning for the LLM supervisor. Through this, the LLM can continuously improve its decisions and update the decision tree. In various simulated scenarios, we find that our method complement and assist the MPC controller, leading to a near-perfect success rate in our tests. Importantly, it performs on par with hand-designed rules specifically created for the tested scenarios, showing that our framework can adapt to and learn from observed scenarios without human supervision. In 20 held-out test layouts where the MPC alone fails, we show that our method instead succeeds in reaching the goal position in more than 90% of the cases. Furthermore, when comparing our method with a baseline of scripted rules specifically designed for those scenarios, we find that our method automatically finds equal or better decisions while reaching the goal in 10% fewer steps, thus verifying its effectiveness at finding reasonable interventions. Our contributions are: • Online distillation of an LLM for AMR failure recovery into a decision tree. At a regular interval, a language model reads the current status report and decides whether an intervention is needed and picks a predefined recovery action, which acts only through the reference of an MPC tracker, the robot never waits for the LLM. To reduce the cost of repetitive interventions, we distill the LLM supervisor’s actions online into a decision tree when they occur more than once. • Counterfactual replay for in-context learning and rule-retirement. We show that when having access to a simulator then re-simulating each situation without the supervisor’s intervention gives labels if the action was needed, unnecessary, or harmful. Those are then used to retire existing rules and train the LLM in context to improve future decisions. The code, data and videos are available at: https://github.com/kristianceder/Counterfactual-Tree-Teaching .

## 3 Method
The problem we address is a mobile robot that has to reach a goal in a warehouse. We consider approaches where a planner such as A* is first used to produces a route over the free space it knows about, and a controller then tracks a dense reference along it. For instances, when using an MPC, as in our case, it solves a constrained optimization every iteration with obstacles as hard constraints to guarantee safety. Specifically, we address scenarios where the controller cannot independently find satisfactory solutions, such as the robot getting stuck due to obstructions unknown to the planner or the solver not finding a valid solution in time. We base our tracker on the MPC part of our earlier hybrid controller [ 19 , 3 ] . The only part our method changes are two inputs that a supervisor needs: a hold that pins the reference to the current pose and a heading target tracked during one. We propose using a supervision loop on top of the existing control framwork as presented in Fig. 2 . This loop regularly receives a report that summarizes the current status of the controller and robot, including various failure modes . This report is then passed asynchronously to a decision tree that tries to classify which of the recoveries to trigger or if none is needed. If the decision tree does not return a result for the current situation, the report is instead passed to an LLM teacher . Decisions taken by the LLM teacher are saved and distilled into the decision tree if they happen more than once to continuously decrease the cost of querying recoveries. Further, we perform counterfactual replay of any taken decision to find out what would have happened if no action was taken instead. This is used to post-hoc verify the effect of a recovery , which is used for in-context learning of the LLM teacher . Report. At each tick, the report builder records recent ego motion, the nearest static and dynamic obstacles and whether they block the route, controller deadline misses, the watchdog’s allowance, the step budget, and what has been tried in the episode. These fields provide evidence, such as time stopped or distance to an obstacle, rather than verdicts such as stuck . Each decider then reaches its own verdict independently. Route progress is deliberately omitted in the report so that deciders cannot score themselves. Further, to identify a blocked route from the first tick, the static obstacle check uses the true map along the entire planned route. Failure modes.
