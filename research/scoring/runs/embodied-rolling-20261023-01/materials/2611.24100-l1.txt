===ABSTRACT===
Embodied robots increasingly rely on large language models (LLMs) to interpret user intent and coordinate with robot-specific action models for physical interaction. This multi-model pipeline creates a critical semantic boundary across mind, model collaboration, and motion: semantic consistency may break between what the large model understands, how the small action model translates that understanding into executable commands, and what physical actions the robot ultimately performs. As a security consequence, commercial robots may execute unintended physical actions even when user inputs contain no explicit action commands. To measure this problem, we develop TARS, a black-box framework that explores the command-reasoning-action pipeline and automatically discovers and validates unintended physical behaviors. We evaluate TARS on four commercial robots across 44 experimental settings, conducting 133,400 executions. TARS uncovers widespread cross-model action misalignment and achieves up to 94% verified action coverage.

===INTRO===
Embodied intelligence is rapidly emerging as one of the most important frontiers of artificial intelligence. Unlike traditional robots that primarily execute pre-programmed control logic, modern embodied robots integrate perception, reasoning, planning, and physical execution within a unified system, enabling natural interactions with users and dynamic adaptation to complex environments. Driven by recent advances in large language models (LLMs), major companies have accelerated the deployment of embodied intelligence platforms, including Unitree’s G1 ( Unitree, 2024 ) and H1 ( Unitree, 2023b ) humanoid robots, AgiBot’s A2 ( Agibot, 2025a ) and X2 ( Agibot, 2025b ) series, Tesla Optimus ( Biba, 2026 ) , and Figure 02 ( Figure, 2025 ) . A defining characteristic of these systems is the collaboration between large reasoning models and small execution models. Rather than directly controlling motors and actuators, the LLM serves as a high-level reasoning engine responsible for semantic understanding, contextual reasoning, and task planning, while lightweight execution modules translate these high-level decisions into executable skills and low-level control commands. This large–small model collaboration combines the reasoning capability of foundation models with the efficiency and real-time responsiveness of traditional robotic controllers, and has become the dominant architecture adopted by contemporary embodied AI systems. Meanwhile, robots are increasingly deployed as interactive companions that directly engage with humans through conversations, gestures, navigation assistance, entertainment, and physical interactions such as handshakes or high-fives. As a result, they naturally become attractive targets for attackers. When discussing robot security, many people immediately think of Asimov’s Three Laws of Robotics ( Asimov, 1965 ) . Modern embodied robots are likewise equipped with numerous safety mechanisms designed to reject harmful instructions and prevent dangerous behaviors. Intuitively, if an attacker wants a robot to perform an unsafe action, the attacker must first issue an unsafe command. But what if this assumption is wrong? Imagine a robot that perfectly follows its safety policies. An attacker walks up to it and recites a poem, speaks a sentence with no obvious meaning, or utters what sounds like complete nonsense. The robot detects no malicious intent, raises no safety warning, and observes no explicit action command. Yet the robot suddenly begins executing physical actions that were never requested by the user. In the worst case, if such unintended actions occur in safety-critical environments or near vulnerable individuals, they could cause physical disruption, property damage, or even harm to nearby humans. This may sound like a science-fiction scenario, but our experiments show that it is already possible on commercial embodied robots. We found that the physical behavior ultimately executed by the robot can deviate from what the user actually intends, even when no explicit harmful command is issued. Our analysis reveals two fundamental sources of this problem. The first is mind misalignment , where the large model itself misinterprets or hallucinates an unstated action goal from benign, ambiguous, or even action-unrelated inputs. The second is model-collaboration misalignment , where the large model correctly understands the user, but the downstream action decoder assigns a different operational meaning to its output. These failures can lead to concrete physical consequences: Unitree Go2 can execute a dangerous front_jump without the required confirmation, Booster K1 can be induced to perform attacker-controlled multi-action sequences, and Unitree G1 can turn a benign code-generation request containing straight_punch into a real punching motion. These observations motivate a fundamental question: how can we systematically uncover inconsistencies between a user’s intended actions and a robot’s actual physical behavior? Our key insight is that this problem can be approached from a fuzzing perspective. Just as traditional fuzzing systematically explores unexpected program behaviors through input mutations, we can systematically explore a robot’s language-to-action boundary by generating and mutating natural-language inputs to expose unintended physical actions. However, unlike conventional fuzzing, where failures can often be identified through crashes or abnormal program states, embodied robots require reasoning about the semantic relationship between user intent and executed actions. This introduces several unique challenges. First , natural-language inputs span an open-ended semantic space, making it difficult to construct effective test cases and systematically explore unexpected behaviors.
