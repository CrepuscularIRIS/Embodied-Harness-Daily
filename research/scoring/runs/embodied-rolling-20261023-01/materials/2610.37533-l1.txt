# Which Hand Was Late? Diagnosis for Robot Failure Recovery in Bimanual Tool Use

## Abstract
Failure diagnosers for robot manipulation are increasingly vision--language models (VLMs), trained and evaluated on single-arm failures produced by perturbing a successful demonstration. Bimanual tool use adds failures of other kinds: a tool that slips out of its socket while still held, two correct gestures made at different times, and attempts that fail for two reasons at once. On a simulated bimanual tool-use task whose failure causes are read from the recorded trace, we measure what each sensing channel lets a diagnoser tell. The tool's slip is read on the tool hand; which hand was late is read on both arms, while a sensor on one hand reads its own hand's delay and at most part of the other's; and whether a late attempt was also pushed too weakly is not identifiable from passive sensing when the tool hand is late. Within two attempts, a repair policy that escalates when the cause is not identifiable repairs as many failures as one told the true cause. A VLM's internal state carries the late hand almost perfectly, along a direction no closer to the one its answer reads than a random one; whether the answer reports it depends on the wording of the question.

## 1 Introduction
Imitation learning and recent Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have enabled impressive advances in robotic manipulation [ 5 , 2 ] and with them the question of what a robot should do when an attempt fails. Deployed in an open world, a robot will encounter mechanisms it has never interacted with before [ 12 ] , and it will inevitably fail on some of them. Some of what a mechanism requires can only be found out by trying: a robot that has never opened a stiff lock cannot know in advance how much force it requires. What matters is whether it can learn from the attempt. Failures can also occur on familiar tasks when the robot misestimates quantities that are not directly observable, such as the force required to push a locked door open or the timing of the other hand’s action. In either case, a failure is useful only if its cause can be identified: to recover from a failure and learn from it, a robot must detect it, attribute it to its cause, and act on that cause. A growing body of work addresses this problem through visual failure reasoning by asking a vision–language model (VLM). AHA [ 3 ] takes an image sequence and returns a natural-language explanation of the failure; RoboFAC [ 8 ] evaluates detection, identification and localisation from video; ViFailback [ 14 ] turns the diagnosis into visual corrections for a policy; and ARMOR [ 9 ] jointly refines detection and open-ended reasoning. These systems address detection , did the attempt fail? , and diagnosis , why did it fail? Both are learned from failures of a particular kind: each involves one arm acting on one object, is produced by perturbing a successful demonstration, and has a cause that is visible in the scene. Bimanual tool use breaks each of these assumptions. Consider unlocking a utility cabinet with a square key: one hand seats the key in the lock and turns it, while the other pushes the door. The attempt can fail because a gesture was wrong (the key was never seated), because the tool slipped out of its socket under torque while still firmly held, because the lock needed more force than was applied, or because both hands did the right thing at different times . The last case is not a mistake of either arm but a relation between them. For such a failure, “why did it fail?” becomes “ which hand was late? ”, a question no single-arm taxonomy can ask (Figure 1 ). In this paper, we ask: What must a failure diagnoser observe to tell which hand caused a bimanual tool-use failure, and do VLM diagnosers report it when they observe it? To answer this, we need a task in which the cause of each failure is known without relying on an annotator’s reading of the video. We build a bimanual, tool-mediated lock in simulation, inject one fault per attempt with a scripted controller, and read the class of every failure from the recorded trace of the mechanism. We then apply the same probe to one sensing channel at a time, and to the internal representation and the language output of the same VLM. We do not propose a new diagnoser, we instead measure what a diagnoser needs. Our contributions are the following: 1. A tool-use task with known failure causes (Sec. 3 ), where the cause of each failure can be recovered from the trace, including failures with multiple simultaneous causes. 2. What can be diagnosed, and from where (Sec. 4 ): the tool’s slip is read on the tool hand and which hand was late on both arms, while whether a late push was also too weak is on no passive sensor when the tool hand is late. Sensor placement determines which of these signals are available. 3. Diagnosis determines repair (Sec. 5 ): failures with two causes are repaired only when both causes are corrected. A policy that asks only whether the hands overlapped, applies the matching correction and, if it fails, both ( escalation ) repairs as many failures within two attempts as one told the true cause. 4. The model represents the late hand (Sec. 6 ): the VLM’s state encodes which hand was late, even though this information is not read out by its answer.

## 3 A tool-use task with known failure causes
We need failures whose cause is known without an annotator (contribution 1). We first describe the scene and its lock (Section 3.1 ), then how the cause of each failure is read from the recorded trace, including failures with two causes (Section 3.2 ), and finally the probe we apply to every sensing channel and to the VLM (Section 3.3 ). 3.1 Scene and mechanism Two Franka Panda arms face a door fitted with a lock (Figure 2 ). One arm fetches a square key-tool from a stand, seats it in the lock’s socket and turns it; the other pushes the door. The tool transmits torque through its shape, so the hand never touches the mechanism, and a tool that is not seated deep enough slips out of the socket while still held: a cam-out , which emerges from the contact geometry. The key springs back when released, so both hands must act at once: the door opens only if the key is held turned while the push exceeds a hidden threshold \tau , drawn for every attempt and absent from every observation. Details of empirical experiments are in Appendix A . 3.2 Causes read from the trace A scripted controller plays a fixed plan for each arm and injects one fault per attempt: the tool inserted short, a hand aimed off axis, the key under-turned, the push too shallow, or one arm’s schedule delayed. We label each failure according to the recorded trace (Figure 3 ): wrong indicates that a gesture was not delivered; timing indicates that both gestures were delivered and the push reached the threshold, but never while the key was held as the coordination failure; and force indicates that the push was on while the key was held, but never reached the threshold. Failures with two causes. An attempt can also fail for two reasons at once: the push was never on while the key was held, and it never reached the threshold. Replayed in the same world, re-timing the late hand alone opens the door in 3 of 119 attempts and a lower threshold alone in none; doing both opens it in all 119 . These 119 attempts ( 75 with the tool hand late, 44 with the push hand late) are neither a timing failure nor a force block, and we keep them as a class of their own. Data and questions. The campaign has 758 attempts on 17 doors: 174 successes, 198 wrong ( 106 of them cam-outs), 133 timing , 134 force and 119 failures with two causes; every attempt replays bit for bit. Each question is asked on the population where it makes sense: detection , failed against succeeded; diagnosis , blocked against wrong ; coordination , timing against force blocks, also split by which hand was late ( tool hand late , 68 against 134 , push hand late 65 against 134 ); and cam-out , a slipped tool against a force block with the tool held ( 106 against 134 ). The lock angle , the barrel’s own joint and the latch’s input, is reported as a mechanism reading, and the latch state (door angle, push over the threshold \tau , their conjunction) as an oracle ceiling, never as evidence.
