# HITCH: Teaching a Vision-Language Model to Operate a Humanoid through a Whole-Body Harness

## Abstract
Enabling vision-language models (VLMs) to control humanoid robots requires both reliable whole-body interfaces and knowledge of how to use the body that carries over across tasks. We present HITCH (Harness Interfaces To Command Humanoids), a harness in which perception returns the 3D position of any point the VLM selects in its image, and execution turns commands for the pelvis, torso, hands, walking, and gaze into whole-body motion with a learned tracker. We find that both measuring the scene and executing commands accurately raise task success. We then fine-tune a VLM on trajectories in which teacher policies solve five base tasks through HITCH, so that it learns how to operate the humanoid. Without task-specific trajectories, the fine-tuned model completes 51% of SIMPLE episodes and 42.5% of HumanoidArena episodes, and adding five trajectories per SIMPLE task raises its SIMPLE success to 95%. On a real G1, it carries a box and walks to a goal without real training data.

## I Introduction
General-purpose humanoid robots should carry out everyday loco-manipulation tasks from language instructions, for example, walking to a table, crouching to pick up a cup, and placing it in a basket on another table. Each of these steps requires the legs, torso, and arms to act together while the head camera keeps the target in view, and every motion must match the geometry of a scene the robot has never seen. A common approach trains vision-language-action (VLA) models [ 1 , 2 ] or world action models [ 3 ] that map observations and instructions directly to whole-body actions. These models produce fluent motion, but they usually need many demonstrations of each task, and their performance can drop when only a few are available [ 4 ] . For a humanoid, each demonstration requires whole-body teleoperation or motion planning, which makes every new task expensive to add. Agents built on foundation models offer another route, but on a humanoid they lack two things. Prompted language models and vision-language models (VLMs) already solve many tabletop tasks without task-specific training [ 5 , 6 , 7 ] . The first thing they lack on a humanoid is an interface to the whole body. Existing humanoid agents mostly select skills from a library or command task-space controllers [ 8 , 9 ] , which leave the posture of the legs and torso to the controller. A task may require the agent to set this posture itself, and the interface must execute each command accurately, since the agent observes again only after a command finishes. The second is skill in operating a humanoid body. Pretraining contains few examples of humanoid motion: a model may understand 3D space well yet know little about how far a humanoid can reach, or when it must step or crouch to bring its hands to a target. RAX acquires such knowledge through online exploration of each new task, with success that grows with the exploration budget [ 10 ] . Yet much of this knowledge is shared across tasks and could be learned once. We first build HITCH (Harness Interfaces To Command Humanoids), a harness through which a VLM observes and controls the robot. Its perception interface lets the model measure the scene: it selects a point in the image and receives the 3D position of that point from depth, so it does not have to guess distances from the image alone. Its execution interface exposes commands for each body part, such as pelvis height, torso orientation, hand waypoints, walking, and gaze. A planner converts them into whole-body references, which a learned tracker executes on a Unitree G1. We train this tracker further on the planner’s references. Given the same commands, it executes them more accurately than baseline trackers, and more tasks succeed with it. With this harness, the VLM only has to choose the next call, but nothing in the harness tells it how to choose. We therefore fine-tune Qwen3.8-27B on trajectories in which teacher policies use the same interfaces to solve five base tasks: tabletop pick-and-place, carrying objects between pieces of furniture, navigation, opening drawers and cabinet doors, and opening room doors. From these trajectories, the model learns skills for operating a humanoid through the harness, such as where to stand, how low to crouch, how far to lean, and what to measure before acting. We find that these skills carry over to new tasks in simulated benchmarks [ 4 , 11 ] , and to real scenes on a G1 without real training data. On the SIMPLE humanoid benchmark, the model trained on the base tasks completes about half of the episodes without task-specific trajectories, and adding five trajectories per task raises its success to 95%. Compared with training on the task trajectories alone, training on the base tasks as well raises success at every number of trajectories we tried. Without its perception interface, the model trained on the base tasks solves only 2.8% of SIMPLE’s episodes. On a real G1, the model completes 13 of 20 carrying and 6 of 10 navigation episodes. Our contributions are: • a harness for humanoid agents with two interfaces: perception, which measures the scene on demand, and execution, which turns body-part commands into accurate whole-body motion; • teacher policies that solve five humanoid base tasks through these interfaces, acting only on what the agent observes, and a VLM fine-tuned on their trajectories that carries over to new tasks; • experiments on two humanoid benchmarks and a real G1, covering transfer to new tasks, learning from a few trajectories per task, transfer to real scenes, and ablations of perception and execution.

## III Method
Our agent is a VLM that follows a language instruction on a Unitree G1 humanoid over a sequence of turns ( Fig. 2 ). In each turn, it makes one call: a query to a perception interface, which measures the scene, or a command to an execution interface, which moves the body. Together, the two interfaces form the harness through which the VLM observes and controls the robot. The agent learns to operate the robot through these interfaces from the trajectories of teacher policies. Section III-A defines the agent loop and two properties of this setting that shape our design, Sections III-B and III-C describe the two interfaces, Section III-D how we collect teacher trajectories, and Section III-E how we fine-tune the agent on their trajectories. III-A Problem Setting We model an episode as a multi-turn interaction between the VLM and the robot. It starts from a language instruction \ell and an initial observation o_{1} . At turn t , the agent produces a response a_{t} that contains one interface call and may contain free-form reasoning, and the call returns the next observation: a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\ell,h_{t}),\qquad h_{t}=(o_{1},a_{1},o_{2},\ldots,a_{t-1},o_{t}), (1) where \pi_{\theta} is the VLM and h_{t} is the history. The agent conditions on the whole interaction so far, so it can refer back to positions it measured in earlier turns. A perception query returns the measured positions and the image with the queried points marked. A command returns a new head-camera image and the body state after the motion, covering the pelvis, torso, feet, palms, fingers, and camera. Each item of the body state gives both the value the agent commanded and the value read from joint encoders and the IMU, so the agent can detect when a hand was blocked by an object or a crouch fell short. In a typical episode, the agent looks for the target, measures it, moves, and looks again to check the result, repeating this cycle until the task is complete. Two properties of this setting shape our design. First, the agent decides at a low rate. A VLA observes again after each short chunk of actions and adjusts its motion as it goes, whereas our agent spends seconds on each turn, and a command runs to completion before the agent observes again. The agent must therefore know where its targets are before it acts: if it could only judge positions from the image, it would have to home in on a target over several turns, moving, looking, and adjusting by the offset it sees. Its commands must also be executed accurately, with the body reaching the commanded posture and the palms their commanded positions. The next observation reveals an error, but correcting it costs another turn, and some errors cannot be undone, such as knocking the object off the table. Second, every decision in the training data must be explainable from what the agent receives: the instruction and its observations. Our teachers are code policies in simulation and could read the pose of any object from the simulator, which the agent cannot.
