# COMBS: Concealed Object Multi-modal Bayesian Search

## Abstract
This work addresses the problem of finding a concealed object in an environment by unifying formalisms for partially observable environments with multi-modal priors drawn from a mix of ontologies, semantic networks, LLMs, and VLMs. We propose a concealment-adaptive mixed-observability MDP (CA-MOMDP), a Bayes-adaptive setting where uncertainty is over both object locations and which nodes can conceal them, held as a hypothesis with a bounded ontology prior and resolved only by interaction. We define a hierarchical transition system (HTS) to abstract the environment into room and object nodes that can be planned over via our CA-MOMDP. We further propose a COMBS-score: a multi-modal fusion of a human informed semantic network, LLM, and VLM, to initialize the belief over object locations, which grounds our abstractions and reduces reliance on a single model. Belief is maintained via Bayes' rule, which never lets a look rule out a closed container. We prove a present target is found in a bounded number of searches under exact perception and that its location is never ruled out given missed detection under noisy perception. We ablate each component to demonstrate their significance. Across five simulator configurations, from a gridworld to Matterport3D with a real detector, COMBS finds 92-100% of in-distribution and 92-100% of out-of-distribution targets, matches or beats the baselines in processing speed, significantly outperforms a per-step LLM policy at lower cost on the larger scenes, recovers more out-of-distribution placements than a TidyBot ranking, and matches an LLM-only prior at a fraction of its per-decision latency. COMBS is the only standalone method that can declare a target absent at a stated error level, and the per-step LLM policy offered a termination action abandons 12-22% of targets that were present, against 1.6-3.2% for COMBS on the same episodes.

## I Introduction
Modern perception models give a robot many partial, uncertain views of a scene, but a planner needs a belief it can act on, and building that belief in a principled way remains an open problem. Building an abstraction of the environment to reason over requires grounding the perception observations, which can draw on learned models, ontologies, and knowledge of how the environment layout limits what the robot can see. Consider searching for a target object that may be fully occluded in a partially known environment. Solving this task requires principled fusion of these data streams together with a hierarchical search that accounts for perception and observation uncertainty. Semantic scene structure has largely asked what structure best represents a scene for a downstream task regardless of exploration techniques, but the structures typically do not account for what perception has not seen, meaning it becomes difficult to reason about where an object may be–a requirement for this problem [ 1 , 2 ] . Works on semantic search consider a single data source or pretrained model and only consider a fully observable environment, which are not robust to LLM-related drawbacks or out-of-distribution data [ 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] . Some works focus on searching for partially to fully occluded objects using existing models but require spatial descriptions of where an object can be found [ 8 , 9 , 10 ] . Other occluded-object search works train a network to guide container search, but explore each container in turn rather than trading off opening against searching elsewhere and fail on out-of-distribution placements [ 11 ] . Belief-based search models uncertainty about the locations of things already known to exist, rather than uncertainty over what exists at all, which is required for searching for a concealed target [ 12 , 13 ] . Manipulation-based approaches search for an occluded object by moving clutter [ 14 ] , sometimes reasoning about target-object geometry relative to the scene [ 15 ] , but they do not consider higher-level exploration. Conversely, household domain approaches [ 16 , 17 ] focus on placement, so the answer is a destination to execute rather than a hypothesis to test, and a wrong guess is never contradicted by observation. We develop a framework, Concealed Object Multi-modal Bayesian Search (COMBS) to both leverage environment hierarchy though abstraction and semantic knowledge through multi-modal data fusion to find an object without any additional input or location descriptions. Our contributions are: 1) a hierarchical transition system (HTS) whose edges are the robot’s actions and whose nodes grow only when an action reveals objects, the structure concealed-object search requires, since a hidden object is reached only by opening what holds it; 2) the CA-MOMDP, a factored Bayes-adaptive mixed-observability formulation, planned over the HTS, in which the robot’s uncertainty covers not only where the target is but which nodes could be hiding it; and 3) a multi-modal prior, fused in log-odds with bounded influence from any single source, which initializes the CA-MOMDP’s belief.

## III Methodology
To solve Problem 1 propose the COMBS pipeline, shown in Figure 1 . We propose a hierarchal transition system (HTS) to provide a search structure with edges as actions with cost and states that represent contents not yet observed, so a belief over concealed objects can be planned, updated, and proven complete directly on the HTS (Def. 2 ). The HTS is initialized with rooms and their corresponding belief from zero-shot priors. We cast the search as a concealment-adaptive mixed-observability MDP (CA-MOMDP) to reason over the chance an object is present or absent and visible or occuluded (Sec. III-E ). The robot follows the search policy and updates existing nodes’ values or adds new nodes based on its observations until the target is found or reported absent. III-A Environment abstraction To perform search we require a structure that can represent the environment concisely, the way an agent can interact with the environment, and the cost associated with those actions, while maintaining belief over the environment understanding. Definition 1 (Scene) A scene is a finite set of rooms, joined by the doorways of a given floor plan, and a finite set of objects. Each object is an instance of a semantic class drawn from a finite class set AP and is located in exactly one room or inside exactly one other object. A class may have any number of instances. An object that holds objects is a container, and a container may hide its contents from view, in which case they are unknown until it is opened. Containment nests so the contents of a container may themselves be another container. We plan over a scene to find a target of semantic class l_{G}\in AP such that cost is minimized. Since a container may hide its contents, no structure built prior to the run can list every state; states must instead appear as the agent acts on the scene. Since the robot’s actions are physical, (e.g., crossing a doorway, opening a container, or examining a place without leaving it) each action carries a cost. Relational graphs such as a scene graphs do not natively capture such costs. We therefore consider a weighted transition system, which is a tuple of states, actions, a transition relation, a weight function assigning each transition a positive length, initial states, atomic propositions, and a labeling function L recording which l\in AP hold at each state [ 20 ] . A weighted transition system has no containment among states, so we extend it into the hierarchical transition system. Definition 2 (Hierarchical transition system (HTS)) The HTS of a scene is a weighted transition system in which the states \mathcal{V} are the scene’s rooms together with one state per room class per location, the state standing for all instances of that class located in that room or container; the labeling function L assigns each state the singleton holding its class, written L(v)=\{l_{v}\}\subseteq AP ; and layer \mathcal{V}^{k}\subseteq\mathcal{V} holds the states at nesting depth k , the rooms being \mathcal{V}^{0}\subseteq\mathcal{V} . The transition relation is the union of E_{\subset}:\mathcal{V}^{k}\times\mathcal{V}^{k+1} , one containment transition from each state to each state located in it; E_{\leftrightarrow}\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{0}\times\mathcal{V}^{0} , one adjacency transition per doorway; and a self-loop at every state, the transition that acts at a state without leaving it. The actions are the choices of transition to execute, and the initial state is the robot’s start room.
