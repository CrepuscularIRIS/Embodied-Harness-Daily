# PyRUA-DataFarm: LLM Agent as a Cost-Efficient Teleoperator for Real-Ready Robot Data

## Abstract
Teleoperation remains the default way to collect robot demonstrations, but every episode costs robot and operator time, and every new task starts from scratch. We ask whether a coding agent can take over the teleoperator's job. In our DataFarm, the agent receives a data request and one calibration episode of the robot in the target scene and rebuilds the task in simulation: it writes the scene, an expert controller, and a success judge. The simulator supplies exact actions, and a video model conditioned on real images renders each rollout as photographic video, generating all camera views together consistently. The output is real-ready data: synchronized camera views labelled with the robot's actions, on which a policy can be trained directly and deployed on the real robot. Independent judges certify the expert and every rendered episode before admission. On five real-robot tasks, policies trained only on farm data match policies trained on as many teleoperated episodes (93 vs. 84 successes in 100 trials), and under an unseen tablecloth and coloured light they keep 87 successes where the teleoperation-trained policies fall to 46. Adapting a task is fully automatic and costs \$21 of API usage on average, paid once per task, plus \$5 for a render contract; every further episode costs \$0.80 on average for simulation, rendering, and review, with no robot or operator. One hour of three-view data for a task costs \$107 on average, these costs included, slightly below a quote of \$117 for teleoperation in China and below \$290 in labour alone in North America.

## 1 Introduction
Teleoperation remains the primary source of training data for robot policies [ 25 , 2 , 3 ] . Its appeal is that the data are faithful by construction: every recorded action was executed by the robot, and every image shows the scene in which the policy will be deployed. Its weakness is cost. Each episode requires a robot, an operator, and resets after failures, so the cost grows with the amount of data and starts over with every new task, scene, or viewpoint. Synthetic data promises to break this scaling, yet existing pipelines trade the cost of collection for two other costs. The first is human engineering: whether demonstrations are multiplied from a few seeds [ 34 , 49 ] or tasks are written by language models [ 46 , 6 ] , a person still has to assemble and debug the scene, the expert, and the success check for every new task. The second is the visual gap between simulation and reality. Real-to-sim-to-real methods narrow it by reconstructing the scene [ 44 , 16 , 37 ] , but whatever is hard to reconstruct, such as deformable cloth or a lit display, still looks synthetic, while video generation models that synthesize the motion themselves can no longer provide exact action labels [ 22 ] . Producing real-ready robot data, exact enough to train on and realistic enough to deploy on the real robot without any real demonstrations, cheaply for every new task therefore remains an open problem. We address this problem with DataFarm , an agentic data farm in which a coding agent takes over the teleoperator’s role, a simulator supplies the actions, and a video model supplies the appearance (Figure 2 ). Given a data request and a single calibration episode, a short recording of the robot in the target scene, the agent compiles the task into simulation as an engineer would: it reconstructs the scene and cameras from the calibration episode, verifies that the simulator can reproduce the required skills, and writes an expert controller together with a success judge. The simulator then executes the expert from many initial states to produce trajectories with exact actions and states, and a video model conditioned on real frames renders each trajectory, all camera views in one generation, in the appearance of the target scene, where new appearances such as a different tablecloth or lighting are specified in text. The output is real-ready: synchronized, mutually consistent photographic camera views with the robot’s own action labels, which a policy consumes exactly as it would consume teleoperated data. Because the agent harness is decoupled from individual tasks, a new task requires no task-specific engineering by a person, and once a task is compiled, every further episode is produced without a robot, an operator, or a reset. Delegating data production to an agent, however, raises a question that teleoperation never faces: who verifies the data? An agent that writes both the expert and its success checks may approve its own mistakes, and a video model asked to look realistic may copy the layout of its references, hallucinate objects, or drift from the simulated motion. DataFarm therefore separates certification from production. The expert is developed against a judge it cannot inspect and admitted only on held-out initial states, the renderer may alter appearance but not geometry, objects, or timing, and every rendered episode is compared with its simulated state by machine checks and an independent reviewer before it enters the dataset. Experiments on five bimanual tasks covering rigid, articulated, deformable, and display-bearing objects show that this design yields real-ready data at low cost: policies are trained on it directly and deployed on the real robot (Figure 1 ). Policies trained only on farm data match those trained on the same number of teleoperated episodes in the original scene (93 vs. 84 successes in 100 trials) and remain robust under a tablecloth and coloured light absent from both training sets (87 vs. 46), which we attribute to the appearance variation the farm adds around fixed, exact trajectories. Rendering with real images is essential to this transfer: the same trajectories rendered with conventional domain randomization yield only 26 and 21 successes (Section 6.1 ). Every camera view of a farm episode both follows the simulation and looks real: image statistics place farm episodes more than twice as close to real camera frames as the simulator’s own renderings, and the farm’s renderer keeps all views faithful to the simulated motion, whereas calling the video model directly does not (Section 6.4 ). The farm also operates without human intervention: adapting a task costs $21 of API usage on average, paid once per task, a render contract adds $5, and every further episode costs $0.80 on average for simulation, rendering, re-rendering, and review, in the original scene or under new appearance, so 100 episodes of a task cost $1.06 per episode on average, and one hour of synchronized three-view data [...]

## 3 Problem Formulation
DataFarm takes a data request and returns a dataset together with the evidence that certifies it. A request \rho=(e^{c},\ell,n,\mu) consists of a calibration episode e^{c} , a natural-language description \ell of the task and its success condition, the number of episodes n , and a data mode \mu . The calibration episode is a short multi-view recording of the robot in the target scene, which identifies the scene and the cameras but never serves as a source of actions. The data mode is either sim2real , which reproduces the recorded scene, or \textsc{ood}(\mathcal{V}) , which additionally permits the appearance variations \mathcal{V} , such as a new tablecloth. No adapter, judge, or prompt for the task exists in advance. The farm delivers n episodes d_{i}=(o^{i},a^{i},x^{i},k^{i},y^{i},q^{i}) , each consisting of photographic multi-view observations o , simulator actions a , simulator states x , contact events k , task labels y , and admission evidence q , together with a ledger \mathcal{L} that records every attempt and its outcome. 3.1 Capability layer and task artifacts The farm separates what is shared across tasks from what is produced for each task. The shared part is the capability layer \mathcal{K} , which comprises skills for the stages of Section 4 , role instructions, render templates that no task may override, and the harnesses, ledgers, and verifiers that execute and check the work; every run is pinned to a fixed version of \mathcal{K} . For each request, the agent then generates the task artifacts \mathcal{A}_{\rho}=\{\,G,\;T,\;S,\;C,\;J,\;R,\;B\,\} (1) namely a grounding record G , a task contract T , a sampler S of scenes and initial states, an expert controller C , a judge specification J , a render contract R , and the batch and dataset configurations B . This separation has two consequences. First, a missing adapter is never a missing input, because the agent produces every artifact itself; an artifact made for an earlier task serves only as an example, and its thresholds, authorizations, and prompts are never loaded for a new task.
