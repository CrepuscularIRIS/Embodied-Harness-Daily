# X-Nav Technical Report: Real-World Long-Horizon Indoor-to-Outdoor Navigation on a Humanoid Robot

## Abstract
Long-horizon navigation from indoors to outdoors remains an open problem for humanoid robots: one instruction covers rooms, doors and roads, and the camera moves with the gait. Worken, AGIBOT's measure of productive robot work, depends on robots that reach their workplaces autonomously. We present X-Nav, to our knowledge the first system to take a bipedal humanoid through a language-instructed long-horizon mission from indoors to outdoors. X-Nav covers a pipeline from data to deployment. Its data engine collects navigation data in open-source scenes, in digital twins of deployment sites and on real robots. Its navigation model, MELIOR, takes only RGB images from the robot's built-in camera and comes in two versions: MELIOR-A predicts actions, and MELIOR-N predicts a navigation goal. Its agentic workflow, X-Navarch, turns a brief human instruction into sub-tasks and deploys MELIOR on the robot with explicit target navigation and obstacle avoidance. On the Lingxi X2, X-Nav completes a mission of about a hundred metres from an office lobby to a store in a neighbouring building; LiDAR and odometry are used for target approach and low-level control. The mission is completed with both versions of MELIOR: the robot follows a change of destination and walks through a door from the lobby to the road, and with X-Navarch it also passes static and dynamic obstacles on the route. During deployment, MELIOR is adapted with data collected in a digital twin of the site; the adaptation raises its closed-loop success rate in the twin, and the adapted model is then deployed on the robot at the real site. In addition, MELIOR achieves competitive results on the R2R-CE and RxR-CE benchmarks.

## 1 Introduction
A humanoid robot that works in the real world must first reach its place of work. Given one instruction, for example to go outside and buy a coffee at a store in a neighbouring building, it has to cross rooms, pass through doors, and walk along roads to its destination. We refer to such a task as a mission . AGIBOT measures productive robot work by Worken , the product of active robots, autonomous working hours, and output efficiency ( AGIBOT, 2026 ) , and each factor depends on robots that reach their workplaces autonomously. This report studies missions in which a humanoid robot walks about a hundred metres from indoors to outdoors ( Figure 1 ). Vision-language navigation (VLN) formalises this task: an agent follows a language instruction using its visual observations ( Anderson et al., 2018 ) . Progress on its benchmarks has been rapid. Navigation models built on video-based vision–language models have raised the success rate on the continuous-environment benchmarks R2R-CE and RxR-CE year after year ( Krantz et al., 2020 ; Ku et al., 2020 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2025a ; Cheng et al., 2025 ; Wei et al., 2026b ; Zeng et al., 2026 ) . On R2R-CE it rose from 37% to above 70% within two years ( Table 4 ), and on RxR-CE the best reported system now matches the success rate of human followers ( Zhou et al., 2026a ) . Over the same period, training sets grew to more than ten million samples ( Zhang et al., 2026c ; Zhou et al., 2026a ) , and real-robot deployments came to include quadrupeds and humanoids ( Cheng et al., 2025 ; Wei et al., 2026a ; Li et al., 2026 ) . Has embodied navigation had its “GPT moment”? On the benchmarks, it looks that way; on real robots, not yet. Where a route length is reported, real-robot runs cover a few tens of metres ( Zhang et al., 2026c ; Li et al., 2026 ) . Reported trials on humanoids stay either indoors or outdoors ( Cheng et al., 2025 ; Wei et al., 2026a ; Li et al., 2026 ) , and systems that cover larger sites follow a route from map software or from a spatial memory built in advance ( Tan et al., 2026 ; Zhou et al., 2026b ) . To our knowledge, no reported system has taken a bipedal humanoid through a language-instructed mission of about a hundred metres across the indoor–outdoor boundary. Why do high scores in simulation fail to carry over to real robots? We trace the gap to three places: the problem that a benchmark poses, the data that models learn from, and the step from a trained model to a walking robot. We start with the problem: a benchmark episode is a narrower task than a mission. An episode covers ten to fifteen metres inside a single building. The scene is a scan, the camera is simulated and stays at a fixed height, every step is executed exactly as commanded, nothing else in the scene moves, and stopping anywhere within three metres of the goal counts as success. A mission on a humanoid breaks each of these assumptions. The route is several times as long and leaves the building, where walls a few metres away give way to open ground and buildings tens of metres away. The camera moves with the gait, and the step that the robot takes can differ from the one commanded. People, carts, and cars cross its path. Nor is stopping within three metres good enough: a robot sent to a store has to arrive at its entrance. A high score therefore shows that a model follows instructions under benchmark conditions; it says little about the conditions that the benchmark leaves out. We therefore take the mission itself as the problem, and define it as follows. A bipedal humanoid receives an instruction in natural language, and the instruction may change during the mission. Its navigation model sees only RGB images from the robot’s built-in camera, and the robot has neither a map of the site nor a reference route. The route is about a hundred metres long, starts indoors, and ends outdoors, with static and dynamic obstacles along the way. The mission is complete when the robot stops at the destination named in the instruction. Will more data close the gap? Scaling up the training set has been the usual response ( Zhang et al., 2026c ; Zhou et al., 2026a ) , and it does push benchmark scores up. But the data are the second place where the gap opens, because a sample can teach only what it contains. Standard VLN data are collected in scanned indoor scenes with a simulated camera ( Chang et al., 2017 ) , the same kind of setting that the benchmarks use. Such samples contain neither the site where the robot will work nor the robot’s own camera, gait, and stopping behaviour, and adding more of them cannot supply what is missing. Reference routes also show only correct behaviour, whereas a model on a long route has to recover from its own mistakes. More is better only when it adds what is missing: the first question is what kind of data, not how much. What kind of data does a humanoid need, and where should it come from? ## 2 X-Nav Overview
X-Nav consists of three parts ( Figure 1 ). The data engine collects navigation data through three complementary routes: open-source scene collection, digital-twin collection and real-robot collection. MELIOR , the navigation model, takes an instruction and RGB frames from the built-in camera of the Lingxi X2 and predicts actions ( MELIOR-A ) or a navigation goal ( MELIOR-N ). X-Navarch , the agentic workflow, divides a mission into sub-tasks, runs the navigation skills on the robot, and passes their goals through obstacle avoidance to the locomotion controller. Real-robot deployments of navigation models commonly mount an additional camera on the robot, for example an Intel RealSense D455 ( Zhang et al., 2025a ; Wei et al., 2026b ; Wei et al., 2026a ) . X-Nav instead uses the cameras that the Lingxi X2 already has: MELIOR navigates with the built-in head-mounted RGB camera, MELIOR-X additionally uses the built-in RGB-D camera, and no camera is added to the robot. Table 1 compares X-Nav with recent navigation systems. These systems predict waypoints or actions from RGB input, and those that cover larger sites follow a route or a spatial memory that is prepared in advance. The navigation model of X-Nav takes a single RGB stream, the system uses no site prior, and it is demonstrated on a mission of about a hundred metres from indoors to outdoors on a bipedal humanoid.
