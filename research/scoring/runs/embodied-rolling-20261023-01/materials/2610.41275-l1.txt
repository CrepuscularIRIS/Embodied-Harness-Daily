# TRACE: Target-State Risk-Aligned Conditional Execution for Embodied Tracking

## Abstract
Embodied visual tracking requires a robot to follow a language-specified person under occlusion, look-alike distractors, and ambiguous instructions. We show that improving tracking persistence can simultaneously increase safety violations. Using a compact 0.6B vision-language-action (VLA) tracker with a frozen backbone, we update only the planner head with proximal policy optimization (PPO). PPO improves success and following rates across all three EVT-Bench settings, but increases the collision rate on distracted tracking because episodes that previously ended in target loss can instead end in contact. We introduce TRACE, an execution-time target-state risk gate that preserves the learned policy and intervenes selectively. A lightweight risk head processes 84 cues, including candidate boxes, identity margins, a learned range-and-bearing probe, and the policy's commands. When the calibrated risk exceeds a threshold, TRACE rescales the translational component of the next waypoint while leaving yaw unchanged. The executed action is identical to PPO's on more than 98% of steps. In paired evaluations on identical episodes, TRACE reduces the distracted-tracking collision rate by 3.96 percentage points, while further improving success and following rates. Matched slowdown controls without target-state timing do not reproduce this improvement. Concurrent evaluation reproduces a collision reduction of 3.77 points, compared with 0.29 points for identical-arm null pairs, indicating that the effect exceeds evaluation variability. These results show that target-state-aware execution can recover the safety cost of policy post-training with minimal intervention.

## 1 Introduction
Embodied visual tracking (EVT) requires an agent to continuously follow a language-specified person from egocentric observations over long horizons ( Luo et al., 2018 ; Zhong et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) . The task requires the joint solution of target identification, temporal association, and motion planning under occlusion, look-alike distractors, and ambiguous instructions. Recent vision-language-action (VLA) models integrate visual perception, language understanding, and robot control ( Brohan et al., 2023a ; Brohan et al., 2023b ; Kim et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2024 ) . The strongest reported results on EVT-Bench come from a 7 B-scale model trained on large-scale data ( Wang et al., 2025 ) , whereas deployment favors compact models with limited adaptation cost and low execution overhead. We construct a compact three-stage tracking system based on a frozen Qwen3-0.6B backbone. The supervised base model keeps the vision encoder, language backbone, and recognition adapter fixed, while a planner head predicts eight future waypoints. We then apply PPO ( Schulman et al., 2017 ; Schulman et al., 2015 ) only to the planner head, which improves success and following rates across all three EVT-Bench settings: the planner head is an effective control point for tracking persistence, and the representation backbone need not be updated. The improvement carries a safety cost in distracted tracking. Relative to the supervised model, PPO reduces early target loss but increases collisions, changing the terminal outcome of some episodes from target loss to physical contact (Figure 1 A). This observation exposes a limitation of optimizing persistence with a scalar task reward. Existing safe-control and recovery methods ( Achiam et al., 2017 ; Ray et al., 2019 ; Johannink et al., 2019 ; Thananjeyan et al., 2021 ; Ames et al., 2019 ; Dalal et al., 2018 ) typically impose geometric constraints or respond to local collision conditions. They do not explicitly model the target-state uncertainty that precedes collisions during distracted tracking. In our experiments, a distance-only execution filter does not recover the safety degradation introduced by PPO (§ 4.4 ). These results motivate an execution-time mechanism that uses the tracker’s target-state estimate to intervene selectively while preserving the persistence learned by PPO. We therefore introduce TRACE , a target-state risk gate applied after policy prediction (Figure 1 B). A lightweight head receives 84 target-related cues, including candidate bounding boxes, identity margins, a learned range-and-bearing probe, and the policy’s commands and predicted waypoints. The head produces a collision-risk score. Once the score exceeds a calibrated threshold, TRACE rescales the translational component of the next waypoint while preserving its yaw component. At all other steps, the executed action is identical to the PPO action. The gate reduces the distracted-tracking collision rate (CR) from 13.78 to 9.83 , below the supervised baseline, while further improving success and following rates (SR, FR). Our contributions are as follows. • A compact VLA tracking stack with an empirical analysis of post-training safety costs. We develop a 0.6 B frozen-backbone VLA tracker and restrict PPO post-training to its planner head. This design improves success and following rates on all three EVT-Bench settings but raises the distracted-tracking collision rate from 11.01 to 13.78 , establishing a measurable trade-off between persistence and collision safety under planner-only post-training. • TRACE : selective target-state risk gating for safe execution. We introduce an execution-time gate that predicts collision risk from target-state cues and modifies only the translational component of the next waypoint when the risk exceeds a calibrated threshold. On distracted tracking, TRACE reduces the collision rate by 3.96 percentage points while increasing the success rate by 2.03 points and the following rate by 2.14 points, at approximately 50\,\mu s per step and fewer than 2\% of executed actions changed. Matched slowdown controls without target-state timing remain at the PPO reference, and the effect persists across two scene corpora, three collision geometries, held-out episodes, and a later code version. • Concurrent evaluation for separating intervention effects from benchmark variability. Re-evaluating an unchanged policy shifts collision rate by up to 1.4 points and success rate by up to 2.7 , so we evaluate the gated and ungated policies concurrently together with identical-arm null pairs. Under this protocol, TRACE produces a collision-rate change of -3.77 points, compared with -0.29 points for identical-arm pairs, providing a paired estimate of the intervention effect relative to evaluator variability.

## 3 Method
Problem and overview. At each step t , the agent receives a language instruction I that specifies the target and an egocentric RGB history O_{t} . A compact VLA tracker maps (I,O_{t}) to an 8{\times}3 waypoint sequence W_{t}=\{w_{1,t},\ldots,w_{8,t}\} , where each waypoint is w_{i,t}=(v_{x},v_{y},\omega) . Only the first waypoint is executed through a PD controller, after which the agent observes again and replans. An episode is successful when the agent keeps the described target within the evaluator’s following band without terminating in collision. Our goal is to increase persistence without converting lost-target failures into collisions. The construction has three components: a compact supervised tracker whose recognition components are then frozen (§ 3.1 ), PPO on its planner head alone (§ 3.2 ), and a gate that rescales the next waypoint only when the risk score is high (§ 3.3 ; Figure 2 ). Each component leaves the previous one unchanged, so we can measure the effect of each separately. 3.1 A Compact VLA Tracker The base tracker couples a vision encoder, a Qwen3-0.6B language backbone, and a recognition adapter that projects visual tokens into the language space, followed by a planner head that emits the 8\times 3 waypoint sequence. It is trained by supervised fine-tuning (SFT; Appendix B ) and then serves as the fixed reference: the encoder, backbone, and adapter are never updated again, so every later change is attributable to the planner head or the gate.
