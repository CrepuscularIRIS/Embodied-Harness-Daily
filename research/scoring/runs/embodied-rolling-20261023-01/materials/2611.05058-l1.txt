# Do Agentic Policies Need to Look at Every Step? Geometric Abstraction for Reasoning and Action

## Abstract
Recent multimodal language models can reason directly about robot scenes, but it is unclear whether they need to repeatedly reinterpret raw visual observations at every step of an interaction. We propose shifting routine spatial reasoning from images to text and symbols through a 3D geometric abstraction that is refreshed locally from observations and read directly by the language models. Specifically, our interface represents scene geometry via typed, evidence-aware symbolic references to object parts, grounds them from calibrated RGB-D, and returns compact textual checkpoints with metric residuals after each action. Symbolic task structure can thus be reused across decisions while metric values are rebound to the current scene, with direct visual reasoning invoked only on demand. Across offline policy synthesis and continuous online execution, reasoning over grounded text and symbols improves multi-step manipulation completion while substantially reducing repeated multimodal queries, positioning explicit geometric state as an effective substrate for agentic robot policies.

## 1 Introduction
Recent frontier multimodal models have demonstrated increasingly strong 3D spatial reasoning and, in some cases, can directly drive robot manipulation without task-specific policy training [ 24 , 10 , 19 , 17 ] . However, deploying such models in closed-loop manipulation often requires repeatedly reinterpreting visual observations as the scene evolves, even though many manipulation decisions are governed by a compact set of task-relevant geometric quantities [ 14 , 26 ] . Repeatedly recovering these quantities from raw images introduces both reasoning uncertainty and additional computational cost: metric and structural relations remain challenging for vision-language models, whereas explicitly representing spatial information in text can improve reasoning reliability [ 21 , 15 , 18 , 23 ] . Moreover, visual tokens often carry redundancy, incurring unnecessary overhead during reasoning [ 2 ] . This raises a central question: does a robot agent need to reinterpret the visual scene at every decision step? Existing interfaces trade off richness and compactness. Raw images retain detailed scene information but require repeated visual interpretation, while conventional perception APIs often expose only coarse object-level quantities such as centers or bounding boxes. ReKep [ 7 ] shows that explicit 3D keypoints and relational constraints can provide an effective interface for manipulation. Meanwhile, language-model-based robotics is moving toward persistent and structured policies: Code as Policies [ 12 ] synthesizes executable programs, ASPIRE [ 13 ] extends this paradigm through execution–diagnosis–repair and skill reuse, and Graph-as-Policy [ 1 ] represents policies as modular graphs. These works make the policy itself structured and reusable, raising a complementary question: what representation of the 3D physical scene should such policies reason over? To address this question, we propose shifting routine spatial reasoning from images to text and symbols through an explicit 3D geometric abstraction (Fig. 1 ). Rather than reducing objects to single 3D centroids or vague natural-language descriptions, our abstraction models scene geometry as a structured set of typed 3D object parts—including centers, vertices, edges, and oriented surfaces—annotated with timestamps and evidence status ( observed , inferred from shape priors, or unavailable under occlusion). Local perception grounds this 3D state from calibrated RGB-D and exposes it to the language model in two forms. First, symbolic references (e.g., red.bottom_face , blue.top_face ) and geometric operators (such as surface alignment and support checks) let policies compose actions independently of specific object coordinates. Second, compact textual checkpoints report updated 3D coordinates, metric goal residuals, and evidence status after each action. Separating reusable symbolic structure from scene-dependent metric grounding lets the language model reason directly in text and symbols, invoking visual reasoning on demand when richer scene context is needed. We study this textual and symbolic 3D interface in two regimes. In policy synthesis , the language model revises an executable policy between rollouts using execution feedback, while geometric grounding and control run locally during rollouts. In online execution , the agent repeatedly reasons and selects skills within a single continuing episode using the updated textual checkpoint after each action, without resetting the scene after failures. Both settings use matched sensors, actuators, and initial skills. Across both regimes, our experiments show that reasoning over grounded text and symbols accelerates policy acquisition, substantially improves multi-step online construction completion, and reduces repeated multimodal queries and interaction latency. We use these two settings to examine when explicit textual and symbolic 3D state is sufficient for manipulation and when direct visual reasoning remains necessary. Our contributions are threefold: (i) we formulate a 3D geometric abstraction that exposes task-relevant scene geometry to language models as structured text and symbols—combining typed, evidence-aware part references with reusable symbolic structure and local metric grounding; (ii) we instantiate this abstraction as a reasoning-to-action interface with composable geometric operators, local RGB-D grounding, and compact textual checkpoints; and (iii) we empirically evaluate the interface across policy synthesis and continuous online execution, characterizing its impact on manipulation completion, model interaction cost, and the remaining role of direct visual input.

## 3 Geometric Abstraction as a Reasoning-to-Action Interface
Our interface represents scene geometry through typed symbolic references that are grounded in current RGB-D observations at execution time. Updated geometry and goal residuals are returned as compact textual checkpoints for subsequent reasoning (Fig. 2 ). 3.1 Grounding geometry from visual observations At time t , calibrated RGB-D observations from the side and wrist cameras, together with robot proprioception q_{t} , define the current geometric state: o_{t}=\big(\{I_{t}^{c},D_{t}^{c},K^{c},T_{WC}^{c}\}_{c\in\{\mathrm{side},\mathrm{wrist}\}},q_{t}\big),\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{t}=\Phi(o_{t};M), (1) where M stores object shape information acquired during initialization. Depth observations are back-projected into the calibrated world frame and combined with M to estimate each object’s current center, orientation, dimensions, and, when supported by the observation, its vertices, edges, and faces. Each geometric element additionally records whether it is directly observed, inferred from the initialized shape model, or unavailable. A core invariant is that shape may persist, but pose must be refreshed : after an object moves, its pose must be re-estimated from a current observation rather than copied from the previous state or inferred from commanded motions. If the required geometry cannot be recovered, the corresponding reference is marked unavailable. 3.2 Symbolic references and geometric operators Policies written by the language model access the geometric state through typed references. A reference r=(i,\rho) identifies object i and a geometric role \rho (such as a center, vertex, segment, or face) and is evaluated against the current state: \llbracket i.\rho\rrbracket_{\mathcal{Z}_{t}}=\begin{cases}(v,\tau,\eta,t),&\text{if the reference is currently supported},\\ \bot,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases} (2) where v is the metric geometric value, \tau its type, \eta its evidence status, and t its observation timestamp. This turns scene geometry into a compositional vocabulary (Table 1 ). Instead of reasoning directly from images, a policy can refer to red.bottom_face and blue.top_face , align the two faces, and request the corresponding placement transform.
