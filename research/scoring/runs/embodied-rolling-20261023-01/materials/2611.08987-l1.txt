# VLA Handover: Failure-Prediction-Based Switching from On-Robot to Server VLA Models

## Abstract
Large vision-language-action (VLA) models generalize well but are difficult to run in real time on a robot, and small VLAs that run there fail more often. Remote inference over beyond-5G and 6G networks lets a robot use a large VLA on a server, but querying the server for every action chunk limits the number of robots per server. We propose VLA handover: the robot runs a small VLA (S-VLA) and switches to a large VLA (L-VLA) on a server only when the S-VLA is likely to fail. We formulate the switching as minimizing the request cost under a required success probability and, since this problem cannot be solved directly, propose a heuristic framework that retrains neither VLA. In simulation with SmolVLA and $π_{0.5}$ on three of the four LIBERO suites, VLA handover raised the success rate by 11-22 percentage points over the S-VLA alone and sent 49-77% fewer requests than server-only control at a 2-4 percentage-point lower success rate, and its completion time grew less with the network latency.

## I Introduction
Robot foundation models have shown strong performance in robot manipulation, in particular vision-language-action (VLA) models [ 1 , 2 ] , which map camera images and a natural-language instruction directly to robot actions. These models rely on vision-language models with billions of parameters and are therefore difficult to run in real time on the computer mounted on a robot. Small VLAs such as SmolVLA [ 3 ] are designed to be deployed on consumer-grade GPUs or CPUs, but they generalize less well: on the LIBERO-Long suite [ 4 ] , SmolVLA succeeded in 35.0% of the episodes in our evaluation, whereas \pi_{0.5} [ 2 ] succeeded in 93.2% (Sec. IV ). Remote control of robots over low-latency, high-reliability beyond-5G and 6G networks [ 5 ] allows a robot to use a large VLA: the robot sends the information required for inference, such as images and its state, to a VLA on an edge or cloud server, and the server returns the output actions. As the number of robots grows, however, the communication traffic and the server load become the bottleneck and limit the number of robots that one server can accommodate. In our setting ( \pi_{0.5} on an NVIDIA GB10 without batching), a server accommodates only about two robots when every action chunk is inferred on the server; the number depends on the server but is inversely proportional to the requests per robot. In addition, the communication and computation latency delays each action; in dynamic scenes, or when the robot cannot hold still while waiting, it can also lower the task success rate. Existing schemes that combine an on-robot model with a server VLA involve the server in the inference of every action chunk. RoboECC [ 6 ] , RAPID [ 7 ] , and ComVLA [ 8 ] partition the inference of one VLA between the edge and the cloud, adapting the split point to the network bandwidth or the number of transmitted visual tokens to the channel capacity. AsyncVLA [ 9 ] runs a large VLA on a workstation and an on-robot edge adapter asynchronously, and CloudEdgeVLA [ 10 ] combines delayed cloud features with current local vision; both train the server and on-robot models jointly. The requests of these schemes do not decrease when the on-robot model would succeed, and the latter two cannot be applied to existing VLAs without retraining. Using the server only when it is needed raises the question of when to switch, which is a trade-off: a switch that is too early sends requests in episodes that the small VLA would complete alone, whereas a switch that is too late comes after a critical mistake of the small VLA or too close to the deadline, so that even the large VLA cannot complete the task. Related decisions have been studied in other settings. In cloud robotics, Chinchali et al. [ 11 ] learn when to offload perception inference from the uncertainty of the on-robot model and the network cost, and Penmetcha et al. [ 12 ] predict whether to offload an application from the execution time; these works decide per inference of a perception or application module, whereas our decision concerns which policy controls the robot for the rest of a closed-loop episode. In interactive imitation learning, a robot requests a human supervisor when it is at risk, under a budget of interventions [ 13 ] , with fewer switches [ 14 ] , or from a limited pool of remote supervisors shared by a fleet [ 15 ] ; the helper is a human, and the communication and computation latency of the help is not modeled. In this paper, we propose VLA handover : the robot runs a small VLA (S-VLA) and switches to a large VLA (L-VLA) on a server only when the S-VLA is likely to fail. Fig. 1 illustrates the system and an example episode. This paper makes the following contributions. Formulation and framework. We formulate VLA handover, i.e., deciding when to switch from an on-robot S-VLA to an L-VLA and to which of the L-VLAs on multiple servers, as the minimization of the expected request cost subject to a required task success probability (Sec. II ). Since this problem cannot be solved directly, we propose a heuristic single-server framework in which a handover manager estimates failure scores of the S-VLA, decides from them when to switch, and resets the arm and switches the model (Sec. III ); neither VLA is retrained. Proof-of-concept experiments. In a proof of concept with SmolVLA and \pi_{0.5} on four LIBERO suites under an emulated latency, we evaluate the requests, the success rate, and the completion time against the alert threshold and the latency, and report both the suites where VLA handover reduces requests at a small loss of success and the suite where it falls well short of server-only control (Sec. IV ).

## II System Model and Problem Formulation
II-A System Model VLA models. A VLA model takes the robot state, camera images, and a natural-language instruction as input. A vision-language model (VLM) processes these inputs, and an action head converts the VLM output into robot actions. With action chunking, the action head generates a chunk of H actions for the next H control steps from a single observation; the robot executes n ( \leq H ) of them and then queries the model again with a new observation o_{t} . A request to a remote VLA therefore carries the images, the state, and the instruction, and its response carries a chunk of actions. System. Fig. 1 shows the system, which consists of a robot, an S-VLA \pi^{\mathrm{S}} and a handover manager on the robot, and K servers, each running an L-VLA \pi^{\mathrm{L}}_{k} , k\in\mathcal{K}=\{1,\dots,K\} . Time is indexed by control steps t with period \Delta , and an episode ends at task success or at the step limit T_{\max} , which we treat as a task requirement (Sec. IV discusses its length). Every n steps, i.e., at each chunk boundary, the handover manager decides from the history h_{t} of observations and model inferences whether the S-VLA keeps control ( u_{t}=0 ) or \pi^{\mathrm{L}}_{k} takes over ( u_{t}=k\in\mathcal{K} ). After a handover, every chunk inferred by \pi^{\mathrm{L}}_{k} is one request to server k ; each request returns after the latency D_{k}=D_{k}^{\mathrm{up}}+D_{k}^{\mathrm{comp}}+D_{k}^{\mathrm{down}} , the sum of the uplink, computation, and downlink latencies, and incurs the cost c_{k} for communication and computation. Let N_{k}(\phi) be the number of requests to server k in an episode under the switching policy \phi:h_{t}\mapsto u_{t} , and N_{\mathrm{req}}=\sum_{k}N_{k} the total number of requests per episode. II-B Problem Formulation The decision variables are when to switch and to which L-VLA, both of which are determined by \phi . We minimize the expected request cost, i.e., the cost per request times the number of requests per episode, subject to a required task success probability P_{\mathrm{req}} : \min_{\phi}\ \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}c_{k}\,N_{k}(\phi)\Big]\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad P_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi)\geq P_{\mathrm{req}}, (1) where P_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi) is the task success probability under \phi . P_{\mathrm{req}} acts as a task-level QoS requirement, and N_{\mathrm{req}} determines the load that each robot places on the network and the server. The choice of the L-VLA affects both the objective and the constraint: a more capable L-VLA recovers more states, whereas a distant or loaded server has a larger D_{k} and c_{k} . We expect P_{\mathrm{s}} to decrease with the time until a handover takes effect, i.e., the switching time plus D_{k} , in episodes that the S-VLA would fail alone, for two reasons. First, once the S-VLA has made a critical mistake, such as dropping an object where the gripper cannot reach it, the L-VLA cannot recover the episode; we denote by \tau_{0} the unobservable time after which the episode cannot be completed.
