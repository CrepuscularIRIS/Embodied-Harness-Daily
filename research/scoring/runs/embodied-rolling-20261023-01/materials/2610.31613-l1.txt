# Progress Probes $P^2$: Lightweight Runtime Monitoring for Vision-Language-Action Models

## Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models can perform diverse manipulation tasks directly from visual observations and natural language instructions, but they remain prone to mistakes and unpredictable failures. Deploying these policies without continuous human supervision will require robust runtime monitors that detect when the policy has gone off-course. Existing monitors use external foundation models, such as vision-language models (VLMs) to supervise the policy, introducing significant complexity and computational cost for monitoring at runtime. In this work, we investigate an alternate approach: \textit{can a VLA's own internal activations provide the information needed to monitor its execution?} Our method trains linear probes on policy activations from demonstrations to estimate progress, then flags executions that deviate from expected progress. In experiments with the open-source VLA $π_{0.5}$ and across simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, this approach is competitive with or outperforms VLM-based monitors in task failure detection, as well as methods which require failure data for training. These findings suggest that simple probes of VLA internals can be used to develop reliable runtime monitors, opening a path toward building policies that can act reliably and monitor their execution without external foundation models.

## I Introduction
Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) have made strong progress toward the goal of generalist, language-conditioned robot policies. Models such as \pi_{0.5} [ 1 ] and GR00T [ 2 ] can execute diverse, multi-stage manipulation tasks across a variety of tasks and embodiment and, especially after task-specific fine-tuning, can achieve relatively high mutli-task success rates. However, even well-trained policies still experience a wide variety of failure modes, (e.g., poor language grounding [ 3 ] , deadlocks, and mistakes [ 4 ] ) which are often sudden and difficult to predict. A natural framework for addressing such failure modes during deployment is runtime monitoring: using an external verifier or monitor to detect when the policy’s execution is straying from typical/successful deployment. Existing approaches obtain this monitoring signal from several sources. Sentinel [ 5 ] uses an external VLM, along with an action consistency heuristic, to judge when the policy is failing to make progress on a particular task. Generalist reward models, such as Robometer [ 6 ] , learn to judge task progress from datasets of robot videos. Other methods instead use policy internals directly for failure detection. For example, SAFE [ 4 ] trains a failure classifier to predict rollout success directly from policy activations, while FIPER [ 7 ] combines an internal-based anomaly score and an uncertainty metric over actions to flag anomalous rollouts. In this work, we investigate a different use of policy internals for runtime monitoring. Specifically, we build on recent successes in mechanistic interpretability for VLAs [ 8 , 9 ] , which demonstrated that the activations of the VLM backbone contain easily-decodable representations of task-relevant features such as end-effector pose, object states, and task phase. We hypothesize that a VLA’s internal activations may also encode task progress : how far the policy has progressed through a task. In successful executions, progress advances predictably over time (monotonically decreasing), while in suboptimal or failed executions, the progress probe falls behind the elapsed time. Furthermore, task progress can be learned entirely from successful executions and can be labeled without human supervision. We propose progress probes—linear decodings from the VLA’s activations to task progress—as lightweight internal-based runtime monitors. We study progress probes by training them on the \pi_{0.5} [ 1 ] VLA using a dataset of success-only task executions. At deployment, runtime monitoring consists of taking the VLA’s internal activations (that are already computed as part of the forward pass of inference), passing them through the linear progress probe, and measuring the deviation between the current estimated task progress and the expected task progress computed from successful demonstrations. Not only does our probe-based monitor require only successful data for training, but it also runs between 5\times and 20,000\times faster than an external foundation-model monitor. Across simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, we find that linear probes can indeed accurately predict task progress for demonstrations, and that they provide an effective signal for detecting unsuccessful policy rollouts. Our monitor achieves competitive or superior failure detection performance with both Robometer and SAFE baselines, while providing fast inference and requiring only successful demonstrations for training. This result indicates that even when VLAs fail to generate successful actions, their activations still retain information useful for detecting task progress and failure, which can be obtained using a simple linear readout. Specifically, our contributions are as follows: 1. We demonstrate empirically that linear probes trained over demonstration data can accurately decode task progress from \pi_{0.5}^{\prime}s vision-language representations, across simulated and real-world manipulation tasks. 2. We develop a minimalist progress-based runtime monitor which uses these probes to detect deviations from expected progress at runtime, without failure data or an external VLM. 3. We evaluate our monitor on 6 VLABench tasks and 6 real-world manipulation tasks, achieving AUROC scores of 0.788 and 0.78 respectively, matching or exceeding competitive baselines including SAFE and Robometer.

## IV Problem Formulation: Runtime Monitoring
Our goal is to detect, during execution, when a VLA policy \pi(\mathbf{a}_{t}\mid o_{t}) is unlikely to complete its task. We follow the finite-horizon runtime monitoring setup of Sentinel [ 5 ] . Setup. We observe a stream of robot observations and actions up until any time t\geq 0 : let \bm{\tau}_{t}=(o_{0},a_{0},\ldots,o_{t}) be this observation-action trajectory. Let H denote the maximum task horizon. Finally, we define the rollout outcome Y\in\{0,1\} , where Y=1 if the policy reaches a successful state within H steps and Y=0 otherwise. Monitor. A runtime monitor , s(\bm{\tau}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}, is a map from the observation-action trajectory to a scalar value measuring how much the data differs from successful or “nominal” data (i.e. larger values imply more different from successful). Given a threshold \delta\in\mathbb{R} and a trajectory \bm{\tau} , let the detection time (i.e., the time at which the monitor detects deviation from success) be T_{\delta}=\inf\{t\mid s(\bm{\tau}_{t})>\delta\} , with T_{\delta}=\infty if the monitor never fires. Monitor Design Objective. The objective of runtime monitoring is to design a s(\bm{\tau}) which (a) reliably discriminates successful trajectories from failures and (b) detects failures early. Formally, this is a multi-objective design problem: \min_{s}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\tau}}\big[\operatorname{FPR}(\delta,\bm{\tau};s)+\lambda\cdot\operatorname{DetectTime}(\delta,\bm{\tau};s)\big], (1) for a given threshold \delta , where \lambda>0 is a parameter balancing the two objectives. Here, the first term is the false positive rate of the monitor, \operatorname{FPR}(\delta,\bm{\tau};s)=P(T_{\delta}\leq H\mid Y=1) . The second term is the mean detection time on failed trajectories, \operatorname{DetectTime}(\delta,\bm{\tau};s)=\mathbb{E}[T_{\delta}\mid Y=0,T_{\delta}\leq H] . An ideal runtime monitor minimizes false positives while also firing early under the true trajectory distribution (i.e., \tau should ideally be drawn from the distribution of closed-loop evaluation trajectories). Goal. We hypothesize that we can design s(\bm{\tau}) to be a linear probe (described in Sect. III ) trained on the VLA’s activations, achieving monitoring quality while being lightweight to run at inference time.

## V Progress Probes as Runtime Monitors
In this work, we propose using progress probes ( P^{2} ), lightweight linear readouts of task progress from a VLA’s internal activations, as runtime monitors. Our key insight is that a VLA’s internal activations encode how far the policy has progressed through a task, and in successful executions this encoded progress advances predictably (monotonically) with elapsed time. Suboptimal or failed behaviors violate this relationship, and thus the progress probe falls behind the elapsed time. Practically, task progress is also a useful signal for designing a runtime monitor because it can be easily labeled without human supervision from offline data (because it decreases monotonically on successful data) and can be learned without explicit failure data.
