## Abstract

Reported systems differ in harness and test subset and often change several designs at once, so it is hard to tell which choices produce the gains. ## 1 Introduction
Frontier multimodal models can now control robots without any robot-specific training. GPT-6 Astra ( OpenAI, 2026b ) drives a bimanual arm through a harness, the software that turns each of its decisions into end-effector targets for a motion planner ( Zhang et al., 2026 ; Galbot Team et al., 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026a ) . No vision-language-action (VLA) model sits in between. The model looks at camera images, decides where each gripper should go next and reads the outcome, much as a computer-use agent looks at a screen and decides where to click ( Xie et al., 2024 ) . By analogy such a system is called a robot-use agent (RUA) ( Isola, 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026b ) . On the RoboDojo simulation benchmark ( Chen et al., 2026a ) , RUAs built on GPT-6 Astra have improved quickly, and each new system adds components of its own, such as depth and point clouds ( Qu et al., 2026 ; Zhou et al., 2026b ; Huang et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026 ) , affordance models ( Kim et al., 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026b ) , in-context demonstrations ( Guo et al., 2026 ; SenseNova and Joint Research Team et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) and self-improvement loops that write and refine their own skills ( Li et al., 2026a ; OpenSenseNova, 2026 ) . Our starting point was a discrepancy. The first evaluation of GPT-6 Astra on RoboDojo found it weak on tasks that require precision under the official L3 harness ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) , and a broader study saw a similar gap between its task decisions and reliable physical control ( Galbot Team et al., 2026 ) . Yet a few reasonable fixes to the harness and the action loop, with no elaborate design on top, brought its Precision score to the level of the strongest VLA models of the time ( Section 3 ). We came to suspect that much of the reported weakness lay in the harness rather than in the model. If the harness matters this much, the gains reported by recent systems are hard to read. These systems start from different harnesses, run on different subsets of the benchmark with different budgets, and often change several parts of the design at once. A reader who sees a higher score cannot tell whether it comes from the new component, from the loop it was added to or from the episodes it was measured on. Reproducing each system in full would carry these differences over. We instead take the designs apart and test them on one testbed and one evaluation set, changing as little as we can between runs. We start from the official L3 harness and group the designs that recent systems add along four axes ( Figure 2 ): the harness around the model, the action interface, the reuse of experience from other episodes and the scene geometry in each observation. Three tools keep the comparison affordable and readable. Since the official evaluation of 2,100 episodes is costly to repeat for every design, we build a 111-episode evaluation set that covers all 42 tasks and spends more episodes on the tasks with high evaluation uncertainty. Since a score says how much of a task was completed and not how the attempt went, we review every first-round episode from its video and count behaviours the score does not show, such as empty grasps and dropped objects. And since an episode resends its whole history at every decision, we report what each configuration costs per episode. A design is kept only if it helps on the evaluation set at a reasonable cost and does not draw on the tasks being scored. The result is JustRUA. It keeps three kinds of change to the official harness. The loop keeps the full episode history and stops each motion after 20 control steps, the observation carries the robot’s own grip readings and depth predicted from RGB, and the model may run code to solve 3D coordinates from pixels. With no learned policy, no self-improvement loop and no task-specific tuning, it raises the evaluation-set score of the official harness from 29.7 to 50.3 ( Figure 1 ). Our findings are as follows. • A lean harness goes a long way ( Section 3 ). Keeping the full episode history and stopping each motion after 20 control steps lift the score from 29.7 to 38.0. Relative targets, named skills and model-chosen step counts do not beat plain absolute targets. • Reusing experience is not a free lunch ( Section 4 ). Demonstrations and a self-written skill library change how the model acts, but neither clearly beats the default, and both make an episode more expensive. They also draw on the tasks being scored, so their scores are not comparable with a standard evaluation. • Scene geometry helps most ( Section 5 ). Depth images cut empty grasps from 41 to 16 in the first round, even when Depth Pro predicts the depth from RGB alone and is off by tens of centimetres. With code that solves 3D coordinates from pixels and no decision cap, the score reaches 50.3. • Remaining failures are mostly long-tail ( Section 6 ). ## 2 Controlled Study Framework
Instead of reproducing each reported system, we take the designs apart and test them on one testbed and one benchmark, changing as little as we can between runs. The framework has to meet two conditions. It must carry out the actions the model chooses reliably, so that we measure the model and its interface rather than the accidents of the loop around them. And it must tell the effects of different designs apart at a cost we can pay many times over. This section describes the benchmark and the harness we start from, the testbed, the four axes, the evaluation set, and the review of videos and logs that we use to count physical behaviours. 2.1 Benchmark and Starting Point RoboDojo ( Chen et al., 2026a ) follows earlier bimanual simulation benchmarks such as RoboTwin 2.0 ( Chen et al., 2025 ) and contains 42 simulated bimanual tasks in five capability groups, Generalization, Precision, Long-horizon, Memory and Open. Each task is scored from 0 to 100, where full success scores 100 and many tasks give partial credit for intermediate stages. The official evaluation runs 50 episodes per task on different layouts, 2,100 episodes in all, and averages tasks within each group and then the five groups with equal weight. Our starting point is L3 Inspect EEF, the official L3 harness released with RoboProbe ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) and the one behind the GPT-6 Astra entry on the RoboDojo leaderboard. RoboProbe sorts model-driven robot systems by what sits in the action path, and at its third level (L3) the model acts through a fixed, non-learned harness with no learned policy in between. Each episode opens with the task instruction and a task recipe from the RoboDojo wiki, which describes the task and its scoring stages without giving an action sequence. At each decision the model sees three RGB views, from the head and both wrists, and the measured grasp-point and joint state of both arms. It acts by calling move_eef with world-frame targets for the grasp point of either arm, given as position, orientation and gripper opening, or give_up to end an episode it judges unrecoverable. Non-learned code plans a joint path to each target with CuRobo ( Sundaralingam et al., 2023 ) , the simulator plays it back, and the next observation reports how far the grasp point ended up from its target. The model decides what to do next and where to move, and the planner decides how the arm gets there. The model receives no depth, object poses, layout metadata or scoring signal, and the simulator alone decides whether an episode succeeds.
