# HospitalBench: Diagnosing Where and Why Embodied Agents Fail on Long Hospital Task Chains

## Abstract
Long-horizon embodied tasks fail in ways that end-task success cannot see. HospitalBench is a diagnostic benchmark of ten hospital chores in NVIDIA Isaac Sim, each two unscored warm-up steps followed by a causal chain of five scored sub-tasks in one continuous simulation: the state one step leaves (an object pose, a choice, a fact seen once) determines what the next step must do, unlike benchmarks that place sub-tasks side by side. Vision-language agents act through skill-level tools from camera images. A scripted robot that makes only the calls a model gets is the solvability check of a chore. From each trajectory the benchmark reads where a chain breaks, what the agent did after a failed call, and how far its own position estimate drifts from the true one. This version reports first results only: 15 of 60 planned runs (three models at high effort, one run each on chores T1 and T10 from both start states and T3 from one), with scripted-robot proofs on three of the ten chores. Of the 15 runs, 2 passed all five sub-tasks. Runs still going after sub-tasks 1 to 5 were 15, 8, 4, 2, 2. Of 189 failed calls, 168 were followed by a different call and 111 by a call that did not fail. These are first results and not a full benchmark. We will release the scenes, task chains, scripted robot, evaluation harness and trajectories.

## 1 Introduction
Where along a chain of dependent chores does an embodied agent’s performance degrade, and why? Long-horizon benchmarks report that success falls as tasks get longer [ 22 , 28 ] , and recent work traces failures to the seams between skills [ 35 , 24 ] , but one end-task score cannot say at which step, at which link or by which kind of failure a chain breaks. A chain shows degradation by carrying only if each step needs the actual result of the one before, or a fact seen earlier: a trolley parked a few centimetres off its mark moves every grasp that follows, whereas a card seen once names the forceps to take and is gone by the time they are needed. HospitalBench is built for this diagnosis: ten chores of five dependent sub-tasks (50 sub-tasks, 40 links) in six simulated hospital areas ( Sec. 3 , Tab. 2 ), each link depending on the step before it in one of four ways ( Fig. 2 ). A model sends every robot call and a controller executes it. A walk is one model at one effort tier (the reasoning-effort setting of its API call) running the sub-tasks of one chore in order, with no reset (a chain ), from one start state (the initial scene). The scene state is the poses of the robot, objects and people, which every pass term and harm event reads. A walk ends at its first failed sub-task. The number passed before it is its passed in a row score. The scripted robot is a hand-written program that makes only the calls a model makes. The calibration model is the model whose walks were used to choose the version of a chore (Opus 5.5 high), so its results are not held out. Held-out models (GPT-6.1 Terra, GPT-6.1 Sol) were not used for that choice ( Sec. 4 ). What we find so far ( Sec. 5 ). This version reports first results only. We ran Opus 5.5 high, GPT-6.1 Sol high and GPT-6.1 Terra high once on each of five cells (chores T1 and T10 from both start states, T3 from start 0), 15 runs of the 60 planned, outside the staged protocol of Sec. 4 . The scripted robot has a proof on those chores and starts, and on none of the other seven chores. Two runs passed all five sub-tasks, both on T1 (GPT-6.1 Terra from start 0, GPT-6.1 Sol from start 1). Runs still going after sub-tasks 1 to 5 were 15, 8, 4, 2, 2. Every other run ended at its first failed sub-task, and in 7 of those 13 runs this was the first free sub-task T#.02, never the guided T#.01. On T1, 3 of the 6 runs that reached T1.02 failed it. Two took the forceps that a card shown only in the first picture of T1.01 named to keep, and one never picked either forceps. After a failed call the next call differed in 168 of 189 cases and did not fail in 111. With one run per cell there is no interval and no ranking of models, and these are first results, not a full benchmark. The other seven chores have no model run. Failure modes and harm events. We read four failure modes from a trajectory ( Sec. 4 ): compounding errors per link, recovery , state drift and tool-use breakdowns . This version reports the last three from the 15 runs ( Sec. 5.1 ). Compounding by landing position and harm events are defined but not analysed here. Harm events (an instrument left off the tray, the robot closing in on a person) are simulator proxies and not injuries. Honest scope. No single task is new: every kind of action in our tasks is in a task prompt of seven existing benchmarks, and five further 2026 benchmarks are in the same comparison ( Tab. 1 ). The design property we add is link dependence, tested link by link (the link test , Sec. 3.2 ) and through a fact shown once. The link test has been run on 1 of the 40 links, for the scripted robot on an earlier T1 version. The rest have only a paper check on assumed numbers ( Sec. 3.2 ). Only T1, T10 and T3 have model runs. No method has been trained on HospitalBench, and we do not claim it works as a training environment. Contributions. (1) A diagnostic benchmark of ten hospital chores, each five dependent sub-tasks in one reset-free simulation, with the kind of each of the 40 links stated, a link kind for a fact shown once, and 36 named harm events ( Sec. 3 ). (2) A staged protocol (stage 0 calibration, an alone check M1, held-out stages 1 to 3) that reports the calibration model apart from held-out models, and the first 15 runs, made outside it ( Sec. 4 ). (3) First results on three chores with per-run records of where a chain ended, what followed each failed call and how far the robot’s position estimate drifted, and a read of the T1.01 \to T1.02 link ( Sec. 5.1 ).

## 3 The benchmark
Rooms. Each of the ten chores has its own room, built with Isaac Lab-Arena [ 4 ] (commit 3e2c70b) and furnished with 74 distinct NVIDIA SimReady assets [ 6 ] (of 2,595 asset folders, each at its pinned revision, card revision 4763fbf). 44 keep their real joints (instrument hinges, drawers, doors, brakes, casters), so a step can fail through a moving part, for example forceps opening when gripped away from the joint. Three missing items (hazardous-drug cabinet, sharps container, waste-bag holder) are flagged stand-ins (supplement Sec. D, rooms: Fig. S1). Every item to pick fits the 8 cm gripper. Robot. A wheeled mobile manipulator (Ridgeback base, 7-DoF Franka arm, parallel gripper opening to 8 cm, a head camera and a wrist camera) in NVIDIA Isaac Sim 6.1.0 [ 5 ] , build 6.1.0-rc.26, a release candidate: results are tied to this build, and we have not rerun on another. The model sees the sub-task instruction and, at every call, four pictures (head RGB, head depth, arm RGB, arm depth) with the base pose and gripper width. In the runs of Sec. 5 only the pictures of the latest call are kept in its context (earlier ones are replaced by a text marker), and it acts through skill-level calls, one call per skill: pick , place , press , pull_trolley , push_pole , open_drawer , scan_over and arm_home (12 tool names appear in the logs, no run called move_to or gripper ), together with drive_to (x, y, heading_deg, speed, accel), look (pan_deg, tilt_deg), wait (seconds) and done (). drive_to drives the base (speed at most 0.6 m/s, default 0.3, accel default 0.3 m/s 2 ), look turns the head camera, wait holds still (people and doors keep moving) and done ends the sub-task. A controller executes each call. The model chooses every one. The world is paused while the model thinks. Each sub-task has a call cap (T1: 18, 12, 24, 48 and 66 calls for T1.01 to T1.05).
