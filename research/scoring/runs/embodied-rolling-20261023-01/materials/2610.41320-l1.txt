# Learning from Physical Experience:Context-Space Adaptation for Robotic Manipulation

## Abstract
Recent multimodal foundation models exhibit strong perception, reasoning, and in-context learning capabilities, but translating these capabilities into physical action remains challenging. Through real-world experiments across diverse manipulation tasks, we find that ChatGPT-6-Astra, a state-of-the-art general-purpose reasoner, can serve as a physically actionable, context-responsive manipulation policy, demonstrating effective fine manipulation and spatial reasoning. Building on these findings, we introduce LEXP, a harness for learning from physical experience through context-space adaptation. LEXP combines task-level planning with an experience module that interprets execution evidence, extracts and retains lessons with applicability conditions, and reuses relevant lessons to generate contextual feedback for subsequent actions. Model weights and the action interface remain fixed throughout. Across 12 real-world manipulation tasks, LEXP achieves $23/36$ successful trials (63.9\%), compared with $19/36$ (52.8\%) with memory alone and $2/36$ (5.6\%) for vanilla Astra. On three tasks, bread transfer, sort trash and toiletries, and retrieve drawer, removing planning reduces success from $7/9$ to $4/9$, while removing retained experience reduces it to $3/9$. Execution traces illustrate feedback-guided corrections and cross-episode reuse of an inspection strategy. Execution analyses illustrate how task structure and experience-derived feedback guide task progression, action correction, and experience reuse. These results support context-space adaptation as a practical mechanism for turning physical interaction into reusable guidance for robotic manipulation.

## 1 Introduction
A long-standing goal of robotic manipulation is to perform diverse tasks reliably in unfamiliar and changing physical environments. This requires robots to adapt when familiar strategies fail and reuse what they learn from physical interaction. The challenge is to turn limited physical experience into reliable, transferable improvements in behavior. Existing approaches pursue this goal through different adaptation mechanisms. Vision–language–action (VLA) models learn from robot demonstrations through supervised fine-tuning [ 11 , 22 ] . World action models [ 29 ] aim to reduce task-specific adaptation by jointly predicting video and actions, enabling zero-shot execution of unseen tasks and environments on pretrained embodiments, while related video-model policies [ 10 ] instead use demonstration-based fine-tuning and rollout-informed planning . Real-world reinforcement learning improves policy parameters through repeated physical interaction [ 21 ] , but requires reward specification and infrastructure for physical trials and resets [ 21 , 17 , 18 ] . These requirements make demonstrations, reward engineering and physical trial infrastructure central to the cost of adaptation. General-purpose multimodal reasoners offer a complementary interface: observations, instructions and execution feedback can condition executable tool calls without updating model weights. Their relevance to robotics depends on whether this contextual flexibility supports effective action revision and reusable improvements under a limited physical interaction budget. We investigate this possibility through real-world experiments with GPT-6 Astra [ 20 ] , across 12 robotic manipulation tasks covering fine manipulation, spatial reasoning, long-horizon execution, tool use, bimanual coordination, and force control. Across these tasks, we vary access to interaction history, and examine recovery demonstrations and online language guidance. Two complementary findings emerge. First, Astra shows promising fine-manipulation and spatial reasoning capabilities when given access to interaction history: interaction history inform subsequent corrections and help the robot reason about object relations and pose. Second, its behavior is steerable through context. Visual demonstrations and textual corrections support revisions to grasp checking, object repositioning, motion direction, arm selection, and action order. These benefits vary across tasks, but together highlight both Astra’s executable competence and its capacity for context steering—two prerequisites for improving manipulation through experience-derived context rather than additional parameter training. Building on these observations,we introduce LEXP, a harness for learning from physical experience through context-space adaptation (Figure 1 ). A context compiler combines task instructions, current observations, interaction history, and agent-generated experience feedback to condition executable actions. A planner decomposes the task into subtasks, tracks progress, and proposes a method for the current subtask. The experience module evaluates this method against recent actions, measured execution outcomes, and current observations, drawing on retained lessons to generate feedback for the actor’s next decision. It also extracts and updates structured lessons from physical interaction, recording their applicability, supporting evidence, and limitations for subsequent reuse. This separates interaction history, which preserves past exchanges, from reusable experience, which retains findings beyond the history window and across attempts. The models remain frozen and the action interface stays fixed; adaptation occurs through changes to the context guiding execution. The design builds on reflective context optimization [ 1 , 3 ] and prior physical adaptation systems [ 16 , 28 , 6 , 34 ] . Across 12 real-world manipulation tasks, LEXP achieves 23/36 successful trials, compared with 19/36 with memory alone and 2/36 for vanilla Astra. Ablations and execution analyses further examine how task-level planning and retained experience support task progression and subsequent action decisions. Our contributions are threefold: • A real-world evaluation suite spanning 12 manipulation tasks to investigate executable capabilities, adaptation through interaction history, and context steering in a general-purpose multimodal reasoner. ## 2 Problem Statement
2.1 Objective: improve policy behavior through reusable experience Our objective is to improve robotic manipulation by converting physical experience into reusable guidance for subsequent behavior. Supervised fine-tuning encodes demonstrated actions in model parameters, while reinforcement learning adjusts a policy using rewards from interaction. We pursue the same practical goal to improve performance but move the variables being optimized from model parameters to the context that conditions the policy. With model weights and the robot interface fixed, contextual guidance can change how the actor advances the current subtask, approaches an object, verifies a grasp, and responds to failure. Context-space Adaptation. Let \pi_{\theta} denote a pretrained actor with fixed parameters \theta , and let \mathcal{I} denote its fixed robot-control interface. Given a task instruction l and a current observation o_{t} , the actor selects an action conditioned on a compiled context: a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{t}),\qquad c_{t}=\mathcal{C}(l,o_{t},H_{t},f_{t}), (1) where H_{t} is memory consisting of interaction history, f_{t} is contextual guidance, and \mathcal{C} is the context compiler. Executing a_{t} through \mathcal{I} produces an execution record r_{t} and a subsequent observation o_{t+1} . The resulting evidence e_{t}=(o_{t},a_{t},r_{t},o_{t+1}) records the proposed action, its execution result, and the observed physical consequences. We implement this adaptation through structured, modular context updates [ 32 ] . After each interaction, new evidence is appended to memory: H_{t+1}=\operatorname{Append}(H_{t},e_{t}) . Let M_{t} denote retained experience and p_{t+1} denote the planner’s current subtask and proposed method.
