# Q-OIL: Optimistic Exploration in Learning from Human Interventions

## Abstract
As robots are deployed in the real world, they will inevitably make mistakes. Human interventions provide valuable feedback for improving their behavior, enabling them to adapt online to new scenarios. However, studying human-in-the-loop robot learning is difficult: real-world experiments are slow and expensive, so most algorithm development relies on simulated human models. We show that common human modeling assumptions do not match how real people intervene in practice. Through analyzing intervention data from human participants, we find that humans tend to intervene when agent progress stalls, and that their corrective actions are often suboptimal. Motivated by these findings, we introduce a progress-based model of human intervention that achieves higher rank correlation with real-user algorithm rankings, enabling realistic algorithm benchmarking and development in simulation. Building on these insights, we propose Q-OIL, a method that adds optimistic bonuses to intervention transitions in reinforcement learning and softly guides exploration toward human corrections. We find that decoupled critics are critical for stability because they prevent the bonuses from backing up to pre-intervention states. As a result, Q-OIL learns more efficiently than competitive baselines, achieving 10-50% higher success in simulation and on real robots, and improving pretrained VLAs on two bimanual manipulation tasks. As we ground both evaluation and algorithms in realistic human behavior, this work provides a practical path toward scalable robot learning from interventions.

## 1 Introduction
Robot policies trained offline on large-scale datasets are brittle [ 1 ] , often struggling to generalize beyond their training distributions [ 2 ] and failing across diverse testing conditions. For robust deployment, robots must adapt online from feedback [ 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] . As robots fail around human observers, we can rely on human feedback through corrections [ 7 , 8 , 9 ] , where humans intervene when the robot gets stuck or makes mistakes. Such signals arise organically in the real world; for example, users may physically steer a manipulator toward the goal [ 10 , 11 , 6 ] or take over in self-driving systems [ 12 ] . Interventions can be viewed as more than resets or recovery actions [ 12 ] . Corrective actions convey implicit preference signals, i.e., information about failures in the robot’s behavior and potential ways to improve [ 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ] . Despite recent progress in robot learning from interventions, algorithm development remains difficult because real-world interaction is slow and expensive. Simulation offers a scalable alternative [ 21 ] . For intervention learning, we also require synthetic human models to determine when and how to intervene [ 16 ] . These enable repeatable evaluation and systematically guide algorithmic components (e.g., how to incorporate corrections, the exploration-safety trade-off, robustness to noise) before deployment. However, common synthetic human models and learning algorithms are insufficiently grounded in real-world behavior. They make assumptions that we show are not reflective of real human intervention data . This creates a gap between methods trained on synthetic human models and their performance when exposed to real users. We need a principled understanding of when and how humans intervene, both to guide our algorithmic design choices and to evaluate these methods. We first analyze real human interventions in robot manipulation. Our analysis suggests both how to better model human interventions and how to learn from them. First, we find that rather than reacting to instantaneous suboptimality, humans intervene when the robot’s progress stagnates over a horizon. This pattern aligns with cognitive models of caregiving: humans act on the agent’s ability over time rather than on isolated states [ 22 ] . Given this finding, we propose a progress-based synthetic intervention model that accounts for value improvement over a horizon, better matches observed humans, and yields algorithm rankings more consistent with real human evaluation (Fig. 4 ). Second, this analysis shows that real corrections are suboptimal and can diverge from the learner policy during training, which suggests that algorithms for learning from interventions must be robust to noisy, divergent interventions, i.e., use feedback to adapt robot behavior without compromising asymptotic performance. This motivates our key algorithmic insight: use interventions as optimistic guidance for exploration, rather than optimal supervision. This speeds up training without compromising asymptotic performance. Building on these insights, we introduce Q-OIL ( Q - O ptimistic I ntervention L earning), a simple online RL framework that learns from real human corrections and efficiently handles diverse users and suboptimal interventions. We start by providing an additional positive reward, i.e., an optimism bonus, to intervention transitions. Intuitively, the optimism bonus uses interventions to guide exploration toward potentially successful behaviors. The influence of optimism naturally decays as interventions become less frequent, shifting learning back to the original task reward. To make this stable, we introduce a critical decoupling of optimism from TD learning [ 23 ] : the optimistic critic drives exploration and policy updates, while a task-only critic provides TD targets, preventing catastrophic bonus propagation to non-intervention states. Importantly, Q-OIL is agnostic to the underlying off-policy RL algorithm, allowing it to be incorporated into different policy learning frameworks. We demonstrate this across simulated and real-world robot manipulation tasks with human participants, and further scale Q-OIL to improve pretrained vision-language-action (VLA) policies on challenging manipulation tasks. In summary, we make the following contributions: 1. We analyze real data to ground synthetic human models for realistic simulation evaluation and informed algorithm design. Our findings show that interventions occur when robot progress stagnates, motivating a progress-based model that promotes improved experiment design. 2. Based on human analysis, we propose Q-OIL , a simple algorithm that treats interventions as optimistic guidance for exploration rather than optimal supervision targets. 3. Across diverse real and simulated robot manipulation tasks with human participants, Q-OIL learns faster, reduces human effort, and remains robust to suboptimal and diverse interventions.  [...]

## 3 Problem Statement
We build on the framework of interactive imitation learning, focusing on learning from human interventions [ 7 ] . Consider an interactive control setting over an MDP \mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{R},\gamma,\rho_{0}) , where \mathcal{S} is the state space, \mathcal{A} is the action space, and \mathcal{T},\mathcal{R},\rho_{0},\gamma are the transition kernel, (sparse) reward function, initial state distribution, and discount, respectively. The objective is to learn a policy \pi_{\theta}(a|s) that maximizes expected discounted return, i.e., \pi_{\theta}=\arg\max_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\big[\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t})\big] . During deployment, a human observes the agent and may intervene. We model the human as a decision function \mathcal{H}=(g,\pi_{h}) with two components: a gating function g that decides when to intervene, and a human policy \pi_{h} that decides how to intervene. To allow temporal context, both may depend on a recent history \tau_{t-L:t}=(s_{t-L},a_{t-L},\dots,s_{t}) , so, g:\mathcal{S}^{L+1}\times\mathcal{A}^{L}\to[0,1] outputs the probability of intervening at time t , and \pi_{h}:\mathcal{S}^{L+1}\times\mathcal{A}^{L}\to\mathcal{A} returns a distribution over actions. The resulting rollout policy is the mixture \pi^{\prime}(a\mid s_{t},\tau_{t-L:t})\;=\;g(\tau_{t-L:t})\,\pi_{h}(a\mid\tau_{t-L:t})\;+\;\big(1-g(\tau_{t-L:t})\big)\,\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s_{t}) . Thus, modeling human behavior requires simulating g and \pi_{h} . In the following sections, we (i) collect and analyze real human interventions to characterize when and how people intervene (Section 4 ), (ii) propose a non-Markovian progress-based model for g validated against this data (Section 4.2 ), and (iii) leverage this analysis to develop a new algorithm addressing key failure modes of prior approaches in learning from interventions (Section 5 ).
