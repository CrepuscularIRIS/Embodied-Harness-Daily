# Learning to Live: A Step Toward Lifelong Embodied Intelligence

## Abstract
Embodied agents are usually organized around individual tasks, yet the physical world keeps changing between tasks and during execution. We introduce LIFE (Lifelong Intelligence from Experience), a framework in which a robot observes continuously, remembers selectively, and turns experience into new capabilities. RoboBeats, a family of lightweight streaming gates, tracks conditions set by the agent and discovers new events, deciding when an observation warrants a response or a memory write. Selected observations enter Plastic Memory, a flat store of images and structured records that keeps the source evidence, so new fields and indexes can be added when later questions need them. When existing tools fall short, Explore-Distill-Mount (EDM) turns solutions found during problem solving into validated, reusable tools and skills. Under matched protocols, RoboBeats substantially improves condition tracking over its base models and, at the same writing budget, retains more useful evidence for memory question answering than uniform sampling. A case study of limited scale, replaying 24 hours of continuous video recorded by a real robot, illustrates how the integrated system builds memory from a day of experience and uses it to answer everyday questions. LIFE takes a step toward robots that become more familiar with their surroundings and more capable through experience: learning not only to complete tasks, but to live in the world.

## Introduction: From Individual Tasks to Lifelong Embodied Agents
We hope for robots to participate in everyday human environments over extended periods, understanding changes, accumulating experience, and gradually learning to handle new situations. A robot on its hundredth day in a building should be more familiar with it than on its first: the people it has encountered, the places it has visited, and the problems it has handled should help it make better judgments and find more effective solutions. We call this goal Learning to Live and study how embodied agents can continually build knowledge and capabilities through interaction with the world. Many embodied-agent systems are organized around user-specified tasks: they interpret an instruction, select or compose skills, and execute actions toward the requested goal ( Ahn et al., 2022 ; Huang et al., 2022 ) . Yet the physical world continues to change both between tasks and during execution. Objects may move, people may arrive, and seemingly incidental events may become relevant later. This creates a mismatch between the agent’s discrete reasoning and tool calls and the world’s continuous evolution. Scene descriptions, success detection, and human feedback can help agents revise their plans during execution ( Huang et al., 2022 ) . A relevant change may require a response before the next planned decision, while a completed tool call may leave the intended physical outcome uncertain: reaching a location, for example, does not confirm that the object sought is still there. The agent therefore needs ongoing observations to update its understanding, bring relevant changes into its decision process, and verify outcomes throughout interaction. Sustained observation must also be practical over long periods. Continuous visual streams contain both brief, consequential events and large amounts of repetitive information, while the relevance of an observation to future tasks may not yet be known ( Zeng et al., 2026 ; Gorlo et al., 2026b ) . Processing every observation with an expensive model would increase computational cost and response latency, while retaining and indexing every observation without selection would make memory increasingly redundant. The system must identify when further analysis is warranted and decide what experience to preserve. At the same time, memory should retain enough evidence to support future questions that were not anticipated when an observation was made. Recent work has explored continuous visual interaction and proactive memory, including systems that decide when to respond or record evidence from video streams ( Yao et al., 2026 ; Zeng et al., 2026 ) . Embodied agents have also used long-term visual and spatial memory to answer questions and guide navigation ( Anwar et al., 2024 ; Hu et al., 2026 ) , while systems such as BetaMem and ABot-AgentOS study memory evolution and integrated robot reasoning ( Liu et al., 2026 ; Tian et al., 2026 ) . These approaches provide a basis for studying how ongoing perception, experience, and capability development can work together in a robot system. To address these challenges, we introduce LIFE (Lifelong Intelligence from Experience) , a framework that connects proactive perception, long-term memory, reasoning, and autonomous learning. LIFE continuously screens observations for changes relevant to the current context or potentially useful in the future. It turns selected experiences into retrievable historical evidence and uses that evidence to support decisions, verification, and further exploration. As the agent encounters problems that existing methods cannot handle, it can also develop and retain new tools and skills, allowing accumulated experience to contribute to future capabilities. LIFE’s proactive perception is implemented by RoboBeats , a lightweight gate that tracks specified conditions and detects new events in streaming video. It decides when an observation should trigger a response, further perception, or memory writing, so that a large model does not have to analyze every frame. Figure 1 summarizes its evaluation across streaming perception and memory question answering benchmarks. Selected experiences enter Plastic Memory , which stores observations with their temporal and spatial context and keeps the source images ( Anwar et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2025 ) . Structured records give fast access through people, places, objects, and events, while the retained images let the agent recover details that were not extracted when the experience was recorded. Writing only selected observations keeps memory compact, and keeping the images leaves it usable for questions that were not anticipated. The information needed for a new problem may already exist in retained observations even when the agent lacks a way to extract or connect it. ## LIFE Overview: From Experience to Knowledge and Capabilities
LIFE connects continuous observation, persistent memory, and capability development through an agent that reasons and acts on its experience, building on work in proactive visual interaction and embodied memory ( Zeng et al., 2026 ; Hu et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Tian et al., 2026 ) . As shown in Figure 2 , the agent sets attention conditions, queries Plastic Memory, and invokes tools to answer questions or act in the world. RoboBeats screens incoming observations for conditions that require a response and events worth recording. A writer turns selected observations into memory, which supplies evidence for subsequent reasoning. When the available tools cannot meet a new need, EDM provides a process for developing and retaining additional methods. The shared tool and skill layer exposes memory access, physical actions, and reusable methods to the agent. From Waiting for Instructions to Attending to the World RoboBeats keeps screening the visual stream during task execution and between user requests. Its output feeds two workflows: condition tracking supports responses to the current situation, and event discovery supplies observations for memory writing. Both use lightweight screening to decide when further processing is needed. For condition tracking, the agent or a standing interaction rule specifies what to watch for, such as a person starting a phone call or the visible completion of a manipulation task. When the condition changes state, the system triggers a response or returns the observation to the agent for reassessment. Monitoring continues while an action tool is running, and the agent combines these observations with tool feedback and task context to decide whether to continue, finish, or revise its next action. Section 3.5 shows this for an elevator door, a handover, a phone call, and a grasp. For event discovery, RoboBeats identifies changes worth describing and passes the selected observations to the writer, which records each event in an event field with its supporting images and timestamps. A later consolidation step links related records about people, objects, and activities. Event discovery decides what enters memory; the writer decides how it is described.
