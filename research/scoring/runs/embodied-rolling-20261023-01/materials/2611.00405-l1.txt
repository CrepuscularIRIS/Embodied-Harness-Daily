# Behavior Graphs: Unsupervised Graph Structures for Interpreting Robot Deployment Data

## Abstract
Data flywheels promise continuous improvement via insights from deployment data. Understanding how the deployed model succeeds and fails can steer subsequent data collection towards patching failures and reinforcing successes. However, robot deployments create large volumes of high-dimensional rollout data, which is challenging to understand and confidently ground data collection decisions in. To make deployment data easier to analyze and act on, we propose behavior graphs, which compress policy rollouts into a single interpretable, directed graph. We construct the graphs by learning to identify similar behavior modes across rollouts, connecting the behaviors temporally, and finally linking them to outcomes. In a user study on three real-world tasks, where participants analyzed rollouts to choose a data collection strategy, 16 of 18 preferred behavior graphs to rollout videos, reaching comparable conclusions with 40.3% lower perceived temporal demand and 37.6% lower effort. Beyond interpretability for deployment data, behavior graphs are capable progress predictors: across 43 datasets, they improve the rank correlation between predicted progress and true temporal order by 22.9% and reduce mean absolute error by 53.5% relative to the strongest baseline. As the number of deployed robots grows, behavior graphs lend structure to high-dimensional policy rollouts and make deployment data easier to interpret and reason about.

## I Introduction
Imitation learning has become a standard approach for training robotic manipulation policies [ 1 , 2 ] . As policies move toward real-world deployment, improving them after initial training relies on the data flywheel : deploy the policy, observe its rollout behavior, collect new demonstrations that address observed shortcomings, and retrain. Closing this loop effectively is difficult. Operators must identify not just that a policy fails, but which execution strategies are responsible, whether those failures stem from missing or low-quality training data, and what new demonstrations would meaningfully change the policy’s behavior distribution. Without a structured account of policy behavior, these decisions reduce to intuition. This raises two problems as robots move from labs to deployments: rollout data quickly outgrows what operators can review, and intuition is hard to communicate, compare, or verify. Closing the data flywheel requires methods that condense deployment data into a shared basis for understanding and reasoning about policy behavior. Most existing works that attempt to learn from policy rollouts focus on direct policy improvement rather than generating interpretable insights. Data curation methods identify which existing demonstrations to discard [ 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] , but not which demonstrations to collect next. While red-teaming approaches [ 7 , 8 ] can generate valuable insights about failure modes of a policy, they rely on actively steering policy inputs to cause failures, which is useful for finding weaknesses in a controlled setting, but may overly represent pessimistic scenarios compared to typical deployment settings. Active data collection approaches such as DAgger [ 9 , 10 ] are effective, but expensive to scale, as they require constant monitoring and human experts to collect recovery data. None of these methods helps operators interpret and act on autonomous rollout data from robot deployments. We address this gap with behavior graphs , a representation that compresses policy rollouts into an interpretable directed graph ( ). Nodes in a behavior graph correspond to semantic behavior modes, which are connected by edges that represent the transition probabilities between different behaviors and rollout outcomes. Construction is unsupervised: we cluster policy embeddings of rollout segments from all rollouts into shared behavior modes, which form the graph’s nodes. Directed edges connect each behavior to its successors and to rollout outcomes, weighted by the empirical transition probabilities observed across rollouts. This structure lets operators trace where, when, and how a policy fails, and steer data collection accordingly. In a user study where participants chose data collection strategies using either behavior graphs or rollout videos, behavior graphs lower perceived temporal demand by 40.3% and effort by 37.6%, without changing the conclusions participants reach. Furthermore, behavior graphs serve as a strong representation for progress estimation: across 43 datasets spanning simulation and the real world, they outperform specialized progress estimators, both learned and built on vision-language models (VLMs). Our contributions are threefold: • Behavior graphs, an unsupervised representation of policy rollouts that clusters policy embeddings of rollout segments into behaviors and links them to each other and to rollout outcomes through empirical transition probabilities. • A user study with three real-world tasks in which 16 of 18 participants prefer the graph over videos of rollouts, and perceived temporal demand and effort drop by 40.3% and 37.6% while data collection decisions remain comparable. • A method for progress estimation using behavior graphs that achieves state-of-the-art results across 43 datasets, improving value-order correlation by 22.9% and reducing mean absolute error by 53.5% relative to the strongest baseline.

## III Methodology
We first introduce methodology for unsupervised behavior graph construction, followed by how graphs can be used for active data collection and progress estimation. III-A Behavior Graph Construction We are given a set of trajectories \mathcal{D}_{\tau}=\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{N}\} , where each trajectory \tau_{n}=\big((s_{1},a_{1}),\ldots,(s_{T_{n}},a_{T_{n}})\big) is a sequence of state-action pairs (s_{t},a_{t})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A} of length T_{n} , and we write \tau_{n}[t] for its t -th element. Trajectories are either policy rollouts, whose actions are sampled from a policy \pi_{\theta}(a\mid s) with parameters \theta , or training demonstrations, whose actions are labeled by an expert. Each trajectory is annotated with a binary outcome o(\tau)\in\{\textsc{Success},\textsc{Failure}\} . Finally, we assume access to an embedding function f\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d} . Our aim is to discover a finite set of behaviors \mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{K}\} from \mathcal{D}_{\tau} , represent each trajectory \tau_{n} as a sequence \sigma_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{L_{n}} of L_{n} behaviors, and compress all sequences into an interpretable directed graph G=(V,E,w) whose nodes V are the behaviors and outcomes, with edges E weighted by w ( Fig. 1 ). Embedding trajectories We embed every timestep of every trajectory as \phi_{n}[t]=f(\tau_{n}[t]) . The choice of f determines the notion of similarity the graph is built on. Unless stated otherwise, we use policy embeddings, where f returns the output of the observation encoder of \pi_{\theta}(a\mid s) . Temporal aggregation Since behaviors span multiple timesteps and per-timestep embeddings are noisy, we smooth each embedding sequence \phi_{n} with a sliding-window mean filter of width W and stride S , and write x_{n}[t]\in\mathbb{R}^{d} for the window starting at timestep t . Dimensionality reduction and clustering To facilitate clustering, we map each window to a lower-dimensional space with a reduction g\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{low}}} , fit on the windows of all trajectories. Unless otherwise stated, we use UMAP as dimensionality reduction. We then run K -means on the reduced windows g(x_{n}[t]) , yielding K clusters with centroids \mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{low}}} . Each cluster defines a behavior c_{k} , giving the set of behaviors \mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{K}\} . Each window x_{n}[t] is assigned the behavior c_{n}[t] based on the cluster assignment in the reduced space, c_{n}[t]=c_{k^{*}},\quad k^{*}=\arg\min_{k}\lVert g(x_{n}[t])-\mu_{k}\rVert_{2}. (1) Segments Since behaviors typically span multiple windows, consecutive windows are often assigned the same behavior. We define a segment as a contiguous sequence of windows that have the same behavior assigned to them. Ordering the L_{n} segments of \tau_{n} in time, the trajectory is reduced to its sequence of behaviors \sigma_{n}=\big(c^{(1)},\ldots,c^{(L_{n})}\big),\qquad c^{(l)}\neq c^{(l+1)}, (2) where c^{(l)} is the behavior of the l -th segment.
