# HomeLoop: A Simulation Framework and Benchmark for Robot-IoT Coordination in Everyday Domestic Assistance and Safety

## Abstract
Recent progress in embodied robotics is bringing robots closer to deployment in homes, as vision-language models (VLMs) are demonstrating significant advances in reasoning, making VLM-driven robots promising home assistant agents. These agents will often be introduced into homes that already have cameras, Internet of Things (IoT) devices, and residents with wearables. To fulfill their potential, they need to integrate with existing smart infrastructure and reason through ambiguous scenarios: a smoke alarm may come from a pan about to ignite, a pan already on fire, a resident cooking or a faulty sensor, and each calls for a different response. To that end, we introduce HomeLoop, a simulation framework and benchmark built on Isaac Sim that combines robot bodies, networked devices and wearables, simulated residents and visitors, faulty sensors, and hazards that evolve over time. Our benchmark is composed of 20 tasks across five families grounded in real-world household hazards and needs, and designed so that the same alert can have different causes. A runtime monitor scores each episode with temporal-logic checks on the final outcome and the actions along the way, and every action carries a predefined cost. We evaluate six open-weight and proprietary VLMs. The best achieves a family-balanced score of 61.9%, and across models we see a pattern of over-reaction: failed episodes involving prohibited interventions outnumber those involving missed required interventions by more than two to one, with models often shutting off the stove before investigating the alarm's cause. HomeLoop is an extensible foundation intended for use in research on robot-IoT coordination, long-term memory, proactive assistance, and privacy-safety trade-offs. HomeLoop will be released with 2,100 home layouts that can be automatically furnished in diverse configurations, five robot bodies and 20 networked device types.

## 1. Introduction
Consider a home assistant robot with access to data from the home’s Internet of Things (IoT) devices including sensors and cameras. A smoke alarm sounds in the kitchen, and the cause may be a pan approaching ignition, a pan already on fire, the resident cooking, or a faulty sensor. The robot could react in several ways: recheck the sensor, inspect the scene through a home camera, physically enter the kitchen to turn off the stove, contact the resident, or call emergency services. These actions have consequences that depend on the underlying situation. Turning off the stove can prevent a pan overheating, and calling emergency services can help when a fire has started. However, turning off the stove during normal cooking interrupts the resident’s meal, while calling emergency services when there is no fire is a costly mistake, illustrating how nuanced reasoning is needed for a home assistant agent to successfully react. We have grounded our task selection in HomeLoop in common hazards that occur in homes. For example, cooking is the leading cause of residential building fires in the United States ( U.S. Fire Administration, 2025 ) . Beyond that, water damage and freezing damage generated 1.5 claims per 100 insured homes annually in 2019–2023 ( Insurance Information Institute, 2026 ) . Seniors falling is another common hazard that assistive robots have to respond to, affecting more than one in four adults aged 65 and older each year ( Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026 ) . The agent could also assist with everyday tasks such as shopping, housework, and meal preparation, which are commonly supported by family caregivers ( AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, 2025 ) . Existing works that combine benchmarks and simulation frameworks capture different aspects of home assistance, but lack a shared environment for IoT interaction and embodied robots across everyday assistance and safety tasks. In HomeBench ( Li et al., 2025 ) and SimuHome ( Seo et al., 2026 ) , language models issue calls to device application programming interfaces (APIs) in a non-rendered simulation of IoT sensors and actuators without robots. In BEHAVIOR-1K ( Li et al., 2023 ) , embodied agents perform household tasks in rendered homes, but without networked IoT devices. SafeAgentBench ( Yin et al., 2024 ) and IS-Bench ( Lu et al., 2026a ) , tested in the AI2-THOR ( Kolve et al., 2017 ) and OmniGibson ( Li et al., 2023 ) simulators respectively, evaluate whether language-model planners refuse hazardous instructions or mitigate hazards, but both lack IoT capabilities. In HAZARD ( Zhou et al., 2024 ) , embodied agents act amid simulated fires and floods without connected devices. Robot–IoT integration has been explored in systems such as RiSH ( Do et al., 2018 ) , CBASH ( Zhu et al., 2023 ) , a system built on SF2D ( Azghadi et al., 2025 ) , and IndoorR2X ( Yang et al., 2026b ) . These systems demonstrate connections between robots and household sensors, but do not provide a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating agents across everyday assistance and safety tasks. Among the 37 works we compared (Table 1 , Appendix E ), none provides such a benchmark and simulation framework with networked sensing and control, house cameras, embodied action, and outcome-based evaluation. To address this gap, we present HomeLoop, a benchmark and simulation framework that brings a robot, networked devices including house cameras, and residents into a shared household environment for everyday assistance and safety tasks (Figure 1 ). Built in Isaac Sim ( NVIDIA, 2025a ) , HomeLoop allows homes, robot bodies, scenarios, tasks, and access to connected devices and cameras to be configured independently. Scenarios define resident activities, device faults, and timed events, while tasks specify required outcomes and time and cost limits. A runtime monitor evaluates task completion and safety conditions on the world state and robot and device actions throughout and at the end of the episode, using finite-trace linear temporal logic. We make three contributions: (1) A simulation framework in which homes, scenarios, tasks, robot bodies and service access are composed from independent configuration files, with 2,100 furnished homes built from real floor plans, five robot bodies and 20 networked device types available (Section 3 ). (2) A benchmark of 20 tasks in five families chosen from household needs and grouped so that tasks starting with the same alert require different responses (Section 4 ). (3) An evaluation of six open-weight and proprietary VLMs; ablations of price visibility, service access and images; an analysis of when they intervene; and a run of one model in ten further homes, all recomputable from the released episode records (Sections 5 and 6 ). ## 3. The Simulation Framework
HomeLoop’s simulation framework, built in Isaac Sim ( NVIDIA, 2025a ) , composes each episode from configuration files and a catalog of parts: furniture, objects, devices, people, hazards and robot bodies (Figure 2 ). The home’s configuration file lists its walls, rooms, doors, windows, furniture, devices and cameras; the scenario file adds people, objects and hazard sources; and the robot embodiment can be chosen from the catalog (Section 3.7 ). Separately, success criteria are defined by the benchmark’s task files as described in detail in Section 4 . 3.1. Floor plans We build the release’s homes from the Swiss Dwellings dataset of real apartment plans ( Standfest et al., 2022 ) , which contains walls, rooms, doors, windows and fixture positions. Of 3,400 plans taken from \sim 900 sites, \sim 600 failed an automated audit (a blocked doorway, no shower or bath, a kitchen without a fridge) or failed to furnish. Each floor plan is abstracted into a vector containing its counts of each room type including outdoor spaces, its windows, its interior doors and its indoor floor area. We group plans by bedroom count (capped at five), outdoor-space type, open kitchen, and whether it supports a washing machine. We then pick plans greedily such that no site or group dominates, each pick must come from a site that supplied under 1% of the plans picked so far, and from a group with the fewest picks. Among those plans, we select a plan at the furthest L1 distance from its nearest picked neighbour, prioritizing plans whose graph of rooms joined by doors is new under a three-round Weisfeiler–Lehman hash ( Shervashidze et al., 2011 ) .
