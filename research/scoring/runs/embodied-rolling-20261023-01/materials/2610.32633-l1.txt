# Natural-Language to Nav2: Operational Policies for Costmap-Based Navigation

## Abstract
Autonomous mobile robots support material transport in warehouses and industrial facilities, where navigation must adapt to changing operating conditions. Sensor observations reveal physical obstacles, but do not by themselves specify temporary access rules, route preferences, or exceptions known to operators. This paper presents a natural-language policy interface that brings this knowledge into ROS 2 Nav2. A local large language model converts instructions into JSON policies specifying destinations, spatial constraints, and temporal or event conditions. Deterministic checks govern mission acceptance, and three costmap adapters project accepted policies into the existing navigation stack without changing its planner or controller. On 150 researcher-authored English commands, four local models produce 107-142 correct accepted policies, compared with 44 for a rule-based parser, with 3-20 incorrect acceptances per model. Fault injection records rejection of eight endpoint/JSON faults and 29 validator-stage faults. Navigation experiments use correct task policies to evaluate keepouts, zone costs, person-dependent regions, and event-conditioned passage in simulation and on a TurtleBot3. For Tasks 1-3, mean travel times are 18.4-24.5% lower in simulation and 23.1-35.0% lower on the physical testbed than with the baseline lacking those policies. A native KeepoutFilter comparison and two-robot demonstrations examine basic keepout behavior and shared policy projection. The interface connects operator instructions to explicit navigation policies while retaining Nav2's planning and control components.

## 1 Introduction
Autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) support material transport in warehouses and industrial facilities [ 1 , 2 ] . Their navigation must account for changing workspace conditions as well as the destination of each delivery [ 3 ] . Some of these conditions are operating rules: an aisle may be reserved for loading, a corridor may be closed for a shift, or an emergency may permit an otherwise restricted passage. Sensors can detect an obstruction, but its expected duration and permitted exceptions require additional information. Operators need a way to make this knowledge available to the robot when planning a mission. ROS 2 Nav2 provides planners and controllers for mobile navigation [ 4 ] . Layered costmaps combine spatial information [ 5 ] , and native keepout filters can receive updated masks during operation [ 6 ] . These mechanisms provide a means to apply constraints once they have been expressed in a form the navigation stack can use. An instruction such as “avoid Zone A for the next thirty minutes” also requires the system to identify the zone, retain the restriction, and determine when it expires. The problem addressed here is to connect such instructions to Nav2 with explicit rules for policy acceptance, mission replacement, and restriction lifetime. We introduce a natural-language policy interface that represents destinations together with constraints on reaching them. A locally hosted large language model (LLM) translates each instruction into a structured policy. The bridge checks the complete candidate against the schema and execution context before accepting it. Accepted state supplies waypoint goals and inputs to three costmap adapters for keepouts, zone costs, and object-avoidance regions (Figure 1 ). The same policy-derived updates can be sent to independent robot instances. The LLM operates when a policy is submitted, outside the navigation control loop. NavFn computes the global path and DWB controls local motion. The intermediate policy makes the requested destinations, constraints, and conditions available for inspection. Structural acceptance and semantic correctness remain distinct: a candidate may satisfy the interface contract while naming a waypoint the operator did not request. The evaluation measures both. The work makes three contributions. (1) A policy schema represents destinations together with spatial constraints and temporal or event conditions, providing a common interface between language interpretation and navigation. (2) Transactional acceptance checks the complete candidate against the schema and execution context before replacing the mission snapshot; rejection preserves the preceding mission, while retained restrictions are managed separately. (3) Nav2 projection translates accepted state into waypoint goals and keepout, soft-cost, and object-avoidance inputs for the existing navigation stack, including shared policy updates to independent robot instances. The evaluation follows this architecture. A 150-command benchmark measures conversion accuracy and accepted errors, and fault injection examines rejection and state preservation. Simulation and physical trials examine route selection under task policies, including a separate comparison of event-conditioned passage permissions.

## 3 From an Instruction to a Navigation Policy
The system treats an operating instruction as a proposed change to a mission. Figure 1 follows that change from language input to robot motion. Interpretation produces a candidate, acceptance establishes the new mission state, and projection converts the applicable rules into Nav2 inputs. All LLM inference runs on an on-premises workstation connected to the robots through the local network. 3.1 A schema for destinations and operating rules An operator publishes an instruction on /nl_command . A frozen local LLM receives the sentence, the registered zone and waypoint context, an output schema, and example conversions. No model weights are fine-tuned. Waypoint labels resolve to stored poses and zone labels resolve to map-frame geometry. This avoids asking the model to invent region coordinates. Table 2 groups the policy fields by execution role. For example, “Go to waypoint 6 via Zone B, but do not use Zone A” requests a destination, a required transit zone, and a keepout. “Set the cost of Zone A to 200 and Zone B to 160, then go to waypoint 4” adds relative traversal costs. Temporal exclusions and conditional rules carry the lifetime or event scope that would otherwise be lost when reducing a command to a destination alone. Table 2 : The JSON policy contract. A candidate may also explicitly reject an instruction using decision and reason_code . Fields Interpretation and execution plan , via_zones Ordered destinations and required transit zones. The bridge resolves transit waypoints and builds the goal sequence. exclusions Zones to keep closed, with optional duration or absolute time interval. weights Integer zone costs for traversal preferences. dynamic_object_rules Object class and avoidance radius used around detections. conditional_rules Zone actions selected by event/state conditions and priority. 3.2 Acceptance depends on the candidate and the current context An endpoint failure or malformed JSON is rejected before a policy object reaches the validator. A parsed candidate is checked for required structure, data types, registered identifiers, admissible values, and conflicts among fields. A zone cannot simultaneously be a required transit region and an exclusion. Conflicting costs for the same zone also invalidate the candidate. The entire update is rejected if a check fails. Valid fields are not applied selectively, and invalid numbers are not clipped. The schema admits person and forklift object rules with finite radii from 0.1 to 10 m. Durations must be positive and no greater than 1,440 minutes; absolute intervals require ordered endpoints. Conditional rules use default , fire_alarm , or low_battery and the actions forbid , allow , or allow_shortest . Default rules only forbid. Equal-priority conflicts, incompatible actions, and event priorities that fail to exceed a default prohibition are rejected. Keepout and soft-cost assignments to the same zone are checked for conflict. A structurally admissible candidate can still conflict with a restriction already in force. The execution-context check tests required transit zones against effective keepouts, including permanent restrictions, retained timed restrictions, and applicable event permissions. It rejects an incompatible candidate without deleting the transit requirement. These checks establish structural and contextual consistency; they cannot detect every requirement omitted from the instruction. Let m denote the mission snapshot, r the separately maintained restrictions, and e the event state. Validation and context checking yield a normalized candidate u^{*}=V(u;r,e) or rejection \bot .
