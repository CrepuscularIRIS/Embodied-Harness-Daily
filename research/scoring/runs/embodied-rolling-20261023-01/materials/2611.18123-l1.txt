# Evaluating Language Models as Spatial Priors for Object-Goal Navigation

## Abstract
Semantic exploration methods for object-goal navigation use large language models (LLMs) to score how strongly a target object is associated with a room type or with a nearby object. This paper considers obtaining from the LLM a spatial prior instead: a distribution over the position of the target in a room. We evaluate whether two open-weight LLMs, Qwen3.5-27B and Gemma-4-31B-it, can provide such a prior. Using real room layouts from CubiCasa5K and ScanNet, we measure the likelihood the models assign to real and perturbed layouts, and the layouts they generate. The object frequencies and room sizes of the generated rooms follow the room type, but the proximity of the objects to the walls does not follow the differences between room types found in real rooms. The likelihood distinguishes real layouts from perturbed ones partly through their geometry and partly through the agreement between the coordinates and a field that states the wall each object touches; for the movable furniture of ScanNet, it is higher for layouts with all objects piled near the room center than for the real layouts in most rooms. When an object is given in the prompt, the models place a related object relative to it. When the wall of each object is given, they place most objects against that wall, but do not keep all objects inside a given room rectangle. As a prior for object search, the generated placements reduce the searched area relative to a uniform prior, but by less than a third of the reduction obtained with a prior built from real layouts, and by less than two thirds when both priors are spatially smoothed. These results support the use of LLMs for object-room associations and for writing coordinates under constraints determined by another component, followed by a geometric check, but not as a source of placement priors where real layouts are available.

## 1 Introduction
Object-goal navigation is a fundamental task for mobile service robots. Semantic exploration is a strategy for object-goal navigation and sometimes utilizes large language models (LLMs) for selecting next viewpoints. Systems such as ESC, L3MVN and their successors query the model for a scalar affinity , which indicates how strongly a target category is associated with a room type or a nearby object, and use it to rank frontiers ( Zhou et al., 2023 ; Yu et al., 2023 ; Ji et al., 2024 ; Jia and Yu, 2026 ) . The model supplies a number per candidate frontier, and the geometry is obtained from a separate mapping process. In this paper, we investigate whether LLMs can provide a placement rather than an affinity. If the layouts generated by a model gave the usual positions of objects in a room type, a robot could enter an unmapped room with a spatial prior over the position of the target instead of a ranking of frontiers. Language-driven scene generation systems use LLMs to generate metric layouts that look plausible ( Feng et al., 2023 ; Yang et al., 2024 ; Çelen et al., 2024 ) . Their evaluations, however, measure the plausibility of the generated scenes or their agreement with the prompt; they do not compare the distribution of generated layouts with that of real rooms, which is the property that a spatial prior requires. We evaluate the placement information that two open-weight LLMs, Qwen3.5-27B and Gemma-4-31B-it (Qwen and Gemma below), provide along two axes: agreement with reality and usefulness for navigation . Fig. 1 shows the overview of the evaluation. We use two outputs of the models, both on real rooms from two datasets, ScanNet and CubiCasa5K. The first output is the likelihood that a model assigns to a layout written as text (Fig. 1 (a)). We compare the likelihood of each real layout with that of perturbed copies, in which objects are moved, exchanged or piled up, and measure the fraction of rooms in which the real layout scores higher. The second output is the set of layouts that a model generates for a given room type (Fig. 1 (b)). We compare their object frequencies, room sizes and wall proximity with those of real rooms of the same type, and examine whether a generated object moves with an object stated in the prompt. These two comparisons measure the agreement with reality. For the usefulness for navigation, we build from the generated layouts a prior over the position of a target object (Fig. 1 (c)), and compute the fraction of the area of a real room that a search guided by the prior examines before it finds the target, compared with a uniform prior and with a prior built from real layouts. The contributions of this paper are as follows: 1. We propose a method for evaluating LLMs as spatial priors. The method uses two outputs of the models, the likelihood of real and perturbed layouts and the generated layouts, on real rooms from two datasets, and controls for the properties of the serialized text that affect the likelihood (Secs. 3 , 4 and 8.3 ). 2. We show that object frequencies and room sizes in the generated rooms follow the room type but object placement does not. We also show that the generated coordinates reflect regularities in how coordinates are written rather than the geometric constraints that determine them (Secs. 5 and 6 ). 3. We show that, when an observed object is given in the prompt, the models place a related object relative to it and move unrelated objects away from it (Sec. 5.3 ). 4. We evaluate the generated placements as a prior for object search and show that they reduce the search cost relative to a uniform prior by 0.036–0.077 of the room area for Qwen and 0.015–0.069 for Gemma, which is 14–32% and 10–23%, respectively, of the reduction obtained with a prior built from real layouts (29–48% and 30–58% when each prior is spatially smoothed). Based on all the results, we derive design guidelines for using LLMs in object-goal navigation (Secs. 7 and 8 ). The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sec. 2 surveys related work. Sec. 3 describes the datasets, the serialization, the placement statistic and the generation conditions. Sec. 4 measures whether the likelihood of a real layout is higher than that of a perturbed copy, separates the effects of the geometry and of the text, and measures whether the likelihood changes when the neighbor of an object is changed. Sec. 5 measures which properties of generated layouts depend on the room type, and whether placement follows an object stated in the prompt. Sec. 6 analyzes the generated coordinates. Sec. 7 evaluates the generated placements as a search prior. Sec. 8 states the design guidelines, Sec. 9 the limitations, and Sec. 10 concludes. Appendix A defines every quantity, Appendix B reproduces the prompts, and Appendix C the output schemas. The tables of Appendix A give every number the main text summarizes, each placed after the definition it depends on.

## 3 Setup for Analysis
3.1 Two datasets with different room and object types 3.1.1 Datasets We use the following two datasets of real rooms: CubiCasa5K ( Kalervo et al., 2019 ) is a corpus of annotated architectural floor plans. Its objects are fixtures : sinks, bathtubs, counters, stoves. Fixtures are attached to walls by construction, and their positions are drawn rather than measured. We use the kitchens and bathrooms, and keep the rooms whose polygon fills at least 0.95 of its oriented bounding box, so that a rectangle describes them: 2,848 kitchens and 4,258 bathrooms, with a closed vocabulary of 14 fixture categories, such as sink, counter, stove, dishwasher, toilet and bathtub. ScanNet v2 ( Dai et al., 2017 ) is a corpus of RGB-D scans of real rooms. Its objects are the movable furniture a robot has to navigate around: chairs, tables, sofas, boxes. We use four room types: bathrooms, bedrooms, offices and living rooms (210, 273, 173 and 224 rooms). We align each scan with its axis-alignment matrix, take the extent of the floor and wall geometry as the room rectangle, and keep the instances whose lower bound lies below 0.30 m above the floor, so that a sensor at floor level would detect them. 3.1.2 Canonical coordinates and serialization Each room is expressed in meters from its south-west corner, with x running east and y north. To remove the arbitrary choice of orientation, we rotate the room so that a door lies on the south wall and mirror it, if necessary, so that the door is left of center (see Fig. 2 ). Layouts are serialized as JSON with one entry per object, giving its category, center, and footprint size. All numbers are written with exactly two decimal places, so that the string length does not vary with the value. The room type is not part of the scored string: it appears only in the prompt.
