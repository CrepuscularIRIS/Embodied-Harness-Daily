# VLAN: Vision-Language Accessible Navigation

## Abstract
Accessible mobility is user-conditioned rather than scene-intrinsic, as walkable paths for one embodiment are inaccessible to another. Yet, existing benchmarks evaluate traversability under a fixed and limited embodiment. We present Vision-Language Accessible Navigation (VLAN), containing 1,000 scenes, 20K navigation trajectories, and 160K keyframes across diverse lighting, environmental conditions, scene semantics, and path geometries. For each trajectory, VLAN provides embodiment-specific actions and accessibility features for legged and wheeled agents, including humanoid and quadruped robots and people with visual or mobility impairments, while everyday obstacles and accessibility labels are categorized as Passable, Bypass, HighRisk, and Blocked. Unlike goal-only evaluation, our benchmark measures physical safety and alignment with user-specific constraints via goal-reaching, collision-aware, and accessibility-aligned metrics. We benchmark modern vision-language models and find they often fail to consistently adapt obstacle relevance, path selection, and safety criteria across user profiles, suggesting accessibility-aware navigation requires dedicated datasets, tasks, and metrics beyond conventional embodied navigation evaluation. All data and code open-source at https://github.com/InSAI-Lab/VLAN.

## 1 Introduction
Embodied navigation Song et al. (2025) ; Ziliotto et al. (2025) ; Zheng et al. (2024) ; Gu et al. (2022) in real-world environments is governed not only by scene geometry and goal location, but also by the physical, perceptual, and operational constraints of the target user or agent. The same obstacle can induce substantially different navigation decisions across embodiments. As illustrated in Fig. 1 , a low temporary barrier can be traversable for a humanoid robot, risky for a BVI user, and impassable for a user with mobility impairments . Conversely, overhanging tree branches may have little effect on quadruped robots, yet become critical hazards for drones . These cases highlight a limitation of user-agnostic navigation evaluation, since reaching a target is insufficient if the resulting trajectory is unsafe, inaccessible, or misaligned with the intended user. Existing navigation benchmarks Ku et al. (2020) ; Krantz et al. (2020) have driven substantial progress in vision-language navigation, embodied navigation, and action prediction. However, they typically assume a fixed embodiment and a user-independent traversability criterion, where a trajectory is considered successful if it reaches the target and avoids collisions for a generic agent. This formulation is insufficient for accessibility-aware navigation Jiang et al. (2025) , where obstacle relevance, safety margins, action granularity, and feasible strategies depend on the target embodiment. Although recent capability-conditioned and embodiment-specific studies Su et al. (2026) ; Windecker et al. (2025) ; Cheng et al. (2024) ; Wang et al. (2024b) have started to examine these differences, existing benchmarks still lack a unified setting for evaluating multiple human and robotic users in the same scenes with user-specific actions and accessibility annotations. Building such a benchmark requires both realistic observations and interactive, editable environments. Real-world videos and street-level imagery capture natural scene appearance, but they are passive and do not readily support scene editing, counterfactual environment modification, action rollout, or collision checking. Simulation provides interactivity and control, but scaling visually diverse and realistic scenes remains costly. Recent work Xie et al. (2025) ; Han et al. (2025) converts monocular videos into photorealistic and physically interactable 3D simulation environments for embodied navigation, enabling reinforcement learning of visual navigation agents in digital twins. Inspired by these works, we find that editable reconstructed scenes can preserve realistic visual appearance while supporting interactive scene modification, trajectory generation, keyframe rendering, and metric-grounded evaluation. Built on these, we present Vision-Language Accessible Navigation (VLAN) , a large-scale dataset for evaluating multi-user accessibility-aware navigation. VLAN contains 1,000 scenes , 20K navigation trajectories , and 160K keyframes , spanning diverse lighting, environmental conditions, scene semantics, and path geometries. We provide user-conditioned navigation actions and accessibility annotations for multiple user embodiments, i.e . , humanoid robots , quadruped robots , drones , blind or visually impaired (BVI) users , and wheelchair users . This design enables us to generate user-conditioned navigation instances from each scene without relying solely on additional real-world data collection. The drone setting is deliberately formulated as fixed-altitude 2D accessibility-conditioned navigation : the action space is (\Delta x\mathchar 59\relax\Delta z\mathchar 59\relax\Delta\theta) and overhead obstacles are handled by a horizontal detour or a stop, so that the drone and the quadruped robot share one action interface and remain directly comparable on the same scene. Given the same visual path with start-goal pair, VLAN evaluates whether a model can reason and forecast future navigation actions from visual observations and action history for different user embodiments. We propose user-dependent accessibility-aligned metrics, enabling systematic analysis of whether modern vision-language models can adapt obstacle relevance, path selection, safety margins, and action behavior across different users. We instantiate the benchmark with general-purpose vision-language models rather than task-specific navigation agents: existing VLN agents assume a single fixed embodiment and consume an interactive egocentric observation stream, whereas VLAN provides a fixed set of keyframes and requires one model to switch its output modality and safety criteria across five user types. We therefore treat generic VLMs as the appropriate first baseline and expect embodiment-specialised navigation agents to be a natural next step. ## 3 VLAN Dataset and Benchmark
VLAN is a large-scale benchmark for multi-user accessibility-aware navigation. It contains 1,000 scenes, 20K navigation trajectories, and 160K keyframes. Each scene contains about 20 trajectories, and each trajectory is represented by eight keyframes. For each trajectory, VLAN provides visual observations, camera poses, obstacle metadata, accessibility labels, and user-conditioned navigation targets. The benchmark is designed to evaluate whether models can adapt navigation behavior to different user embodiments under the same visual scene, start-goal pair, and obstacle layout. 3.1 Dataset Construction and Diversity Scene reconstruction and editing. VLAN is constructed from egocentric scene videos and associated navigation routes. Fig. 2 summarizes the overall pipeline from video collection to Gaussian reconstruction Li et al. (2025b) ; Wang et al. (2024a) ; Xie et al. (2025) , Unity-based scene editing, trajectory generation, and sample construction. We first reconstruct each scene into a Gaussian-splat representation and import the reconstructed scene into Unity Juliani et al. (2018) for controlled editing. Instead of treating captured environments as fixed visual observations, VLAN uses reconstructed scenes as interactive and editable environments. This design preserves realistic visual appearance while enabling systematic variation of scene states, route conditions, and obstacle configurations. As shown in Fig. 3 , the same reconstructed scene can be assigned different traffic-light states and modified with different obstacle configurations. This editability is important for data scaling because one base environment can produce multiple navigation instances with different accessibility implications. VLAN therefore scales along two complementary axes. Increasing the number of reconstructed scenes improves scene-level diversity across appearance, layout, lighting, and environmental conditions. Increasing the number of edited routes within each scene improves navigation-level diversity under shared visual geometry. Scene and obstacle diversity. VLAN covers diverse navigation conditions across lighting, scene type, path geometry, weather, and surface condition. The scene set includes daytime, nighttime, dusk, and low-vision settings, as well as urban, suburban, rural, and indoor environments.
