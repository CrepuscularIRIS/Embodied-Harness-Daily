# ActOR: Ambient AI-Based Agentic Orchestration for Humanoid Autonomy in Operating Rooms

## Abstract
Operating rooms (ORs) increasingly contain ambient sensing infrastructure to enable AI-based optimization of workflows and utilization. We pursue an alternative path: using ambient AI to guide robot action and optimize utilization. Deploying humanoids in the OR without ambient AI is severely limited by cumbersome de novo orientation and planning due to partial onboard observations. We posit that this can be achieved by coupling an ambient AI-based agentic orchestrator with the humanoid robotic actor, providing shared perception and execution feedback for humanoid autonomy in the OR. ActOR fuses a wall-mounted ambient camera and the robot's head-mounted camera into a shared object-level digital twin (DT). A large language model agent plans over the DT using pre-programmed locomotion and manipulation skills without task-specific training. During execution, DT updates and execution outcomes inform retries, alternative approaches, or replanning. We evaluated ActOR in simulated and physical environments. Across three tasks in two simulated ORs reconstructed from real rooms, ambient perception reduced exploration from 1.05 to 0.12 Explore calls per trial when targets were initially outside the robot's view. Closed-loop feedback increased success from 46.7% to 83.3% under nominal execution and from 8.3% to 51.7% under injected navigation, object-relocation, and grasp perturbations. In a physical mock OR with a humanoid, ActOR completed 14 of 20 single-object disposal trials and demonstrated replanning after scene changes. Ambient AI-based observations provide scene state that improves robot action planning, increasing success and reducing exploration compared with robot-only observations. Feeding skill outcomes and DT updates back to the planner improves multi-step task success. Our results show the feasibility of humanoid task autonomy for multi-step disposal tasks in a mock OR.

## 1 Introduction
Operating rooms (ORs) face a growing shortage of perioperative nursing and support staff Xie et al. (2024) . Room turnover, supply retrieval, and disposal of used materials consume substantial staff time and directly affect OR efficiency Portney and Demirci (2024) . These tasks are repetitive and physically demanding, and do not require highly trained personnel. Automating these tasks with robots rather than relying on an already strained workforce is a promising avenue to improve turnover times. The postoperative phase provides a practical setting for automation: the patient has left, most staff have stepped away, and the environment is comparatively controlled Kernbach et al. (2026) ; Wachs (2026) . Humanoid robots are attractive for such tasks because they can, in principle, operate within spaces designed for humans without major modifications. At the same time, modern ORs are becoming increasingly instrumented. Ceiling- and wall-mounted cameras have been used to recognize surgical phases, localize staff and equipment, and construct digital twins (DTs) of the OR Zhang et al. (2026a) ; Perez et al. (2025) ; Özsoy et al. (2026) ; Wang et al. (2026) . Yet this ambient sensing infrastructure has primarily been used to inform clinical teams rather than directly support robotic autonomy. We ask whether the instrumented OR itself can instead become part of the robot’s perceptual infrastructure, providing persistent room-scale context that complements the robot’s local, egocentric view. This question is particularly relevant for humanoid autonomy in the OR, where two limitations make robot-centric operation difficult. First, autonomous task planning requires an accurate representation of the current environment, but an onboard camera provides only a moving and frequently occluded view of a cluttered room. Upon entering the room to complete an assigned task, a robot may therefore need to explore the environment before it can decide where and how to act. Second, humanoid execution remains imperfect: navigation can terminate short of a target, and grasps can fail. A plan generated from the initial scene state therefore becomes unreliable when execution deviates from expectation. Foundation-model agents can decompose open-ended instructions into robot skills Huh et al. (2026) , but reliable physical autonomy also requires broader access to the world state and feedback about executed actions. We address these limitations with ActOR , an ambient AI-based agentic orchestration framework for humanoid autonomy in the OR (Fig. 1 ). ActOR fuses observations from a wall-mounted ambient camera and the robot’s head-mounted camera into a shared object-level DT, combining room-scale awareness with near-field observations for manipulation. A large language model (LLM) agent plans over the DT using a library of pre-programmed skills, without task-specific training. During execution, the DT is updated and the outcome is fed back to the agent, which retries, changes its approach, or replans. We evaluate ActOR on postoperative support tasks in two simulated ORs reconstructed from real rooms and on a Unitree G1 humanoid in a physical mock OR. Our contributions are: • A shared scene representation integrating room-scale and egocentric sensing for robot orientation and task planning in ORs. • A closed-loop execution mechanism that feeds skill outcomes and scene updates back to an LLM planner for recovery and replanning. • Simulation ablations of ambient perception and execution feedback, together with physical validation in a mock OR.

## 3 Method
3.1 Problem Formulation We consider a humanoid robot performing postoperative support tasks in an OR. Given a natural-language instruction I (e.g., “put all used supplies into the biohazard bin”) and a skill library \mathcal{K} , the robot must complete the task autonomously. The system maintains an object-level DT \mathcal{S}_{t}=\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{t}} , where each node o_{i}=(l_{i},\ \mathbf{p}_{i},\ \mathbf{B}_{i},\ h_{i}), (1) consists of a semantic label l_{i} , a 3D position \mathbf{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3} in a shared world frame, a 3D extent \mathbf{B}_{i} , and an interaction state h_{i}\in\{\texttt{free},\texttt{in-hand}\} . As shown in Fig. 1 c, ActOR couples this DT (Sec. 3.2 ) with an LLM agent that plans and verifies over it (Sec. 3.3 ) and a humanoid that executes the resulting skills (Sec. 3.4 ). 3.2 Shared Scene Representation The ambient camera provides persistent room-scale coverage independent of robot pose, while the robot camera provides accurate near-field geometry for manipulation. Given an RGB image from either camera, a vision-language model (VLM) OpenAI () detects task-relevant objects with labels l_{i} ; each 2D box prompts SAM2 Ravi et al. (2025) for an instance mask, whose pixels are back-projected with stereo depth and transformed into the world frame to obtain \mathbf{p}_{i} and \mathbf{B}_{i} . The DT is kept consistent with the scene as the robot acts. It is updated after each manipulation skill and on each Observe call, and only nodes inside the observing camera’s view are modified. A detection is merged into the existing node it overlaps most when the two occupy the same volume; otherwise, a new node is created. A node is removed when consecutive depth observations find its volume unoccupied. Manipulation also changes object state: when the gripper stops before full closure, the grasped node is marked in-hand and follows the gripper until Place . Spatial relations such as in , near and on are derived from the object positions and extents and passed to the planner with the DT. 3.3 Closed-Loop Agentic Orchestration A large language model (GPT-6.1 Sol OpenAI () ) serves as the planner. Its system prompt specifies its role, OR domain knowledge, the skill library (Table 1 ), and rules for handling failures. At each decision step, it receives the instruction I , the DT serialized as JSON, and the execution history. It then calls a skill, emits a multi-step plan, or declares the task complete or failed. Skills in a multi-step plan execute sequentially. On skill failure or plan completion, the planner receives the updated DT and execution history to determine the next action (Algorithm 1 ). Recovery is left to the planner, which is instructed to retry a failed skill and otherwise change its approach or skip the object. An episode ends when the planner declares the task complete or failed, or when the elapsed time reaches T_{\mathrm{timeout}} .
