# What Can a VLA Learn from the Agent That Calls It? Task-Level Experience Distillation for Embodied Navigation

## Abstract
Navigation agents use a large language model (LLM) planner to call a vision-language-action (VLA) model. Each episode needs the LLM, adding cost and delay. We distill successful agent experience into the VLA instead of distilling the planner. The VLA then navigates alone at test time. Agent sessions are not VLA demonstrations. They include motions driven by planner tools and sub-instructions, along with detours. We present H2E, a task-level experience distillation method that converts these sessions into VLA training data. Every motion becomes a VLA action under the original task instruction, with the resulting poses as targets. H2E masks the loss on steps that move away from the goal. It labels every visited state within 1 m of the goal as a stop. We then update the VLA with low-rank adaptation. The VLA learns to stop at the goal and find goals the base model never reaches. On all 3,189 REVERIE-CE val-unseen episodes, H2E raises the success rate of an 8B VLA from 35.0% to 44.9%.

## 1 Introduction
Goal-directed navigation asks a robot to find a remote object that an instruction describes, such as go to the second-floor hallway and pick up the picture above the table [ 27 ] . The robot sees only its own camera views [ 15 , 36 ] . Strong recent systems split this task in two [ 51 , 18 ] . A frontier LLM planner looks at the scene and keeps a map of visited places. It hands short goals to a vision-language-action (VLA) model and corrects the VLA when it goes wrong. The planner calls the LLM throughout every episode. On our test episodes this costs a median of 70k tokens per episode, and an episode takes more than twice as long as with the VLA alone. The sessions of such an agent show the VLA succeeding with help. We ask whether the VLA can learn from these sessions and then work alone. Current agents keep the VLA frozen [ 51 ] , and distillation from such agents has so far shrunk the planner [ 9 , 41 , 21 ] . We instead train the VLA itself and remove the LLM at test time. A session is hard to use as training data for the VLA. The planner often moves the robot with its own tools, and it often gives the VLA new sub-instructions. Together these produce 45% of the motion in our sessions. The sessions also contain detours that the planner later fixes, so nearly a quarter of the steps move away from the goal. We present H2E , a task-level experience distillation method. Figure 2 gives an overview. H2E uses three simple rules. First, it labels every motion in a session as a VLA action under the original task instruction, whoever made the motion. Second, it masks the loss on steps that move away from the goal. Third, it labels every visited state near the goal as a stop. A LoRA update then trains the VLA, which runs without the LLM at test time. H2E works well on the full REVERIE-CE val-unseen split. It raises the success rate of an 8B VLA from 35.0% to 44.9%, while geodesic adaptation gives no gain. The distilled VLA learns two skills from the agent. It stops in the right place far more often, and it finds goals that the base VLA never reaches. Against the full agent, the distilled VLA reaches a higher success rate with no LLM tokens and about a quarter of the time per episode. Our contributions are as follows. • We distill a frontier LLM navigation agent into the VLA that it calls, so that the VLA navigates without the LLM at test time. • We present H2E . It turns whole agent sessions into VLA training data with task-level labels, progress masking and one stop rule. We also show why an L_{1} loss with this stop rule teaches the VLA to stop. • We show that H2E clearly beats the base VLA and geodesic adaptation on unseen scenes. We also show that the distilled VLA outperforms the agent at a small fraction of its cost.

## 3 Method
Figure 2 and Alg. 1 summarize H2E . We first define the task, the VLA and the agent. We then describe the agent’s sessions, turn them into VLA training data, and train the VLA. 3.1 Problem setup An episode in REVERIE-CE gives a natural-language instruction \ell that refers to a remote object, a start pose and a goal g near that object. At step t the robot stands at pose p_{t} , receives four RGB views o_{t} and takes an action. Let d(p) be the geodesic distance from p to g . An episode succeeds if the robot stops with d(p_{T})\leq 3 m. Each episode allows at most 500 steps. VLA. The VLA \pi is the 8B NavGPT video VLA [ 51 ] . It reads its last 16 observations and the instruction, h_{t}=(o_{t-15:t},\ell) , and predicts K=8 waypoints \hat{y}_{t}=\pi(h_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times 3} . Each waypoint holds a planar offset and a heading in the robot frame. The robot executes the first waypoint and then queries the VLA again. The VLA has no stop head. It stops when two outputs in a row are all zero. Moving and stopping thus come from one regression output, so the labels near the goal teach both. Agent. The agent \mathcal{H} pairs a frontier LLM planner with the VLA as a tool [ 51 ] . The planner can send an instruction or a sub-instruction to the VLA. It can also move the robot itself or walk back to a place in its place graph. It can look at a panorama or the map, and it can end the episode. The planner thus decides with h_{t} and with extra information z_{t} that the VLA never sees, namely the place graph and the whole episode history. 3.2 Agent sessions on training scenes We run \mathcal{H} on training episodes from their original start pose. Each session is a record \tau=\{(p_{t},\,o_{t},\,\ell_{t},\,c_{t})\}_{t=0}^{T}. (1) The label c_{t}\in\{\mathrm{exec},\mathrm{tool}\} says whether the VLA or a planner tool moved the robot from p_{t} to p_{t+1} , and \ell_{t} is the instruction the VLA received at that step. We split planner tool motions into environment steps and render the views at the visited poses. We keep only the sessions that reach the goal. This gives 2395 sessions from 57 training scenes. Table 1 shows who moved the robot in these sessions. The VLA acting on the original instruction produces only 54% of the labelled steps. Planner sub-instructions and planner tools produce most of the rest, and they also produce nearly half of the stop labels. Step t enters the loss, m_{t}=1 , if one of p_{t},\dots,p_{t+K} lies near the goal by Eq. 4 or if d(p_{t+K})\leq d(p_{t})+\delta, (3) with \delta=0.25 m. Turns in place keep d and stay in the loss. One rule labels stops for every data source, including the geodesic expert, y_{t}=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{if }d(p_{t})\leq r_{\mathrm{arr}}, (4) with r_{\mathrm{arr}}=1 m. A radius tighter than the 3 m success region places the stop targets well inside it. The rule also adds stops at states that the VLA walked through without stopping. This happens when the first VLA call passes within 1 m of the goal and then leaves the goal region.
