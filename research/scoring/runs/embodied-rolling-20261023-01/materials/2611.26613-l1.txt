===ABSTRACT===
Frontier multimodal language agents such as GPT-6 Astra have demonstrated strong capabilities in controlling robots directly via low-level numerical actions without high-level primitives. However, existing benchmarks report only episode-level success rates, which show neither how these agents succeed nor where they fail. In this paper, we present a systematic action-level analysis of frontier agents on direct robot control across 12 manipulation benchmarks. We introduce AgentRoboCritic, an intent-grounded critique framework in which the policy agent states a local intent alongside each action, and a critic evaluates the action's outcome and failure mode against this intent. Our action-level analysis demonstrates the promising capabilities of frontier agents on direct robot control, including closed-loop recovery from local execution failures, reliable semantic grounding across diverse scenes, and strong zero-shot manipulation without task-specific adaptation. It also identifies their current limitations, including task-relevant physical execution bottlenecks such as grasping and alignment, and uneven recovery across failure modes. Finally, we validate our critic on a 400-action human-annotated benchmark and release a dataset of 12,844 annotated actions alongside a fine-tuned open-source critic, Muse-Glimmer-30B, whose failure-mode accuracy rises from 32.58% to 74.24%, enabling low-cost action-level analysis.

## 1 Introduction

===INTRO===

Recent advances in robot foundation models follow two main paradigms: Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs), which adapt Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to output low-level robot actions, and World-Action Models (WAMs), which build upon video-generation world models. Despite building upon existing foundation models, both families still suffer from brittle out-of-distribution generalization [ 15 , 56 , 44 ] and limited long-horizon memory [ 12 , 39 ] . Moreover, deploying VLAs and WAMs to new downstream tasks typically requires task-specific fine-tuning [ 21 , 6 , 35 , 50 ] , limiting their zero-shot adaptation. Beyond these two paradigms, frontier multimodal language agents such as GPT-6 Astra [ 34 ] offer a promising alternative: they control robots directly via low-level numerical actions without relying on high-level primitives, show strong zero-shot generalization and working memory in robotic manipulation [ 55 , 20 , 22 , 18 ] , and outperform specialized VLAs and WAMs [ 55 , 20 , 47 ] . These results demonstrate the vast potential of leveraging frontier language model agents directly as generalist robot policies. Existing evaluations assess these agents at the episode level , with binary success rates or aggregate task scores that show neither how an agent succeeds nor where it fails. In this paper, we provide a systematic, fine-grained investigation into the capabilities and limitations of frontier agents on direct robot control by analyzing their behavior at the action level . Evaluating an individual action can be ambiguous because robot manipulation is inherently multimodal [ 16 , 38 , 10 ] : since multiple distinct trajectories can validly advance the same task, whether an action succeeds depends on its intended sub-goal. We resolve this ambiguity by letting the policy agent state a local intent alongside each action, establishing a verifiable local contract for every action (Figure 1 (a)). Conditioned on the global task goal, this local intent, and multi-view observations, our AgentRoboCritic framework determines whether each executed action succeeded and assigns a specific failure mode to every failed action. Using GPT-6 Astra as the critic, we annotate episodes across 12 robot manipulation benchmarks for further analysis. Our analysis characterizes both the promising capabilities and the current limitations of frontier agents on direct robot control. On the capability side, frontier agents show three major strengths (Figure 1 (b)). First, they recover from local execution failures in closed loop, unlike imitation-trained policies whose errors compound under covariate shift [ 37 ] , and half of their successful episodes contain at least one failed action that the agent later corrects. Second, they show reliable semantic grounding across diverse scenes, executing actions that follow the target object, category, and sequential order specified in the local intent. Third, they achieve strong zero-shot manipulation without task-specific adaptation. On the limitation side, frontier agents face task-relevant physical execution bottlenecks, led by object acquisition and retention ( Grasp ) in general manipulation, spatial mating ( Alignment ) in tight-tolerance visuo-tactile tasks, and unintended collisions ( Interference ) in cluttered scenes. Recovery is also uneven across failure modes: Alignment failures are recovered less often than Grasp failures. To validate the reliability of our automated annotations, we construct a 400-action human-annotated evaluation benchmark and demonstrate that GPT-6 Astra aligns closely with human judgments, while ablating local intent causes a sharp collapse in failure-mode attribution. Finally, to enable low-cost action-level critique for direct robot control, we curate an open-source dataset of 12,844 annotated actions and fine-tune an open-source critic VLM, Muse-Glimmer-30B, which approaches GPT-6 Astra on both action outcome and failure-mode accuracy (Figure 1 (c)). Our main contributions are as follows: • Intent-Grounded Action Critique Framework: We propose AgentRoboCritic , a framework that evaluates frontier language model agents on direct robot control at the action level by conditioning the critique on both global task objectives and the policy’s local intent. • Action-Level Analysis of Frontier Agents on Direct Robot Control: We go beyond episode-level success rates to establish frontier agents as a promising path toward generalist robot policies and to identify the physical execution bottlenecks that remain. • Open-Source Critique Dataset and Critic VLM: We curate and release an action-level critique dataset alongside a 400-action human-annotated test set, as well as a fine-tuned Muse-Glimmer-30B critic VLM that enables accurate, low-cost failure analysis.

## 3 Method
To systematically understand the capabilities and current limitations of frontier agents in physical manipulation, we first formulate the paradigm of direct robot control (Section 3.1 ) and then introduce AgentRoboCritic , our intent-grounded failure attribution framework that diagnoses agent execution at the granularity of individual actions (Section 3.2 ). 3.1 Frontier Agents on Direct Robot Control We define direct robot control as a setting in which a frontier multimodal language agent directly generates numerical robot control commands from a global task instruction, visual observations, and interaction history. Consider a manipulation episode governed by a task instruction G (e.g., “open the box, take out the eraser, and close the lid” ) over discrete decision turns t\in\{1,\dots,T\} . At each turn t , given G , the current visual observation O_{t}^{\text{pre}} , and the interaction history h_{t} , the frontier agent policy \pi outputs a control command a_{t} : a_{t}\sim\pi\!\left(\cdot\mid G,\,O_{t}^{\text{pre}},\,h_{t}\right), (1) where a_{t} specifies low-level numerical targets such as 6-DoF end-effector poses, joint configurations, gripper openings, or primitive motion trajectories. The robot executes a_{t} in the physical environment and returns the updated observation O_{t}^{\text{post}} , forming a closed-loop control cycle. Unlike specialized VLAs or WAMs, which require additional action heads or continuous policy decoders atop pretrained vision-language or video-generation backbones, frontier agents express these numerical control actions as structured text through their native language-generation interface. To assess zero-shot capability on robot manipulation, we evaluate frontier agents without task-specific fine-tuning or in-context demonstrations, and compare their performance against VLAs and WAMs under the same zero-shot evaluation protocol. 3.2 Intent-Grounded Action-Level Critic While direct robot control allows frontier agents to solve complex manipulation tasks zero-shot, standard episode-level success rates obscure how individual actions succeed or fail during closed-loop execution.
