# OopsieRecover: Learning Transferable VLA Corrections from Semantic Interventions

## Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models can perform a wide range of robotic manipulation tasks, but often fail during deployment. Teaching them to recover from these failures requires substantial corrective data: existing approaches learn to recover from failures by directly associating corrective actions with the observations in which they were demonstrated. For instance, DAgger-like fine-tuning can require tens to hundreds of interactions, yet the resulting policy may still fail to generalize the learned corrections beyond the demonstrated scenarios. We hypothesize that semantics can capture the underlying reasons for failures and their corrections, enabling policies to learn from only a handful of corrective demonstrations and generalize those corrections to novel objects, and scene variations. We propose OopsieRecover, a framework that uses abduction to infer the intent behind a small set of corrective demonstrations and encodes the inferred knowledge as dense spatial representations and conditional costs. These costs capture when a behavior is undesirable and steer the policy toward more desirable action regions. We experiment across 6 tasks and variations in object poses, scenes, and related tasks, and find that only a handful of corrective demonstrations can enable a VLA to overcome failures across a broad range of novel situations, without requiring new corrective data for each variation. Please refer to https://robin-lab.cs.utexas.edu/oopsierecover for videos.

## I Introduction
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) provide broad manipulation capabilities [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] , but can still exhibit systematic failures during deployment [ 4 ] , such as collisions, dropping objects, etc. Human interventions offer a natural source of corrective data [ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] , yet existing interactive imitation methods generally treat corrections as additional state-action supervision [ 5 , 6 ] , making generalization depend on collecting enough corrections in similar states. Humans can instead infer a reusable principle from only a few corrections. We ask whether a robot can likewise infer what prompted an intervention, how its behavior should be steered to avoid it, and when the same correction should apply again. An intervention provides only partial evidence about this principle. Before takeover, the robot executes the VLA actions; afterward, the human provides a preferred continuation. Instead, the takeover time localizes the context that prompted the intervention and that generalizes to other similar situations, while the subsequent human-controlled segment provides a preferred continuation from the reached state towards a more optimal situation. A description such as “keep the mug upright when placing it on the plate” identifies the semantic property motivating the takeover, which may remain valid despite changes in object(s) poses, robot configuration, background, or even task. Trajectories alone leave ambiguous which property mattered; language alone lacks the sensorimotor grounding needed for visuomotor control. Together, they constrain a reusable correction. This ambiguity motivates OopsieRecover , which formulates correction inference as abduction [ 9 ] : finding a simple explanation consistent with the observed interventions despite missing or imperfect information. A vision-language model (VLM) leverages its visual-language priors, together with the scene, robot-controlled initial demonstration, human intervention, and language annotation, to propose candidate explanations. Since several explanations may fit one intervention, OopsieRecover evaluates each hypothesis across all available interventions and selects explanations that capture the underlying corrective behavior without being overly narrow or permissive. In this formal-methods inspired procedure, the VLM proposes plausible semantic hypotheses, and the corrective evidence determines whether they are retained or refined. We represent the selected explanation as one or more gated costs . Each combines a logical predicate specifying when the correction applies with a continuous cost specifying how the behavior should optimize/change. E.g., a gate may activate during placement, while its cost penalizes tilting the mug away from upright. This separation keeps corrections local rather globally biasing the policy. To execute these costs, OopsieRecover grounds task-relevant robot parts, object parts, and spatial locations and regions as semantic point sets. At test time, the VLA samples candidate action chunks, and OopsieRecover predicts how each candidate would move these point sets, how they evaluate on the gated costs, and which candidate would lead to the lowest cost overall. The frozen VLA supplies plausible behaviors, while the inferred correction steers its choices whenever the same semantic situation reappears. We evaluate OopsieRecover on 3 tasks in both sim and real using pi0 in RoboCasa and DROID, with only 5-8 corrections per task. OopsieRecover improves real-world task-progress from 35.1% to 61.0%, a 74% relative improvement over the next-best baseline, and transfers corrections to unseen poses, objects and scenes. We show that our gated costs generalize to novel variations in object poses and scenes zero-shot, and successfully steer the base policy. Our contributions are: i) a semantic, abductive formulation of few-shot VLA correction from human-in-the-loop interventions; ii) a generate-and-test procedure that synthesizes and verifies gated costs; and iii) a test-time steering mechanism that applies and generalizes them without retraining the VLA.

## III OopsieRecover
OopsieRecover learns reusable correction objectives from human interventions and uses them to steer a pretrained VLA. Figure 2 summarizes our method. A base policy \pi_{\mathrm{base}} executes a task instruction T , while a human monitors and intervenes to provide a semantic correction: a corrective trajectory and correction semantics expressed as a free-form language description of the cause of the correction (Sec. III-A ). OopsieRecover uses these semantic corrections to synthesize programmatic spatiotemporal objectives over semantically grounded scene entities (Sec. III-C ) in the form of gated correction costs (Sec. III-B ). During subsequent execution, OopsieRecover uses the learned gated correction costs to determine when policy steering is necessary and ranks candidate actions by their predicted costs under a learned dynamics model (Sec. III-D ). Further interventions provide additional evidence for refining an existing correction when specified by the human, or introducing a new one. Throughout this process, the base VLA remains frozen, adaptation occurs through the learned correction objectives; generalization to other domains is thus achieved by using the generalization of the unaltered VLA with the transferrable nature of OopsieRecover ’s gated correction costs. III-A Collecting Semantic Corrections During VLA execution, a human can intervene, taking control when the robot’s behavior requires correction, providing a corrective action sequence and the correction semantics \ell_{i}^{H} , and returning control to the VLA when the situation has been corrected [ 6 ] . For intervention episode i , we record all observations and actions as an intervention record \mathcal{I}_{i} , which also includes, when the episode continues after the human returns control, the base-policy window W^{i}_{\pi_{\text{base}}} : the K VLA-controlled steps immediately following the correction. Within an intervention, we distinguish the human-controlled window, W_{\mathrm{human}}^{i} , from a pre-intervention window, W_{\mathrm{pre}}^{i} , the K steps immediately preceding human takeover [ 11 , 13 , 7 ] . The pre-intervention window, though part of autonomous execution, provides evidence for the failing behavior that prompted the intervention, while the human-controlled window provides a preferred continuation from the reached state. After n interventions, the accumulated intervention dataset is \mathcal{D}_{n}=\left\{(\mathcal{I}_{i},\ell_{i}^{H},c_{i},T)\right\}_{i=1}^{n} . where c_{i} denotes the human-provided correction type, a semantic category capturing the underlying corrective behavior. For each correction type c , we group the corresponding records as \mathcal{D}_{c}=\left\{(\mathcal{I}_{i},\ell_{i}^{H}):(\mathcal{I}_{i},\ell_{i}^{H},c_{i})\in\mathcal{D}_{n},\;c_{i}=c\right\}. (1) OopsieRecover synthesizes a unifying correction objective from each type-based group of corrective interventions. To do so, we first define a representation capable of capturing transferable intent.
