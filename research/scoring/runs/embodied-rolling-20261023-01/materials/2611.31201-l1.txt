===ABSTRACT===
Frontier multimodal models exhibit extraordinary generalization in generating robot actions from observations and instructions, enabling a new paradigm in which they serve directly as policies within closed-loop robotic harnesses. However, the design principles of such harnesses, and the capabilities underlying their strong performance, remain unclear. We present Embo-Harness, a framework for systematically studying frontier-model-as-policy harnesses. We investigate four components of the robotic agentic loop, namely, prompting, planning, action execution, and tool use, and distill six empirical findings into a practical recipe. This recipe prioritizes interfaces that exploit the model's native visual reasoning and tool-use capabilities over handcrafted plans, skills, or action priors, and identifies effective choices for action representation, chunking, reasoning effort, visual observations, short multi-action sequences, and policy refinement. On RoboLab-120, Embo-Harness achieves an 83.3\% success rate, outperforming the strongest reported baseline by 26.2 percentage points. It also succeeds in 15 of 20 zero-shot trials across four real-world manipulation tasks. Beyond performance, controlled interventions indicate that the studied model depends more strongly on accurate spatial target estimation than on explicit low-level joint or trajectory generation. These results provide both a practical recipe for building strong frontier-model-as-policy harnesses and a clearer account of the capabilities that drive their performance.

===INTRO===
Recent advances in frontier models, such as GPT-6-Astra [ 49 ] , are reshaping robotic manipulation [ 74 , 62 ] . Their remarkable generalization capabilities enable them to handle complex instructions, diverse environments, long-horizon manipulation, and failure recovery, substantially outperforming existing robot foundation models (RFMs), such as Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) [ 78 , 6 , 38 , 36 , 67 ] and World Action Models (WAMs) [ 71 , 42 , 73 ] , on recent benchmarks [ 46 , 77 , 68 , 14 ] , offering a different possibility for future robotic policies. A robotic harness is the software loop that connects observations and instructions to planning, tool use, action generation, execution, and feedback. As illustrated in Figure 2 , existing robotic harnesses can be broadly divided according to how actions are produced. Tool- or code-as-policy [ 31 , 44 , 33 , 32 , 20 , 24 , 16 , 65 , 29 ] harnesses use multimodal models [ 3 , 27 ] for high-level planning but execute actions through predefined tools or generated code. Model-as-policy [ 58 , 75 , 56 ] harnesses instead delegate action generation to specialized learned policies. Recent frontier-model-as-policy harnesses take a further step by using the frontier model itself to plan and generate robot actions within a closed loop. Recent studies have established the feasibility of this paradigm [ 15 , 61 , 74 , 43 , 63 , 62 ] . For example, SenseNova-RoboRSI [ 62 ] improves a frontier-model policy through recursive self-improvement based on prior training datasets and test-time execution rollouts, while GPT-as-Policy [ 61 ] and related studies [ 49 , 17 ] profile the native robotic capabilities of frontier models. However, these works primarily demonstrate or improve the performance of particular systems. They do not systematically study which harness components are responsible for the gains, or why general-purpose frontier models can outperform policies specialized for robotic control. We address these questions through Embo-Harness , a controlled study for frontier-model-as-policy harnesses. Starting from a common baseline, we vary individual design factors and progressively integrate the effective choices, rather than optimizing the complete system as a single configuration. Our study covers four dimensions of the robotic agentic loop: prompting, planning, action execution, and tool use. The resulting six findings can be summarized into three principles. First , suitable action representations and execution strategies are essential: frontier models more effectively generate end-effector actions than joint-space actions and favor short, fixed-length action chunks over adaptive chunking. Second , their performance benefits substantially from stronger reasoning and richer visual observations, including higher resolutions and complementary viewpoints. Third , tools and learned policies should complement the model’s native capabilities rather than replace them: manually designed plans and failure-derived reminders provide limited benefits, whereas short multi-action sequences and VLA refinement improve effectiveness and efficiency. Guided by this recipe, Embo-Harness achieves an 83.3% success rate on RoboLab-120, compared with 57.1% for SenseNova-RoboRSI, the strongest existing baseline. It also completes 15 of 20 zero-shot trials across four real-world manipulation tasks. Beyond performance, we further investigate why frontier models succeed as robotic policies. Although they can generate joint-level and trajectory-level actions commonly learned by VLAs and WAMs, explicitly requiring such generation degrades performance. In contrast, directly predicting the final EEF destination outperforms short-range action generation, suggesting a stronger reliance on spatial perception than low-level action generation. We further validate this hypothesis through spatial position estimation experiments, observing broadly aligned trends with manipulation performance across reasoning efforts and visual resolutions, as well as comparable errors between direct position estimates and gripper positions in failed grasps, both measured against ground-truth target positions. These findings further highlight the central role of spatial perception in frontier models’ robotic capabilities.
