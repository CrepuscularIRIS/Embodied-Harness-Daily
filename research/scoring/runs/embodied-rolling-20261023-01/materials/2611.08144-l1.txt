# Detecting Silent Faults at the Environment-Trainer Boundary in Reinforcement Learning

## Abstract
Robot reinforcement learning increasingly runs environments outside the trainer: in another process, on another machine, in another language. Many faults at this boundary raise no error. We make the boundary a wire protocol and ask what notices a fault in it. On Pendulum, HalfCheetah, Hopper and a 7-joint arm (Pusher), we injected up to 23 faults into what crosses the wire and 2 into the trainer's log, from one value to every value. Unless every value was wrong, a learning-curve threshold caught at most one run in ten, and a comparison with ten fault-free seeds at most 21%. A clean evaluation of all 1,060 final policies agreed with the curves: of the 728 boundary-fault runs both curve checks passed, 723 scored no more than three standard deviations below fault-free seeds, though nearly all ended on other weights and, for reward faults, logged other returns. Checks of the run itself, not of its outcome, saw every fault. A step ledger kept on both sides and compared step by step caught and located every fault that crossed, on Pendulum, Hopper and Pusher on one machine and on Pendulum across two, with no fault-free run and, on Pendulum, a median 3% more training time. An episode record kept at the environment caught the faults in the trainer's log. We list such faults found in practice, in Gymnasium 1.3 and our own code and, reported by others, in five multi-agent libraries, and recommend a step ledger and episode counts kept at the environment.

## I Introduction
An RL run has a boundary between the code that steps the environment and the trainer , which collects experience and updates the policy: the agent–environment interface [ 1 ] , drawn in software. In many stacks nobody designs it; it falls where the trainer puts it. A Gymnasium environment [ 2 ] is an object in the trainer’s process. RLlib [ 3 ] , SRL [ 4 ] and RLinf [ 5 ] run environments in workers that install the framework and join its cluster. Robot learning moves the boundary further: to simulators in other languages, machines with no GPU stack, and robots reaching a policy over the network [ 6 , 7 ] . Many faults at this boundary are silent: they raise no error or warning [ 8 ] . Under its SAME_STEP autoreset mode, Gymnasium 1.3’s vector episode statistics drop the first reward of every episode after an environment’s first, and count its length one short, until 1.4 (Sec. IV ). Training goes on. For environments on another machine, our own evidence that the boundary was right was that the policy still learned. We test that evidence. PlugRL makes the boundary an explicit wire protocol (Sec. II ). The environment side sends observations, applies the actions it gets back, and feeds back rewards and episode ends. A written specification and conformance checkers say what each side must do, and lockstep bridges let Stable-Baselines3 (SB3) [ 9 ] , CleanRL [ 10 ] and RLinf train through it. On one machine a run through it is deterministic, so we can compare a run with an injected fault against the same seed without it. We call a difference between what the environment took and gave and what the trainer sent and received a boundary fault . Our method is mutation testing as RLMutation [ 11 ] applies it to deep RL, and the checks that work are the ones production ML uses against training–serving skew [ 12 ] ; what we add is measuring both at the environment–trainer boundary. We find: • Neither a learning curve nor a clean evaluation shows most boundary faults (Sec. III , Fig. Abstract ). We injected stale, zeroed, swapped or rounded observations, actions and rewards, wrong episode ends and skipped steps. On Pendulum, the gain threshold we had relied on caught 1 of 21 faults at doses up to 10% of values, and 9 when every value was wrong. On a clean environment, 723 of the 728 boundary-fault runs that passed the curve checks scored like fault-free runs. • A step ledger catches and locates every boundary fault, as it must, and cheaply (Sec. III ). A boundary fault is a difference between the two sides’ records, so comparing them must catch it. We measured that it needs no fault-free run or determinism, works across machines, names the step, environment and field, and on Pendulum costs a median 3% of training time. An episode record kept at the environment catches faults in the trainer’s log. • Such faults occur in practice (Sec. IV ): in Gymnasium 1.3 and our own code, and, reported by others, in five multi-agent libraries. A silent fault, then, mostly changes the run rather than the policy: its weights and, for reward and log faults, the numbers it reports. The record of the experiment stops describing the experiment. Every experiment follows a protocol written before its registered runs, most after a pilot. Changes to it go in a dated amendment. Captions, tables and appendices name each result’s experiment (E43, …), whose protocol, logs and findings are public with the code, specification and checkers: https://github.com/PlugRL .

## II The Boundary as a Protocol
Messages. A connection opens with the server’s metadata (version, chunk length H , offered features). The client then repeats three messages (Fig. 4 ), msgpack-encoded with NumPy arrays over a WebSocket. An infer carries the observations of n environments, their indices and their step ids; an action reply carries a chunk of H actions; after the chunk, a feedback carries the observation it ended on, the summed reward, the termination and truncation flags, and an info map. The client resets its own environments: the feedback that ends an episode carries the terminal observation, and the next infer the reset one. A connection closes with stop (the run is over) or resync (reconnect at once). What the protocol does about faults. • One convention, written down. The specification fixes one episode-end convention where Gymnasium has several (Sec. IV ), and states every MUST clause. A checker for each side tests against them, with active probes. • Structural faults are loud. The bridge rejects a message with the wrong shape, keys or environments and closes the connection. It never fills in a missing step: losing a client stops the run, and a client too many is told to stop. • Runs are deterministic on one machine. The bridge is a lockstep server: each trainer step sends every environment its action, and returns once every client has fed back. So two runs through it can be compared byte for byte. • Each side can keep its records. A wrapper at the env client can keep an episode record and half a step ledger; the bridge keeps a bridge log of the rewards it received. Adapters present an episode end as RLinf, Gymnasium ( SAME_STEP ) and SB3 expect it, so these trainers use the protocol through the environment interface they already have.

## III What Notices a Fault
The faults. SB3’s PPO trained Pendulum, HalfCheetah, Hopper and Pusher, a 7-joint arm that pushes an object to a goal, through the bridge (Appendix F ), while the env client changed one field of what it sent, in one way. The observation it sent, the action it applied, the reward, or the observation an episode ended on was replaced by zeros, by the previous step’s value or by the next environment’s, or rounded through float16; an action could also be clipped to half its range. An episode end was invented, or a time limit left out or sent as a termination; on Hopper, which ends an episode when it falls, a termination was also left out or sent as a time limit. Or a step was skipped and its feedback filled in. Two log faults sat in the trainer’s own episode log, of the kind we found in Gymnasium (Sec. IV ): each episode’s first reward left out, or its length counted one short. Each fault changed one value, 1% or every value it could reach (on Pendulum also 0.1% and 10%), the same values at every seed, seeds 0–2. The checks (Fig. 4 in the appendix). A run’s fault was caught by the gain threshold if its gain , the mean logged return at the end of training minus that at the start, fell below a bar fixed before the runs (on Pendulum, 500); by seed spread if its final return lay more than three standard deviations from the mean of ten fault-free seeds; by byte identity if its final weights differed from the fault-free run’s at the same seed; by the episode record if the trainer’s log disagreed with the env client’s record, kept before any fault, in count, length or return; and by the step ledger if the two sides’ records of any step differed. Learning curves miss them (Fig. 2 ). At doses up to 10% of values, the gain threshold caught at most one run in ten on all four environments. With every value wrong it caught 43%, 35%, 48% and 33%, and mostly faults that broke learning or emptied the log.
