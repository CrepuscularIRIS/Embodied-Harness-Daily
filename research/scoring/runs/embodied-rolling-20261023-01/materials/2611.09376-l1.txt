# Can LLMs Debug Robots by Editing Their Weights?

## Abstract
A language model can repair a robot policy's lexical instruction grounding by editing just 65 token-embedding rows. We bring weight-space programming to robot foundation policies: GPT-6 Astra reads weights and uses a CPU workspace and budgeted simulator feedback, without gradient updates or new demonstrations. Three independent sessions on $π_{0.5}$ all improve success on 765 held-out paraphrases; the best raises success from 86.3\% to 92.0\% (48 repairs, four regressions; exact paired $p=1.31\times10^{-10}$). Among the 335 instructions containing edited tokens, success rises from 74.3\% to 87.5\%; the other 430 episodes have zero outcome changes. Matched random-direction edits do not help. Full replacement using the editor's own lexicon explains much of the gain but produces more regressions; its direct difference from the editor is not significant at 0.05. Across 13 further robot sessions, the editor declines 12 candidates; the sole retained edit does not show a significant held-out gain. Separately, benchmark metadata lowers FastWAM success from 70.0\% to 48.6\%, while the evaluated VLA's aggregate rate is unchanged. The evidence supports precise lexical repair, not general acquisition of missing robot capabilities.

## 1 Introduction
A language model can repair some robot-policy failures by changing a few rows of the policy’s weights. Our strongest result changes 65 token-embedding rows: success rises from 74.3% to 87.5% on 335 held-out instructions containing an edited token, with no outcome changes on the other 430 instructions. The manipulation skill is already available; the edit changes which language reaches it. Consider a policy that turns on the stove under its canonical instruction, but picks up a plate when asked to “activate the cooking appliances heat source” (Figure 1 ). Both actions are familiar tasks in the same scene. The failure is a mismatch between wording and the task represented by the policy, rather than the absence of a stove-turning behavior. A camera perturbation or a dropped object presents a different debugging problem: changing the interpretation of a word may not supply the required visual or recovery behavior. Takehi et al. [ 21 ] introduced weight-space programming : a general-purpose LLM inspects and changes another network’s parameters directly, without gradient optimization. Their demonstrations span games and language models, and motivate a distinction between removing an obstacle to an existing ability and creating a new ability. We bring this experimental setup to robot foundation policies. We test both whether an editor can find a useful intervention and whether it declines interventions that fail its own checks. GPT-6 Astra receives policy weights, a persistent CPU Python workspace, and a budgeted forward tool. It can inspect tensors and write replacements, but cannot train the policy or change the deployment program. Practice rollouts and probes guide editing; held-out variants are unavailable to the editor. Across three independent sessions on \pi_{0.5} [ 20 ] , Astra converges on sparse lexical remapping. All three submitted edits improve held-out paraphrase success. The best changes less than 0.004% of exported parameters and raises overall success from 86.3% to 92.0% on all 765 held-out language variants. The boundary matters as much as the gain. In 13 additional sessions over FastWAM [ 24 ] , LIBERO-Plus [ 6 ] , LIBERO-PRO [ 27 ] , and LIBERO-RECOVER [ 12 ] , the editor declines 12 final candidates. The sole retained candidate has no significant held-out gain. Two archived, declined candidates do have positive aggregate estimates; we report them rather than treating final-directory contents as accepted repairs. These observations support a limited, practical form of debugging, not a general impossibility theorem about acquiring capabilities. Our contributions are: • A reproducible lexical repair of a VLA: three independent editing sessions, one fixed held-out population, and outcome changes confined to the edited-token instructions. • Controls that separate direction from edit size and full replacement from the editor’s interpolation, together with an explicit account of what a dictionary control already explains. • A 16-session record of retained and declined edits, plus a separate finding that benchmark instruction metadata strongly affects FastWAM but not the evaluated \pi_{0.5} checkpoint in aggregate.

## 2 A Fixed Interface for Weight-Space Debugging
Weights, tools, and constraints. Figure 2 shows the common interface. The editor receives tensor names and shapes, read access to original weights, a tokenizer where applicable, and a CPU Python kernel whose state persists between calls. It can perform static linear algebra and save or remove replacement tensors. The protocol forbids gradient updates, training loops, iterative loss fitting, and execution of the target network inside the kernel. The model is evaluated with gradients disabled. Only weight values may change: tensor names, shapes, input instructions, action scaling, and inference code remain fixed. A separate forward tool runs simulator episodes or replays saved model inputs for action and activation probes. The kernel can inspect returned observations and outcomes. Thus no additional demonstrations does not mean no observations or feedback; the policies were already pretrained and fine-tuned on demonstrations. A forward request is a batch, not one neural-network evaluation. Robot sessions receive 40–50 requests; all three language-repair sessions receive 50. A Python execution may use up to 900 seconds and 96 GB of CPU memory. No extra policy training is performed for this report. Splits and candidate selection. Practice and held-out variants are separated by the parity of a SHA-256 hash of suite and variant ID. The language task exposes 4,986 practice variants across perturbation categories, but no held-out list. Each session reserves its own practice validation before making a final retain/revert decision. The three \pi_{0.5} sessions start separately with the same checkpoint and task specification; they do not inherit each other’s notes or edits. They are independent searches, not independent test datasets. In contrast, the three FastWAM suffix follow-ups inherit the first session’s notes and archived candidate. After editing, each submitted language candidate is evaluated on all 765 held-out Language Instructions variants and a 360-episode sample of the other six categories. The latter sample is shared across sessions. A smaller, earlier sample of 200 held-out language variants is disclosed as well (Appendix A ); it did not support a significant gain. The all-variant population is fixed by the original split, not selected from successful examples, and the candidates are not tuned on either held-out evaluation. Pairing and statistics. Original and edited weights are compared on the same variant, seed, and initial state. Held-out language evaluation uses seed 7. Weight overrides are applied in place; restoration is verified afterward. Let g count original failures that become successes and l original successes that become failures. We report (g-l)/n , paired normal-approximation intervals, and exact two-sided McNemar tests [ 13 ] . Tests refer to the evaluated episode pairs, not uncertainty over editor sessions. Category and follow-up comparisons are exploratory. The three primary language effects also remain significant under Holm correction within that three-test family. Runtime-error handling and complete counts are in Appendix A . What is and is not replicated. Pairing measures changed outcomes, not just two independent success rates: C has 52 discordances, of which 48 favor the edit. Its exact test conditions on those discordances. The three language effects remain significant after Holm correction (adjusted p=3.92\times 10^{-10} , 3.64\times 10^{-4} , and .0106 for C, A, and B). This correction covers the three primary candidates, not every exploratory probe in the study. Independent sessions test whether the editor can find an edit again; shared variants and one held-out seed limit claims about environmental generalization. The all-variant evaluation follows the weaker 200-episode check. Its population is the entire pre-existing held-out language split, and the candidates remain unchanged. This avoids selecting variants that respond well to an edit, but does not turn the larger test into a preregistered independent replication. We disclose both evaluations and their overlap.
