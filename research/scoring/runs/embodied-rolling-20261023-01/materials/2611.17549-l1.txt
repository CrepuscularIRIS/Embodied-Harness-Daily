# Talk to Act: A Synthetic Multi-Turn Dialogue Benchmark for Human Intent Extraction and Action Planning in Robotics

## Abstract
Multi-turn conversational human-robot interaction (HRI) continues to be a challenging problem in robotics, as many existing systems rely on rigid, command-driven interfaces that can be unintuitive for users. Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) provide a promising direction, given their ability to process natural language, generate structured outputs, and support tool-based reasoning, all of which are relevant for interpreting user intent in robotic settings. However, progress in this area is constrained by the limited availability of datasets and benchmarks that capture the nuances of real-world human communication, such as multi-turn dialogue, ambiguity, and context-dependent requests. In this work, we present a synthetically generated multi-turn dialogue dataset for HRI, annotated with action intents, object references, and task plans across a range of scenarios and user personas. Our primary focus is on evaluating the capability of small, open-weight language models for robotic tasks, particularly in settings where local deployment and efficiency are important. To this end, we frame intent extraction as a tool-calling problem where each predicted action corresponds to a structured function call with arguments grounded in the dialogue and benchmark models ranging from 270M to 8B parameters on their ability to perform intent extraction and action planning without task-specific fine-tuning. Our results show that these models can achieve reasonable performance on the task even at relatively small scales and without any task-specific fine-tuning. This suggests that compact, locally deployable models already provide a useful baseline for conversational robotic systems. We expect that task-specific fine-tuning and more realistic training data will enhance performance further and make such models increasingly suitable for real-world robotic applications.

## I Introduction
Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) has emerged as a critical frontier in robotics, enabling robots to operate alongside humans in shared environments such as assisted living facilities, warehouses, and domestic settings. As robots move from controlled industrial cells into homes, care environments, and shared workspaces, communication becomes a core capability rather than a peripheral interface. In recent years, substantial progress has been made in robotics, particularly in perception and control. Large embodied datasets and generalist robot policies have advanced robotic performance. Vision-language-action models (VLAs) have demonstrated the effectiveness of web-scale pretraining in enhancing semantic generalization in robotic control. However, interaction between humans and robots is still unnatural. A robot that is capable of perceiving objects and executing movements with precision may still struggle when a user makes an underspecified, multistep, or revised request in everyday language. Nevertheless, natural language is the most intuitive communication medium for people. Previous studies have contrasted natural language with complex graphical interfaces, structured command languages, and formal abstractions, which require training before a user can effectively control a robot. For robots in homes, assistive scenarios, or shared human environments, it is often unrealistic to expect the human to learn a rigid interaction protocol. A more realistic objective is for robots to be able to interpret natural, unconstrained, and potentially messy human language as it is actually spoken. However, unrestricted language is challenging because human requests are often context-dependent, incomplete, and ambiguous. Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have introduced new opportunities for integrating natural language into robotic tasks. Models such as GPT-5, Claude and Gemini demonstrate strong capabilities in common sense reasoning, following instructions, and tool calling — properties that are directly relevant to interpreting user intent and translating it into structured robot actions. LLMs ability to handle compositional instructions and generate structured outputs makes LLMs a promising foundation for the interaction layer. Furthermore, LLMs offer a promising approach to generating synthetic data, enabling the creation of extensive and diverse dialogue corpora that would otherwise be too costly to collect through human annotation. Although LLMs have great potential in HRI, existing approaches tend to reduce interaction to single-turn intent extraction within narrowly defined task domains [ 1 ] . However, real human communication is multi-turn, context-dependent, and often under-specified. Users revise goals, refer to objects implicitly, and issue instructions that are only interpretable when considering the prior conversational context. While large frontier models may be attractive for intent understanding due to their strong reasoning capabilities, their deployment in robotic systems is constrained by latency, connectivity requirements, cost, and privacy considerations. In contrast, recent compact open-weight models such as Phi-4, Gemma series, and Qwen series demonstrate that smaller models are becoming increasingly capable at instruction following and structured output generation, making them compelling candidates for on-device or edge-assisted HRI pipelines. Whether such models can reliably handle the full complexity of natural, multi-turn human dialogue in HRI contexts, however, remains an open question. Alongside language, perception capabilities have also advanced significantly. Modern models such as SAM 3 [ 2 ] and Grounded DINO [ 3 ] now support open-vocabulary segmentation and detection driven by natural language queries. Yet current HRI systems rarely leverage these capabilities, leaving a gap between what perception models can accept and what interaction pipelines actually provide. A well-designed dialogue system that extracts structured referential expressions from human conversation could directly bridge this gap, feeding natural language grounded outputs into modern perception models. A key bottleneck in advancing natural language-based HRI is the lack of datasets that reflect the complexity of real human communication. Existing benchmarks are limited in scale, domain diversity, and conversational depth. To address this, we introduce a synthetically generated multi-turn dialogue dataset for HRI, designed to capture natural, ambiguous, and context-dependent human instructions annotated with action intent, action plans, and object intent. The dataset spans a variety of real-world scenarios and user personas, reflecting the diversity of people and contexts a robot may encounter in practice. ## III Method
III-A Synthetic Dataset Generation We used Qwen 3.6 [ 17 ] as the backbone for synthetic data generation; the full pipeline is illustrated in Figure 2 . Each conversation consists of at least ten turns between a user and an assistant, spanning everyday chitchat to explicit task-based requests. We formulate intent parsing as a tool-calling problem, where the model must invoke one of four predefined actions, pick, place, pour, and handoff, in response to user requests. Each conversation contains more than one task request, requiring the model to plan multiple actions per conversation while maintaining state across turns. For example, if the robot has already picked an object, it cannot pick again until it has placed it, and the model must track this state throughout the dialogue. The dataset covers a range of scenarios from household tasks to warehouse settings, and includes diverse user personas to reflect realistic interaction conditions. Every generated sample is validated against a predefined JSON schema and then verified and corrected by an LLM to ensure consistent, well-formed annotations. In total, we collected 977 conversations. We release the dataset publicly to facilitate future research in this area. III-B Action Schema and Object Intent We define the following four actions: pick, place, pour, and handoff. Each action is represented by a structured JSON object containing parameters that describe the action type and the objects involved. Object parameters include a name and a full grounding phrase extracted from the conversation. Optional properties include color, shape, material, and texture. The place action also captures the spatial relationship between the target and a reference object. This structured representation is directly motivated by downstream perception requirements.
