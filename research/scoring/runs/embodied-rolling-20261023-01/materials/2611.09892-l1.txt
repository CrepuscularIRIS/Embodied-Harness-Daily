# On Agentic Manipulation Policies

## Abstract
Recent work on robot manipulation has focused on specialist policies trained on demonstrations. We show that generalist AI models can control manipulators more reliably than specialist policies such as vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world-action models (WAMs). We systematically analyze agentic manipulation policies (AMPs), in which a generalist AI model commands the manipulator directly, adapting its actions as the task unfolds. We present AMP1, a reference harness that runs unchanged across single-arm, bimanual, and mobile manipulation. AMP1 achieves state-of-the-art results on the RoboLab-120 and CaP-Gym benchmarks, with no demonstrations or task-specific training. On RoboLab-120, AMP1 reaches an 85.6\% success rate zero-shot, compared to 49.2\% and 42.9\% for leading VLA and WAM policies. The agent succeeds zero-shot in 104 of 108 real-world trials spanning twelve tasks and three robot instances, reducing failure rates by 20x and 15x relative to strong VLA and WAM baselines. The basic harness supplies no grasping, pushing, or placing skills; the agent develops its own strategies, such as searching for hidden objects, using objects as tools, and calibrating its cameras. With the addition of a persistent memory store, the agent can also learn from experience: an augmented harness with a memory representation that is carried across episodes lowers the failure rate on RoboDojo bimanual manipulation tasks by 4.6x relative to zero-shot deployment. Our analysis suggests that AMPs are substantially more general and robust than specialist manipulation policies, providing a promising foundation for robot manipulation.

## 1 Introduction
In nature, manipulation draws on general intelligence. Animals can recruit their memory, reasoning, and problem-solving faculties when interacting with objects, with cognitive processes continuing to shape action as it unfolds [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . In robotics, much recent work has focused on models specialized for robot control, including vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world-action models (WAMs), trained or fine-tuned on robot demonstrations [ 4 , 5 ] . Generalist AI models have also been applied to manipulation, often through skill orchestration or offline program synthesis. In the first setting (skill orchestration), the model selects and sequences low-level skills such as grasping and placing [ 6 , 7 , 8 ] . It can revise the plan as new observations arrive, but the available skills limit the generalist model’s control authority and the granularity at which it can command the manipulator. Since the generalist model’s interactions with the world are mediated by the provided skills, it is constrained by the skills’ limitations. In the second setting (offline program synthesis, sometimes known as ‘code-as-policies’), the model writes a control program before execution [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] . The program can incorporate perception and feedback, but the generalist model is not in the loop and cannot reconsider the overall control strategy as the task unfolds. A key obstacle to direct manipulation has been the limited spatial cognition of generalist frontier models [ 13 ] . However, spatial cognition has been advancing [ 14 , 15 ] , with recent models demonstrating convincing spatial planning, sensorimotor coordination, and embodied fluid intelligence [ 16 , 17 ] . These advances make a third approach increasingly viable: the generalist model remains in the control loop and commands the manipulator directly, revising its actions as the task unfolds [ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ] . We refer to this category as Agentic Manipulation Policies (AMPs). In this paper, we systematically analyze agentic manipulation policies across models and harness configurations. We present AMP1, a reference harness that provides visual observations, code execution, and direct control of manipulator motion. Paired with frontier models, AMP1 achieves state-of-the-art zero-shot results on the RoboLab-120 [ 22 ] and CaP-Gym [ 18 ] benchmarks. The agent succeeds in 104 of 108 real-world trials spanning twelve tasks and three robot instances, compared to 49 for FLUX 3 Action [ 23 ] and 30 for \pi_{0.5} [ 24 ] , thus reducing the failure rate by 15x and 20x, respectively, relative to these WAM and VLA baselines. See Fig. 1 and the supplementary video for curated highlights. We document emergent behaviors that arise as the agent brings its broader cognitive faculties to bear on manipulation. When a book lies flat on a table and cannot be grasped as is, the agent pushes it to the table edge to expose a graspable surface. When a target object is hidden, the agent moves occluding objects out of the way to find it. When a large fruit is too wide to grasp, the agent pins it against a heavier object, slides the gripper underneath, and lifts it like a forklift. Failed actions can themselves supply information: the agent infers an unseen shelf from a blocked motion and changes its grasp and approach. On the real robot, the agent diagnoses camera calibration errors from discrepancies between commanded and observed motion, then compensates for them in subsequent actions. None of these behaviors are specified in the prompt or supplied as skills in the harness. The generalist model develops them within each episode by reasoning about geometry, contact, and the consequences of its own actions. We also investigate few-shot and continual learning from the agent’s own experience. We equip AMP1 with a persistent memory, allowing the agent to record useful observations, strategies, and code after reflecting on its successes and failures. Experience accumulates in this memory bank, while the model’s weights remain fixed. No expert demonstrations or gradient updates are required. We evaluate this approach on the 42 bimanual manipulation tasks of RoboDojo [ 25 ] . Practicing individual tasks raises the success rate on unseen layouts from 26.4% to 84.1%, above all entries on the benchmark’s leaderboard. We also test whether the agent can accumulate knowledge across tasks via continual learning. In this setting, the agent attempts each task once, in sequence, and updates its memory representation in the course of this continual deployment. A single pass through the tasks more than doubles the success rate, to 57.6%. Learning transfers to tasks not yet attempted without erasing gains on tasks already encountered. Experience improves efficiency as well: an agent with memory uses about 35% fewer model calls in comparison to zero-shot deployment. ## 2 Results
2A AMP1 achieves state-of-the-art zero-shot results 2A.1 Benchmarks We chose four simulation benchmarks that facilitate comprehensive quantitative evaluation of AMP1 against the most effective learned policies and other agents ( Fig. 2 A). RoboLab-120 comprises 120 manipulation tasks specified in natural language [ 22 ] . The tasks span visual, relational, and procedural competencies; each is posed with default, vague, and specific instructions. The other three benchmarks are suites of CaP-Gym [ 18 ] : LIBERO-PRO perturbs object positions and instructions to test whether a policy generalizes beyond familiar scenes [ 32 ] ; robosuite has five unimanual and two bimanual tasks [ 33 ] ; and two tasks from BEHAVIOR-1K that require mobile manipulation in a simulated home [ 34 ] . We run 10 episodes per task on RoboLab-120 (3,600 in total across the default, vague, and specific instruction conditions), 50 on LIBERO-PRO (3,000 in total), 100 on robosuite (700), and 25 on BEHAVIOR-1K (50). The AMP1 harness is the same on all four benchmarks; only the robot description and camera configuration change.
