===ABSTRACT===
General-purpose multimodal foundation models enable robots to adapt during deployment from observations and execution feedback, without task-specific demonstrations or training. However, the robot retains what it learns only in the model's working context. Once the task ends, it loses this execution knowledge, such as how to carry a bowl between two grippers. On the next attempt at the task, the robot may take just as long and repeat the same mistakes. To avoid this repetition, robots must retain execution knowledge across attempts. Yet replaying past actions is insufficient, because their effects depend on the current conditions. Procedures that determine actions from the current scene address this, but when they meet conditions that earlier attempts did not cover, revising them requires the measured effects of earlier actions. We therefore propose SEEK, an agentic framework that retains both, enabling robots to improve across attempts while keeping the underlying model fixed. To adapt retained knowledge to the current scene, SEEK adopts a Self-Evolving Programmatic Policy (SEPP) that encodes manipulation as executable procedures and determines each action from current observations. As the robot executes these procedures, SEEK records the measured effects of its actions, including those from failed attempts, in a Persistent Action-Effect Bank (PAEB). Where a retained procedure applies, it acts without a model request; where it meets a condition it does not cover, this evidence guides its revision. On RoboDojo, knowledge retained over ten attempts raises SEEK's average task score from 27.0 to 54.4 and its success rate from 19.7% to 48.0% across all 42 tasks. On 34 of these tasks with GPT-6.2 Astra, SEEK reaches 80.1 after five attempts, against 49.6 for the same model acting directly, and uses 77.6% fewer model requests. https://zeyangguan.github.io/SEEK-Agentic-Robot-Manipulation/

===INTRO===
Recent advances in robot learning have been driven by scaling demonstration data and model capacity, producing policies with broader task coverage and stronger generalization [ 4 , 28 , 40 , 10 , 20 , 27 ] . Yet these capabilities remain bounded by the distributions represented in the training data [ 4 , 20 , 13 ] . Extending these policies to new embodiments, tasks, or conditions beyond this coverage typically requires additional data collection, training, or fine-tuning [ 18 , 10 , 20 , 9 , 1 ] . As a result, capability growth remains closely coupled to pre-collected demonstrations and the imitation distribution they define [ 28 , 13 ] . General-purpose multimodal models offer another route to robot adaptation through reasoning during deployment [ 17 , 22 , 14 , 16 ] . By combining current visual observations with task instructions and execution feedback, these models can guide the robot’s actions and adjust its behavior as execution unfolds [ 15 , 14 , 24 ] . Through this interaction, the robot acquires execution knowledge : the effects its actions produce under given conditions and the procedures that prove effective. For example, it may find that a thin coin remains on the table when pinched at its center but lifts when pinched at its rim. However, the robot retains this knowledge only within the model’s working context. Once the task ends, the knowledge is lost. On its next attempt, the robot may again pinch the coin at its center before rediscovering the rim, taking just as long as before. Avoiding this repetition requires retaining execution knowledge across attempts. Yet simply replaying past actions is insufficient. The same action can yield different effects under different conditions. The robot therefore needs reusable procedures that determine each action from the current scene. Existing and concurrent approaches have begun to retain such procedures as skills and programs [ 34 , 33 , 21 , 23 , 13 ] . However, retaining a procedure does not ensure that it will handle conditions beyond those encountered in earlier attempts. When a procedure encounters such conditions, measured action effects provide evidence for deciding how to revise it. This evidence should include actions from failed attempts. Procedures and measured action effects therefore need to be retained together, so that a procedure can act without the model where it applies and be revised from this evidence where it does not. Existing methods that keep action-level evidence still leave each action to the model or a learned policy [ 36 , 35 , 23 ] , while programs that act without the model are frozen once developed [ 12 ] . To this end, we introduce SEEK, a framework for agentic robot manipulation that enables self-evolution through execution knowledge while keeping the underlying model fixed. SEEK retains this knowledge in two coupled forms. At the procedure level, a Self-Evolving Programmatic Policy (SEPP) encodes observation, action generation, verification, task progression, and recovery as executable procedures. During execution, these procedures use current observations to determine actions. At the action level, a Persistent Action-Effect Bank (PAEB) records the measured effect of each executed action with its execution context, including actions from failed attempts. Drawing on these records, the fixed model makes decisions or revises a procedure, and each revision retains references to its supporting evidence. Together, the two components form a continual self-improvement loop: execution supplies evidence for policy revision, and revised procedures carry the resulting capability into subsequent attempts. Once a retained procedure applies to the current scene, it can generate actions without another model request, so successive attempts reuse previously acquired behavior instead of repeating model deliberation. We evaluate SEEK on RoboDojo [ 6 ] , a simulation benchmark of 42 dual-arm manipulation tasks. To isolate the contribution of retained knowledge, we compare the same system with and without it on scenes unseen during self-evolution; both conditions allow adaptation within an attempt. Across the 42 tasks, knowledge retained over ten attempts more than doubles task performance. Compared with GPT-6.2 Astra acting directly on 34 of these tasks, SEEK with the same model raises the task score from 49.6 to 80.1 after five attempts and uses 77.6% fewer model requests. We further measure the fraction of executed steps generated by retained procedures, and ablate PAEB and SEPP to separate the contributions of action-level evidence reuse and procedure-level policy evolution. Finally, on a smaller set of tasks, we replace the underlying model and run SEEK on a real robot; in both settings, SEEK again improves task performance. Contributions.
