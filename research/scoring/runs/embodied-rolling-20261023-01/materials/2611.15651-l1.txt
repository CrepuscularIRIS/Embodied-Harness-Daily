# Recover, Don't Just Continue: Learning from Feedback for Physical Failure Understanding and Correction

## Abstract
Multimodal large language models are regarded as the cognitive foundation of embodied intelligence, but planning from instructions alone cannot make them reliable. During execution, actions deviate from intended effects, constrained by the embodiment and environment. An embodied brain must understand why execution deviates, identify what can be reliably realized, and revise decisions using interaction experience. These correspond to causal failure localization, capability-constrained adaptation, and experience-driven revision. Current embodied foundation models largely lack these capabilities. To address this, we introduce Embodied-Corrector. We construct failure-recovery data by injecting perturbations at subtask boundaries of successful trajectories, replaying affected subtasks, and keeping failures that are observable from public evidence and recoverable from the current state. We align actions, consequences, failure causes, and recoveries into Judge-Cause-Sub-Fine supervision. Training uses evaluation-first SFT: the model learns Judge and Cause first, then Sub and Fine conditioned on accurate prefixes. Since deployment uses self-generated prefixes, evaluation errors propagate into recovery planning, and response-level rewards are too coarse to locate affected planning tokens. We therefore use OPD to internalize the planning distribution under accurate prefixes into that under self-generated ones: the model generates plans from self-generated prefixes, while a contrastive forward pass with accurate prefixes compares token probabilities to reinforce or suppress planning tokens. At deployment, this branch is removed. Experiments show stronger failure analysis and corrective planning: with a frozen VLA, composite task success improves by 6.1 percentage points; with fixed GPT-5.5 execution, manipulation and navigation tasks improve by 13.9 percentage points.

## 1 Introduction
Multimodal large language models are becoming an important foundation for embodied intelligence ( Driess et al., 2023 ; Brohan et al., 2023a ; Ahn et al., 2022a ; Reed et al., 2022 ) . By combining visual understanding with language reasoning, they can interpret task goals, reason about scene relations, and decompose complex instructions into executable steps ( Liang et al., 2023a ; Huang et al., 2022a ; Singh et al., 2023 ; Song et al., 2023 ; Pan et al., 2026a ) . Recent embodied foundation models such as RynnBrain ( Dang et al., 2026b ) , RoboBrain 2.0 ( BAAI RoboBrain Team, 2026 ) , and Embodied-Reasoner ( Zhang et al., 2026a ) have advanced spatio-temporal memory, physical-world reasoning, and embodied planning; VeBrain-1.5 ( VeBrain Team, 2026 ) further unifies multimodal perception, spatial reasoning, and robot control in a shared decision interface. However, a reliable embodied brain requires more than planning. During physical execution, actions deviate from intended effects, and what can be realized is jointly constrained by the embodiment, controller, and environment ( Anonymous, 2026b ) . An embodied brain must therefore understand why execution deviated, recognize what the current executor can reliably realize, and adjust decisions using interaction experience. This raises the central question: how can a multimodal model evolve from planning what to do into an embodied brain that understands physical interaction, adapts to execution capability, and corrects its behavior after failure? We argue that this requires three related but non-substitutable capabilities. First, physical causal understanding and failure localization ( Ying and others, 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026a ; Anonymous, 2026d ) requires determining not only that an execution failed, but also which action, physical relation, or unmet prerequisite caused it. Existing failure-memory and detection methods can flag unsuccessful outcomes ( Ying and others, 2026 ) , yet a model may observe an object not placed into a container while failing to distinguish incorrect grasp timing, inappropriate contact pose, or an earlier uncompleted step. Effective correction requires tracing execution back to the condition that first invalidated progress. Second, execution-capability identification and adaptation ( Physical Intelligence Team, 2026 ; Ghosh et al., 2024 ) requires translating intent into a form the current executor can reliably realize. Even a correct diagnosis is ineffective if the recovery exceeds the controller’s capability. The brain should adapt instruction content and granularity, e.g., decomposing a long request into state-conditioned subtasks for a frozen VLA Physical Intelligence Team (2026) ; Brohan et al. (2023b) ; O’Neill et al. (2024) . Third, in-context learning from interaction experience ( Chen et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026c ; Sridhar et al., 2025 ) requires information from one execution to change the next decision. Prior work indicates that a robot’s own physical interaction experience can support in-context learning even when the policy model is frozen ( Chen et al., 2026 ) . Failure observations, previous actions, and successful corrections should become evidence for planning rather than accumulating in context while the model repeats the same behavior ( Anonymous, 2026a ; Anonymous, 2026c ) . We first examine whether current embodied brains possess these capabilities. On failed EB-ALFRED ( Yang et al., 2025b ) executions, we supply frozen multimodal models with the same task goal, visual observations, executed actions, and execution feedback, and inspect predicted failure cause and proposed next-step correction. As shown in Figure 1 (a), models such as RynnBrain ( Dang et al., 2026b ) , MiMo-Embodied ( Xiaomi MiMo Team, 2026 ) , and InternVL3 ( OpenGVLab, 2025 ) frequently produce plausible descriptions while failing to identify the condition that actually blocks progress, and their plans often fail to repair it. This exposes weak physical causal understanding and failure localization. The missing capability is not merely recognizing failure, but transforming execution evidence into an explanation of why it failed and what must change next . We next ask what form this missing information should take. Keeping the evaluated brain, visual input, action interface, and decoding fixed on EB-ALFRED, we vary only the feedback after failure. As shown in Figure 1 (b), repeating the failed outcome provides little benefit ( Shinn et al., 2023a ) , whereas supplying the concrete failure cause consistently improves performance. For RoboBrain2.5-4B, MiMo-Embodied-7B, and InternVL3-8B, cause-level feedback changes task success from 0.22 to 0.28 , from 0.20 to 0.27 , and from 0.20 to 0.24 . Useful feedback does not simply state failure; it identifies which expected condition was violated and why that prevents progress, revising the brain’s interpretation of the task state. ## 3 Failure Data and Corrective Brain Training
Recent code-based embodied systems improve robot-function invocation, skill composition, and routing to fixed execution backends, while execution-aware frameworks provide trajectory feedback for diagnosis and recovery ( Fu et al., 2026 ; Ding et al., 2026 ; Pan et al., 2026b ) . However, they provide limited supervision for task-level failure understanding: models are rarely trained to judge whether a subtask succeeded, identify the broken observable prerequisite, and decide whether to advance or recover. We therefore construct controlled failure trajectories from successful demonstrations and convert them into supervision for outcome judgment, cause diagnosis, recovery-subtask selection, and fine-grained planning. Because evaluation and planning are difficult to learn jointly, we use an evaluation-first curriculum and finally apply on-policy distillation (OPD) to internalize verified evaluation into the planning-token distribution. 3.1 Dataset and Benchmark Construction Controlled failure trajectories. We begin with successful RoboCasa365 and OmniBehavior trajectories ( Nasiriany et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2024 ) . Subtask boundaries are identified from task-graph transitions and task-relevant state changes, such as reaching a target, grasping an object, or opening a container. At each boundary, we retain the task goal g_{i} , current subtask, executed workflow w_{i} , pre-execution observation o_{i} , and corresponding successful segment. We then clone the simulator state before the subtask, inject one known perturbation, and execute the same subtask again. Each branch changes only one factor, making its failure source identifiable from the intervention.
