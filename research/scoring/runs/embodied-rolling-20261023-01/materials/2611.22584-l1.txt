===ABSTRACT===
Existing agentic manipulation systems treat pretrained vision-language-action (VLA) policies as opaque, language-only primitives: when a policy call fails under unfamiliar instructions or scene perturbations, the agent cannot steer its behavior and is forced into futile retries or costly manual takeover. We present InteractVLA, a framework that turns pretrained flow-based VLAs into geometrically steerable and inspectable tools through a bidirectional geometric interface with vision-language agents. In the forward direction, test-time geometric guidance translates an agent-specified end-effector target into input gradients through the action expert via a differentiable command-to-pose mapping, while soft-time target alignment treats arrival time as a latent execution index to match the target pose without distorting the policy's approach motion. In the reverse direction, action-informed agentic refinement exposes generated action chunks and calibrated camera reprojections back to the agent, allowing it to diagnose spatial discrepancies, revise targets for subsequent VLA calls, or locally adjust motion segments on top of the guided trajectory. Making the VLA controllable not only boosts online agentic tool use, but also unlocks effective recursive self-improvement (RSI), achieving state-of-the-art performance of 99.4\% on LIBERO-Plus, 48.62\% on RoboDojo, and 89.4\% on RoboCasa365. ===INTRO===

Combining vision-language agents with pretrained vision-language-action (VLA) policies promises an appealing division of labor for robotic manipulation: the agent handles long-horizon reasoning and task decomposition, while the VLA provides temporally coherent motor execution [ 40 , 2 ] . Yet in practice, existing VLAs hardly function as true interactive tools. Because standard VLAs only accept high-level language instructions and operate as opaque black boxes, the agent cannot steer how the policy executes a skill [ 42 , 18 ] . When a VLA call fails, simply commanding a retry is largely futile because it produces nearly identical motions under similar observations, whereas rephrasing the language instruction often pushes the VLA out of distribution and sharply degrades success (Figure 1 , middle). Across scenarios requiring generalization, the agent cannot rely on the VLA and must take over low-level control entirely, acting as a fallback supervisor that cleans up after an uncontrollable policy rather than a planner wielding a reliable tool [ 41 , 26 , 8 , 34 ] . This limitation stems from an inadequate interface between high-level reasoning and low-level control. Even when unfamiliar instructions or scene layouts prevent a VLA from grounding the right target, its pretrained spatial perception and motor priors, such as smooth approach trajectories, coordinated rotations, and grasp timing, remain highly capable [ 1 , 16 ] . As shown in Figure 1 (right), explicitly steering the end effector toward the intended interaction target allows the exact same policy to redirect its manipulation behavior and complete the task while preserving execution precision. Turning a VLA into a genuinely usable tool therefore requires moving beyond language-only invocation to a bidirectional geometric interface : the agent must be able to specify explicit spatial targets to guide the VLA’s action generation, and conversely inspect the resulting motion proposals to refine subsequent execution [ 25 , 4 , 33 ] . Building such a bidirectional interface at test time poses two coupled challenges. In the forward direction (Agent \rightarrow VLA) , the agent supplies a sparse target configuration to approach rather than a dense trajectory or exact arrival time. While the VLA should synthesize the intervening motion, naively applying a fixed-time penalty pulls the wrong execution step toward the target, whereas penalizing every step uniformly collapses the approach trajectory altogether. In the reverse direction (VLA \rightarrow Agent) , a binary success or failure signal is insufficient for recovery. Once guided by an agent-specified target, the VLA’s generated action chunk already reflects the agent’s spatial intent. By comparing the executed prefix and unexecuted continuation against visual observations, the agent can determine whether a failure requires calling the VLA again with a revised target or merely applying a lightweight local adjustment to the policy’s well-structured trajectory, making recovery far more tractable than full manual takeover. To address these challenges, we introduce InteractVLA , a framework that turns pretrained flow-based VLAs into geometrically steerable and inspectable tools for agentic manipulation (Figure 2 ). Drawing on VLM-based policy steering [ 20 ] and visual geometric constraints [ 13 , 12 ] , InteractVLA bridges agent reasoning and action generation through three components. First, test-time geometric guidance uses a differentiable command-to-pose mapping to translate an agent-specified end-effector target (position, orientation, and gripper state) into input gradients through the action expert within a single denoising trajectory, reshaping motion toward the agent’s intent while returning control early if low confidence indicates an incompatible target. Second, soft-time target alignment formulates arrival time as a latent execution index, jointly matching complete target poses at a shared arrival step that emerges naturally as the trajectory takes shape. Third, action-informed agentic refinement exposes generated action chunks alongside calibrated camera reprojections and execution history to the agent, enabling it to diagnose spatial discrepancies, revise subsequent targets, or locally edit motion segments. Because InteractVLA makes the VLA steerable rather than rigidly repeating a single action mode, it also provides a strong foundation for recursive self-improvement (RSI) : across attempts, the agent iteratively sharpens how it specifies geometric targets and refines policy-generated motions. Across LIBERO-Plus [ 9 ] , RoboDojo [ 6 ] , RoboCasa365 [ 23 ] , and real-world manipulation, InteractVLA substantially outperforms standalone VLAs, language-only agent-VLA baselines, while reducing token consumption by over an order of magnitude compared with step-by-step agent-only control. ## 2 Method
InteractVLA connects a vision-language agent and a pretrained flow-based VLA at test time through explicit geometric targets and motion feedback (Figure 3 ). Given observation o_{k} and instruction \ell , the policy generates an action chunk A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times D} guided toward an agent-specified target G_{k}=(p^{*},R^{*},w^{*}) comprising end-effector position, orientation, and gripper state. The agent then inspects the generated motion proposal to refine subsequent decisions. 2.1 Test-Time Geometric Guidance To steer a pretrained VLA toward the agent’s geometric intent, we differentiate a pose objective through its action expert during denoising. Let v_{\theta}(x_{t},t;c_{k}) denote the flow velocity field conditioned on c_{k}=(o_{k},\ell) , with generative time t\in[0,1] running from noise to clean actions. From the one-step clean-action estimate m_{t}=x_{t}+(1-t)v_{\theta}(x_{t},t;c_{k}), (1) a differentiable forward mapping \mathcal{C}_{o_{k}} converts native policy actions into command poses. At each execution step h , the complete-pose error E_{h} aggregates normalized position, rotation, and gripper discrepancies relative to G_{k} (summed across effectors at a shared index for bimanual control), directly linking the spatial target to native action commands. Given a temporal objective \mathcal{L}_{t} over step-wise pose errors, we steer the denoising trajectory via \displaystyle g_{t} \displaystyle=\nabla_{x_{t}}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\mathcal{C}_{o_{k}}(m_{t}),G_{k}), (2) \displaystyle x_{t+\Delta t} \displaystyle=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[x_{t}+\Delta t\bigl(v_{\theta}-\mathcal{B}_{t}(g_{t})\bigr)\right], (3) where \mathcal{B}_{t} scales the gradient, caps its magnitude relative to the native velocity v_{\theta} , and restricts guidance to an interior active window so endpoints retain the native flow field. The stop-gradient operator \operatorname{sg}[\cdot] detaches successive integration states so each update differentiates solely through the current expert, whose input Jacobian couples corrections across the chunk. To prevent incompatible targets from distorting generation, we monitor a target compatibility confidence C_{t} comparing the minimum pose error r_{t}=\min_{h}E_{h} and relative correction demand d_{t} against tolerances r_{0} and d_{0} : C_{t}=\exp\!\left[-\max\!\left(\frac{r_{t}}{r_{0}},\frac{d_{t}}{d_{0}}\right)\right]. (4) If C_{t}<e^{-1} after the first guided step, generation terminates early and returns control to the agent for replanning. 2.2 Soft-Time Target Alignment While the agent specifies a desired interaction pose, its appropriate arrival step within the horizon H depends on task progress and the policy’s speed. Imposing a fixed-step penalty forces an arbitrary timing assumption, whereas penalizing all steps uniformly collapses the approach motion toward the target. We instead treat arrival time as a latent execution index and softly align the target to the trajectory (Figure 4 ). Let \tau=1-t and \beta_{t}=t^{2}/\tau^{2} . We aggregate the step-wise pose errors via a log-sum-exp objective: \mathcal{L}_{\rm soft}(t)=-\tau^{2}\log\!\left(\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\exp[-\beta_{t}E_{h}]\right). (5) Taking the gradient yields a temporal assignment q_{h}(t)=\operatorname{softmax}_{h}(-\beta_{t}E_{h}) that distributes guidance across compatible arrival steps: \mathcal{L}_{\rm guide}(t)=\frac{\tau}{t}\mathcal{L}_{\rm soft}(t),\quad g_{t}=t\tau\sum_{h}q_{h}(t)\nabla_{x_{t}}E_{h}. (6) Because position, orientation, and gripper discrepancies enter E_{h} jointly, they share the same temporal weight q_{h}(t) , ensuring the policy matches a complete interaction pose at a unified arrival step. As flow time t advances and \beta_{t} increases, q_{h}(t) sharpens around the most compatible step while the multiplier t\tau modulates overall guidance amplitude.
