===ABSTRACT===
Generalist robot policies execute atomic skills reliably but fail when the same skills are composed into long-horizon tasks. Self-improvement from the policy's own experience does not close this gap: experience collected by the policy alone stays within its own distribution and reproduces what it can already do. We show that what the policy lacks is not a skill but the next step. We present CoachSI, a self-improvement method in which a frozen, off-the-shelf vision-language model (VLM) coaches the policy in language: it watches the robot and names the next step in the format of the policy's atomic instructions, and never outputs an action. Successful coached rollouts are relabeled in hindsight with the original task instruction and used to fine-tune the policy. The fine-tuned policy is then deployed on its own. On states where a World Action Model and a Vision-Language-Action model have stalled, naming the next step raises subgoal completion by 4.6x over the unchanged task instruction, and the frozen VLM supplies it from the same camera images the policy sees. After fine-tuning, unassisted success on long-horizon tasks in unseen scenes rises from 47.4% to 59.3%, 9.8 points above fine-tuning on human demonstrations and 7.3 points above fine-tuning on self-collected successes at an equal data budget. With a coach that sees only camera images, it reaches 56.8%, above both baselines. The gain is obtained with the action backbone frozen: language coaching teaches the policy what should happen next, and the skills it already has do the rest.

===INTRO===

Humans become skilled in the physical world not by being shown every motion, but by practice: they act, observe the outcome, and adjust. Repeated over many attempts, this loop turns a clumsy first try into a reliable skill, and it continues long after instruction ends. Generalist robot policies now perform a wide range of manipulation tasks [ 9 , 8 , 42 , 41 , 24 , 6 , 5 , 40 ] , yet they rarely improve this way: when a policy fails, making it better typically means collecting new human demonstrations. Enabling a robot to improve from its own experience is the goal of self-improvement in physical AI [ 35 , 7 ] , and it would scale robot learning beyond what human teleoperation can supply. The most direct form of self-improvement changes the policy itself: the robot acts, keeps its successful rollouts, and is fine-tuned on them. This recipe has two limitations. First, a policy acting alone stays within its own distribution, so its successes show it only what it can already do. Second, on the long-horizon tasks where improvement matters most, the policy fails often and has few successes to collect. Experience improves a policy when it contains successes the policy cannot yet produce, and self-collected experience contains few of them. Existing methods obtain such successes with supervision from outside the policy. Interactive imitation learning brings in a human operator who takes over when the robot errs and supplies corrective actions [ 23 , 2 ] . Every correction costs operator time, so the supervision scales with human effort, the cost that self-improvement is meant to remove. Reinforcement learning replaces the operator with reward and value models that score the robot’s own attempts [ 45 , 10 ] . These models must themselves be trained before they can supervise the policy. Hierarchical systems place a vision-language model above the policy to decompose the task into steps [ 1 , 47 ] . The policy itself is left unchanged, and the system depends on the high-level model at every deployment. To understand what keeps a policy from producing these successes on its own, we analyze rollouts of generalist policies on long-horizon tasks. We find that the missing successes are closer than they appear. The policies execute atomic skills reliably, yet fail when the same skills are composed into a long-horizon task [ 37 , 29 , 16 , 48 ] , and the failures take a specific form: partway through the task the policy stalls, and a stalled policy almost never recovers on its own. In a recurring case, the robot is already at the right object and holds the required skill, yet does not act. What the policy lacks in that moment is not a skill but the next step. What it needs is therefore far less than corrective actions or rewards: a single instruction. We introduce CoachSI ( Fig. 1 ), a self-improvement method guided by language alone. Its central component is a language coach: a frozen, off-the-shelf vision-language model (VLM) that watches the robot and replaces the task instruction with the next step, phrased in the format of the policy’s atomic instructions. The coach never outputs an action; every motion is produced by the policy’s own skills. Successful coached rollouts are relabeled in hindsight with the original task instruction [ 3 ] and used to fine-tune the policy, which is then deployed on its own. On a World Action Model, fine-tuning updates only the video backbone and keeps the action backbone frozen. CoachSI needs no human operator, no reward model, and no VLM at test time. We evaluate CoachSI on RoboCasa365 [ 39 ] with a World Action Model (WAM) [ 49 ] and a Vision-Language-Action model (VLA) [ 55 ] . On states where the two policies have stalled, naming the next step raises subgoal completion by 4.6\times over the unchanged task instruction, and a VLM coach that sees only the policy’s camera images delivers 91% of the gain of an oracle that always names the correct step. With the coach, the policy completes more than a quarter of the episodes it fails on its own. After fine-tuning the WAM on coached experience, unassisted success on 18 long-horizon tasks in unseen scenes rises from 47.4% to 59.3%, 9.8 points above fine-tuning on human demonstrations and 7.3 points above fine-tuning on self-collected successes at an equal data budget. With a coach that sees only camera images, it reaches 56.8%, above both baselines. The action backbone is frozen throughout, so the entire gain comes from what the policy predicts will happen next: the skills were already there. ## 3 CoachSI
3.1 Preliminaries We consider a language-conditioned policy \pi_{\theta} [ 22 , 9 ] that maps an observation o_{t} and an instruction \ell_{t} to an action chunk [ 63 , 11 ] of length h , a_{t:t+h-1}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid o_{t},\ell_{t}). (1) The policy is trained on atomic tasks, each a single skill such as opening a cabinet, and on composite tasks that chain several skills toward one goal. A composite task is specified by a task instruction g . In unassisted execution, the policy is conditioned on \ell_{t}=g for the whole episode. A World Action Model (WAM) implements \pi_{\theta} with two coupled components. A video backbone predicts future frames from the observation and the instruction, and an action backbone predicts the action chunk while attending to the features of the video backbone. The two are denoised jointly and trained with \mathcal{L}=\lambda_{v}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{video}}+\lambda_{a}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{action}} . A vision-language-action model (VLA) maps observations and language to actions without a video-prediction branch. We fine-tune the WAM, whose separate backbones let us update what the policy predicts next while every action is still produced by the pretrained weights ( Sec. 3.3 ). 3.2 Language Coach Each composite task g has a set of next-step instructions \mathcal{V}(g)=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\} , one per step of the task, written in the format of the policy’s atomic instructions. For the task “Pick the croissant and place it on the pan. Then turn on the stove to warm the croissant.”, \mathcal{V}(g) holds “Pick the croissant from the plate and place it in the pan.” and “Turn on the front right burner of the stove.” Each v_{i} asks for a skill the policy already has. The coach c_{\phi} is a frozen, off-the-shelf VLM. At every action-chunk boundary it receives the task instruction, the candidate set, and the camera views the policy sees, denoted x_{t} , and selects the first step that is not yet done, or returns KEEP when every step is done: \displaystyle z_{t} \displaystyle=c_{\phi}(g,\mathcal{V}(g),x_{t}), (2) \displaystyle\ell_{t} \displaystyle=\begin{cases}g,&z_{t}=\texttt{KEEP},\\ v_{z_{t}},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} (3) The selected instruction conditions the policy for the next action chunk, and the coach is queried again afterwards. The coach never outputs an action: every motion in a coached rollout is produced by \pi_{\theta} . We also use a state-based variant of the coach, which reads a text description of object and robot state in place of the images. 3.3 Hindsight Relabeling and Fine-Tuning We run the coached policy from the initial state on a set of collection scenes and keep the rollouts that reach task success within the task horizon. For each kept rollout \tau , we retain the observations and the executed actions and replace every coaching instruction with the task instruction g [ 3 ] : \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{coach}}=\{(\tau,g):\mathrm{succ}(\tau,g)=1\}. (4) The relabeled data shows the task being completed under g alone. We fine-tune the policy on \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{coach}} mixed with the RoboCasa365 human demonstrations \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{human}} on which it was trained, with a fraction \alpha of the training samples drawn from \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{coach}} . Because the coach outputs no actions, the actions in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{coach}} are the policy’s own and carry no new action knowledge. What is new is the future that unfolds under g , which is a target for video prediction. We therefore update only the self-attention layers of the video backbone, with parameters \theta_{v} , using the video loss, and keep the action backbone frozen: \min_{\theta_{v}}\;(1-\alpha)\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{human}}}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{video}}\big]+\alpha\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{coach}}}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{video}}\big].
