# ThinkLocoManip: A Whole-Body Control Agentic Harness for Legged Loco-Manipulation

## Abstract
In this paper, we propose ThinkLocoManip, an agentic harness for legged loco-manipulation designed to tackle complex, long-horizon tasks in large-scale environments. Existing approaches typically follow either direct action generation or a plan-then-act paradigm, both of which can struggle with the generalization and error-recovery capabilities required for extended task execution. In contrast, ThinkLocoManip adopts an interleaved reasoning-and-acting paradigm, allowing the agent to continuously reason about its current state, execute actions, observe their outcomes, and adapt its strategy accordingly. Building on this paradigm, our agentic harness orchestrates a suite of specialized tools for perception, navigation, manipulation, and locomotion, enabling flexible composition of heterogeneous capabilities. Unlike existing robotic agentic harnesses, ThinkLocoManip explicitly incorporates locomotion as a tool domain for agent reasoning and execution. We further design a set of skills that explicitly account for both tool usage within each functional domain and coordination across domains, supporting effective execution of complex behaviors over long horizons. To tackle task failure during long-horizon interactions, we develop keyframe thinking mechanism to preserve critical execution context and support efficient error recovery. Extensive real-world experiments demonstrate that ThinkLocoManip substantially outperforms representative methods based on both direct action generation and plan-then-act paradigms across challenging long-horizon legged loco-manipulation tasks. Notably, ThinkLocoManip achieves a success rate of 77%, more than twice that of the strongest plan-then-act baseline (37.1%). Our experiments further validate the effectiveness of interleaved reasoning and acting, tool orchestration, and coordinated skill execution.

## I INTRODUCTION
We are entering an era in which intelligent robots are expected to move beyond laboratories and operate autonomously in large-scale real-world environments, such as homes, supermarkets, factories, and hospitals. Performing labor-intensive and repetitive tasks, such as cleaning, organizing goods, and assisting humans, requires more than manipulating objects within a fixed workspace: robots must navigate through the environment, physically interact with it, and coordinate whole-body motion over extended task horizons. This capability, known as loco-manipulation , tightly integrates locomotion and manipulation, enabling physical interactions beyond the reach of stationary robots. Robust and generalizable loco-manipulation is therefore a fundamental capability toward general-purpose embodied intelligence. In this work, we focus on legged loco-manipulation , where legged mobility provides the flexibility and robustness required to traverse complex terrains. Existing learning-based approaches to loco-manipulation broadly follow two paradigms: direct action generation and plan-then-act . Direct action generation typically learns an end-to-end policy through reinforcement learning or imitation learning, mapping observations directly to robot actions. While effective for reactive control, such policies often struggle to generalize to complex, long-horizon tasks in large-scale environments, largely due to the limited diversity of training data or simulation environments. In contrast, plan-then-act methods explicitly construct a high-level plan before execution, providing stronger task-level structure for complex tasks over extended horizons. However, they can be brittle when new observations invalidate the initial plan or when execution errors require recovery. Consequently, both paradigms remain limited in the generalization and error-recovery capabilities required for robust execution of complex, long-horizon tasks in large-scale environments. Toward this end, we propose ThinkLocoManip , an agentic harness for legged loco-manipulation designed to tackle complex, long-horizon tasks in large-scale environments. In contrast to the two paradigms above, ThinkLocoManip adopts an interleaved reasoning-and-acting paradigm, allowing the robot to continuously reason about its current state, invoke appropriate tools, observe execution outcomes, and adapt its strategy accordingly, as seen in Fig. 2 . This paradigm enables stronger generalization across diverse environments and robust recovery from execution errors. To support such adaptive execution, we organize the toolset into four functional domains: perception, navigation, manipulation, and locomotion . Unlike existing robotic agentic harnesses that primarily orchestrate perception, navigation, and manipulation capabilities, ThinkLocoManip further treats locomotion as a first-class tool domain, allowing the agent to explicitly reason about and invoke locomotion capabilities as part of task execution. Each domain comprises specialized tools that can be selected according to the task requirements and environmental conditions. For example, the robot can employ map-based navigation when a prior map is available, while target-based navigation allows it to actively approach and observe a target when such prior knowledge is unavailable. Building on this modular organization, we further design a set of skills that govern both tool usage within each domain and coordination across domains. In total, ThinkLocoManip integrates 11 tools and 5 skills, enabling flexible and coordinated execution of complex behaviors over long task horizons. We also develop Keyframe Thinking error-recovery mechanism that enables the agent to identify execution failures and adapt its behavior, thereby improving robustness over long-horizon task execution. Extensive real-world experiments demonstrate that ThinkLocoManip substantially outperforms representative methods based on both direct action generation and plan-then-act paradigms across challenging long-horizon legged loco-manipulation tasks. In particular, ThinkLocoManip achieves an overall success rate of 77% , more than twice that of the strongest plan-then-act baseline at 37.1% , corresponding to an absolute improvement of nearly 40 percentage points . Further analysis validates the effectiveness of interleaved reasoning and acting, tool orchestration, and coordinated skill execution, while the accompanying video demonstrates the versatility of ThinkLocoManip across diverse real-world tasks and environments.

## III METHOD
ThinkLocoManip comprises three hierarchical components for tackling long-horizon legged loco-manipulation: primitive tools , ThinkLocoManip-Skill , and Keyframe Thinking . We first provide a formal definition of the legged long-horizon loco-manipulation task in Sec. III-A. In Sec. III-B, we introduce a suite of primitive tools specialized for long-horizon loco-manipulation, covering perception, navigation, manipulation, and locomotion . Sec. III-C presents ThinkLocoManip-Skill , which defines both the workflow for composing primitive tools within each skill and the behavioral constraints for coordinating different skills. Finally, in Sec. III-D, we introduce Keyframe Thinking , an error-recovery mechanism that enables the agent to identify execution failures and adapt its behavior, thereby improving robustness over long-horizon task execution. III-A Task Definition Given a natural-language task instruction \ell , long-horizon legged loco-manipulation requires an agent to autonomously coordinate locomotion and manipulation over an extended horizon to accomplish the specified task. We formulate this process as closed-loop decision making, where the agent produces an action decision \mathbf{u}_{t} conditioned on the task instruction and its execution history: \mathbf{u}_{t}=\pi(\ell,\mathbf{o}_{1:t},\mathbf{u}_{1:t-1}), (1) where \mathbf{o}_{t} denotes the observation at time step t , and \mathbf{u}_{t} denotes the agent-level decision that ultimately determines the locomotion and manipulator behaviors. As illustrated in Fig. 3 , the agent first decomposes the task instruction into an ordered sequence of sub-tasks. To complete each sub-task, the agent first navigates the robot to the corresponding task location and then executes the required loco-manipulation behavior by coordinating the robot’s body posture and manipulator end-effector pose. Since long-horizon execution can be disrupted by external interference or execution errors, the agent must continuously incorporate new observations, identify task failures, and replan from the current state to continue execution.
