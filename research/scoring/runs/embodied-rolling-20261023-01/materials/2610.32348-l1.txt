# LogLook: Execution-Grounded Memory for Frontier-Model Robot Planners

## Abstract
Many manipulation tasks require a robot to remember what it has done and what it has seen. Frontier vision-language models can plan such tasks without task-specific training, but how such a planner should remember an episode is an open question. We argue that its memory must keep track of what the planner has done and learned, and must let the planner choose what to look at, since every frame costs tokens; a note rewritten at every step meets both poorly. We propose LogLook, whose execution-grounded memory lets the system keep the record, an execution log and the model's earlier notes, while the model decides where to look: frames are returned only where it expects a change, and demonstrations are skimmed before it inspects the parts it chooses. Measured with the benchmark's motion planner, LogLook matches the success of a note-only memory with 43% fewer tokens and less than half the images, and fails far fewer episodes through what it remembered or saw. With direct control, LogLook solves 93.5% of the RoboMME test set.

## 1 Introduction
Many everyday manipulation tasks cannot be solved from the current camera image alone. A robot asked to place a cube on a target five times must know how many placements it has already made. A robot asked to lift the container that hides the green cube must have followed the containers while they were shuffled, and one asked to move a cube “in the same manner as before” must have noticed whether the demonstrator pushed or carried it. RoboMME [ 1 ] collects sixteen such tasks, and its evaluation shows that memory is the bottleneck for today’s robot policies: the best learned memory reaches about half the success of a \pi_{0.5} [ 2 ] executor driven by oracle subgoals. Frontier vision-language models now perceive and reason well enough to act as robot planners without task-specific training [ 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] , and on RoboMME such a planner already succeeds more often than learned memory policies ( Table 5 ). How the planner should remember an episode, however, is still open. The usual choices trade reliability against cost: a note that the model rewrites at every step, the scratchpad memory of language agents [ 7 ] , is cheap but has to carry everything the planner will need later, while showing the model every frame keeps all the evidence and makes each decision more expensive as the episode goes on. The question we ask is what a frontier planner needs to remember, and who should keep it. We argue that this memory has to meet two requirements. First, it must keep track of what the planner has done and learned, so that a placement that has just been completed is counted and a colour seen before the containers covered the cubes is still known later; a note that is rewritten at every step can lose either in a single rewrite ( Figure 1 a), and continuously rewritten memories are known to degrade [ 8 ] . Second, it must let the planner choose what to look at: frames recorded during an action rarely change but cost tokens, while some facts, such as a container swap or whether a demonstrator pushed or carried a cube, are visible only for a moment. Both requirements suggest a division of labour in which the system keeps the record and the model decides where to look, and our execution-grounded memory ( Section 3 , Figure 2 ) is built this way. The system writes a log of every executed action with the gripper state measured right after it, and keeps every note the model has written. The model, in turn, names the image regions where it expects something to change during an action, and only frames that change there are returned; it skims each demonstration and inspects the parts it chooses. The design is the same for every task and involves no training. Measuring what the memory contributes turned out to need an executor whose own failures are rare and can be told apart from the planner’s. With the learned \pi_{0.5} executor, swapping only the memory leaves the success rate unchanged, because empty grasps, stalls and late button presses decide almost all episodes in which the two memories disagree. With the benchmark’s motion planner, whose execution fails far less often, our memory reaches the success of a note-only memory while sending the model 43% fewer tokens and less than half as many images. Separating the failures shows why the success rates end up close: the note-only memory fails three times as many episodes for reasons other than motion planning (12 against 4), and at least 11 of its 12, against one of our 4, come from what the planner remembered or saw; motion-planning failures, which the benchmark counts as failed episodes, strike both memories at random and decide most of the remaining episodes. With direct control our planner solves 93.5% of the RoboMME test set, and over the learned executor it comes close to oracle subgoals while using a quarter of the tokens of a planner that re-plans after every action chunk. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We identify two requirements for the memory of a frontier-model robot planner, keeping track of what was done and learned and choosing what to look at, and show in a per-episode analysis that a rewritten note fails on both. • We propose an execution-grounded memory in which the system keeps a record of what was executed and measured while the model decides which frames and which parts of a demonstration to look at. • We show that a learned executor hides differences between memories, and that with the benchmark’s motion planner our memory matches the success of a note-only memory at well under half the image cost, with far fewer failures caused by memory and observation.

## 3 Method
We first state the problem and the two requirements on the memory ( Sections 3.1 and 3.2 ); the remaining subsections describe our memory. 3.1 Problem formulation We consider a frozen vision-language model that plans an episode through a sequence of decisions. At each decision it receives the current observation (front and wrist images, end-effector pose, gripper opening and environment step), the task instruction, a task note and a memory, and returns a free-text note and an action, which an executor carries out. Everything the planner knows about earlier parts of the episode, including the demonstration, reaches it only through the memory. The question we study is what this memory should contain and who should write it, so that the planner succeeds while being shown as few tokens and images as possible. 3.2 Two requirements on the memory The most common memory for a language-model agent is a note that the model rewrites at every step [ 7 ] . In a robot episode this note has to do two jobs. Keeping track of what was done. If the note is the only record of the episode, every rewrite can change it: a repetition that has just been completed may not be counted, and a fact recorded earlier, such as which container covers which cube, may disappear a few rewrites later. Continuously rewritten memories are known to degrade in this way [ 8 ] . The record of what was executed and what was learned should therefore not depend on every rewrite getting it right. Choosing what to look at. Some facts are visible only for a moment, during an action or in a demonstration, yet every image the planner is shown costs tokens. Showing every frame recorded during an action, or a whole demonstration at a fixed stride, is expensive although in most tasks nothing moves unless the robot moves it, and a fixed stride can still miss the moment that matters, such as whether a demonstrator pushed or carried a cube. Showing none would hide container swaps and moving cubes. The planner can predict where and when something is likely to change, so we let it decide what it is shown. 3.3 Execution-grounded memory The memory consists of an execution log and the history of earlier notes, both kept by the system, and of frames and demonstration excerpts that the model asks to see.
