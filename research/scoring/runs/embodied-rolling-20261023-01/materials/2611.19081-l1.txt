# RoboCoEvo: Expanding Robot Capabilities through Agent-Policy Co-Evolution

## Abstract
Integrating MLLM-based agents with robotic foundation models has attracted increasing attention as a means of enhancing robotic capabilities. However, the improvement of such integrated systems remains largely decoupled across their agent and model components, with advances in one component not necessarily benefiting the other, substantially limiting the capabilities of the combined system. As both MLLMs and robotic foundation models become increasingly capable, fully realizing their combined potential calls for co-evolution, an iterative process in which improvements in agents and robotic foundation models reinforce one another. We propose RoboCoEvo, a failure-guided co-evolution framework that couples the refinement of the agent's explicit and reusable executable knowledge with parameter learning in robotic foundation models, such as vision-language-action (VLA) models. Through a code-as-policy formulation, the agent develops and refines executable failure-detection and recovery programs, generating targeted corrective experience. Post-training consolidates the resulting corrective behaviors into the robotic model's end-to-end policy, improving autonomous task execution. Changes in the updated policy's failure distribution, in turn, guide subsequent revisions to the agent's programs. Experiments on BEHAVIOR-1K, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin demonstrate sustained improvements in autonomous VLA task performance across successive co-evolution rounds.

## 1 Introduction
Recent work has shown that MLLM-based agentic harnesses can enhance the capabilities of robotic foundation models ( Huang et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026b ) . In these works, robotic foundation models provide visuomotor skills acquired from large-scale demonstrations ( Kim et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2024 ; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025b ) , while agents support their policy execution through failure diagnosis, recovery, and coordination ( Liu et al., 2023 ; Lin et al., 2025 ) . This complementary division of roles enables the combined system to address limitations of the robotic foundation models. However, the improvement processes of the agent and the robotic foundation model in the combined system typically remain separate or only loosely coupled ( Zhang et al., 2026a ; Yu et al., 2026a ) , with advances in one component not necessarily benefiting the other. Without learning from corrective experience, the policy may remain dependent on agent assistance in similar situations. Meanwhile, an agent whose knowledge is not refined as the policy is updated may continue to target weaknesses that no longer constrain performance. Sustained improvement therefore requires a co-evolution mechanism that connects policy learning with the adaptation of agent assistance. For corrective interventions to yield lasting policy improvements, the policy must incorporate the demonstrated behaviors into its own capabilities. Agent interventions provide training experience at states where execution fails, including states induced by accumulated errors and underrepresented in the training distribution ( Ross et al., 2011 ) . Interactive imitation learning obtains such corrections from human supervisors ( Kelly et al., 2018 ; Liu et al., 2022 ) , while recent approaches automate recovery and generate corrective data for policy training ( Zhang et al., 2026c ; Liao et al., 2026 ; Lang et al., 2026 ) . Training on this experience allows the policy to internalize behaviors previously supplied by the agent. As these failures are resolved, however, the policy’s need for assistance changes, and further improvement requires corrective experience tailored to its remaining limitations. On the other hand, acquiring this experience requires the agent to adapt alongside the policy. It must reassess the updated policy’s failures, determine where intervention is still needed, and revise its recovery strategies accordingly. Automated failure analysis can support this reassessment ( Duan et al., 2025 ; Yu et al., 2026b ) , while a coding agent can use execution feedback to refine the corresponding detectors and recovery programs. These revisions improve the agent’s assistance and provide relevant corrections for the next policy update. However, applying conventional code-as-policy approaches without accounting for task-specific failure characteristics may compromise the accuracy of failure diagnosis and the efficiency of recovery, thereby limiting subsequent policy improvement. The two directions of improvement therefore form a continuous feedback loop: the robotic foundation model learns from the agent’s interventions, and its remaining failures guide what the agent must diagnose and resolve next. We present RoboCoEvo (Figure 1 ), a co-evolution framework that couples Policy-Guided Agent Evolution with Agent-Guided Policy Capability Expansion . The agent evolves through revisions to executable failure-detection and recovery programs. Guided by policy rollouts and execution feedback, it identifies recurring failure modes, develops and evaluates recovery strategies, and generates corrective demonstrations across varied task configurations using a code-as-policy formulation ( Liang et al., 2022 ) . The robotic foundation model then incorporates these corrective behaviors into its end-to-end policy through post-training. Subsequent evaluation of the updated model identifies residual capability gaps and directs the next round of program refinement. This iterative exchange improves the agent’s diagnosis and recovery strategies while expanding the policy’s capacity for independent execution. We instantiate and evaluate RoboCoEvo with vision-language-action (VLA) models on BEHAVIOR-1K, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin. Experiments demonstrate sustained improvement across successive cycles, with higher autonomous task success and stronger generalization to unseen configurations. In summary, our contributions are: 1. ## 3 RoboCoEvo: Failure-Guided Agent–Policy Co-Evolution
3.1 Problem Setup and Overview As shown in Figure 2 , RoboCoEvo formulates the co-evolution of an agent and a policy as an iterative process. The base policy \pi_{0} is a robotic foundation model trained on reference demonstrations \mathcal{D}_{0} . In round i , Policy-Guided Agent Evolution (Section 3.2 ) collects rollouts of the current policy \pi_{i} in a set of scenarios \mathcal{S} . An MLLM-based agent groups the recurring failures into failure modes (FMs) and writes or revises a detector d_{m} and a recovery program \rho_{m} for each FM. Agent-Guided Policy Capability Expansion (Section 3.3 ) then applies these programs to the detected failure states and to perturbed copies of them, which yields the corrective experience R_{i}=f(\pi_{i},\mathcal{S}). The policy is then post-trained on \mathcal{D}_{0} and the corrective experience accumulated up to round i to obtain \pi_{i+1} , whose rollouts are used in the next round. RoboCoEvo observes the scene primarily through a scene state s_{t}=(q_{t},\{p^{k}_{t}\}_{k\in\mathcal{O}},\mathcal{F}_{t},I_{t}) , where I_{t} denotes the camera images. Here, q_{t} is the robot state, including the base pose and joint configuration, \mathcal{O} is the set of task-relevant objects, and p^{k}_{t} is the pose of object k . The symbolic state \mathcal{F}_{t} is the set of facts that hold at step t , such as \mathrm{open}(k) , \mathrm{on}(k,k^{\prime}) , and \mathrm{held}(k) . The agent derives and reconstructs these facts from the simulator state. 3.2 Policy-Guided Agent Evolution In each round, the agent adapts its detectors and recovery programs to the failures of the current policy \pi_{i} . If the agent instead diagnosed and recovered each failed rollout separately, its cost would grow with the number of failures, and independent recoveries of similar failures could follow different strategies, leading to inconsistent demonstrations. RoboCoEvo therefore groups recurring failures into FMs and writes one detector and one recovery program per FM. The cost of diagnosis and program writing is shared by all failures of an FM, and all recoveries of an FM follow the same strategy (Section 4.3 ). Failure mode identification.
