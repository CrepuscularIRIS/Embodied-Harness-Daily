# ESI: An Embodied Self-Improvement Flywheel for LLM-VLA Agents

## Abstract
Independently pretrained language models and vision-language-action (VLA) policies provide complementary building blocks for embodied agents, but connecting them does not automatically produce coordinated behavior. The VLA may generate actions that diverge from the LLM's intended subtask and, upon regaining control after a correction, undo the promising state restored by the LLM. We introduce ESI (Embodied Self-Improvement), an automated flywheel that uses environmental interaction to align reasoning and action. ESI treats the modular agent as the unit of post-training, repeatedly refining the prompts that guide the LLM, the shared skill interface that expresses its intent, and the VLA parameters that realize that intent, then redeploying these components together. Our key insight is that collaboration itself can supply supervision for improving this interface. The LLM's subtask decisions provide semantic supervision for what the policy should execute, while its effective corrections and subsequent student continuations provide action-level supervision for recovery and handoff. The student also learns from its own effective execution, allowing locally useful experience to contribute even when the overall episode fails. On ten selected RoboDojo tasks, ESI achieves a mean Score of 62.03 and a mean success rate of 49.41% using an LLM-VLA agent without generating task-specific control code. On both metrics, it outperforms the published reference results of all hybrid embodied agents, including the Agent+WAM system GO-3 Nano Preview (47.27 Score and 36.60% success), as well as all compared code-based methods.

## 1 Introduction
Robotics is entering a stage in which strong general-purpose reasoning models can participate directly in the control loop. They can interpret unfamiliar instructions, identify relevant objects, reason about intermediate goals, and revise a plan after observing a failure. At the same time, VLA policies provide a powerful substrate for generating continuous robot actions from images, language, and proprioception [ 3 , 2 ] . Agentic systems that combine these capabilities are a compelling route toward more general robots: a reasoning model decides what should happen next, and an action model provides the motor behavior needed to make it happen [ 9 , 19 , 7 , 6 ] . The central challenge is making this division of labor effective. Both components may be capable in isolation, yet their composition can fail at the interface. A task-finetuned VLA may recognize a familiar scene and reproduce a familiar motion while underusing the teacher’s more specific instruction. Related work identifies visual shortcuts that allow scene information to dominate language constraints [ 11 ] . A teacher may correctly diagnose a bad approach, adjust the gripper, and restore a promising configuration, only for the student to undo that correction when control returns. These failures expose two closely related gaps: an intent gap between the teacher’s decision and the student’s behavior, and a handoff gap between the state produced by an intervention and the student’s ability to continue. Consider an illustrative packing task. The teacher selects the red object and asks the left arm to grasp it. The student instead approaches another object, or closes its gripper before reaching a suitable contact configuration. The teacher corrects the approach and gives control back. If the student has never learned to act from this corrected state under the intended subtask, it may repeat the original motion or abandon the target. The immediate problem can be solved with more teacher control, but the same coordination failure may recur in the next episode. A system that only intervenes repeatedly spends reasoning effort without necessarily improving its underlying action capability. Our premise is that these interactions are valuable learning data. They identify where the deployed policy diverges from a selected goal, show how that divergence can be corrected, and expose the states from which execution must resume. We therefore treat the reasoning–action interface as a target of collaborative post-training . The teacher remains an online planner, reviewer, and source of corrections. Their joint experience updates the teacher’s guidance, the shared skill vocabulary, and the student policy so that future cooperation becomes more effective. This creates a path from episodic assistance to persistent capability. This motivates a shift from model-level to system-level post-training . In a modular embodied agent, decision guidance, the language interface, and motor behavior jointly determine what the system can accomplish. ESI makes their cooperation a learning target: interaction supplies evidence for updating each component, and their joint deployment determines the experience collected next. Through this process, the LLM’s scene understanding and corrective judgments become supervision for persistent action capability. Web-to-robot knowledge transfer [ 22 ] and language-guided policy distillation [ 8 ] illustrate how foundation models can support embodied learning. ESI provides a pathway for converting advances in general-purpose foundation models into executable embodied capabilities. This pathway has two stages. During deployment, the LLM contributes scene understanding, planning, and corrective decisions to the current agent. Through the flywheel, effective interaction becomes supervision that updates the action policy and guidance for subsequent rounds. A foundation model thus participates both in performing tasks and in producing the experience from which the embodied system learns. We instantiate this idea in ESI (Embodied Self-Improvement), a configure-once flywheel built on the open-source GPT-as-Policy system [ 6 ] (Figure 1 ). During collection, the teacher selects a structured subtask, the VLA proposes an action chunk, and the teacher accepts useful motion or applies a correction. The resulting observations, instructions, reasoning, executed actions, and feedback support round-level updates of decision guidance, the shared skill catalog, and policy parameters. These components are deployed together in the next round, while the LLM’s weights remain fixed. The link from interaction to policy improvement is the selection of locally effective behavior. ESI retains successful subtasks from unsuccessful episodes and connects teacher corrections with valid student continuations on the executed timeline. ## 3 ESI: System-Level Post-Training for Embodied Agents
3.1 Three coupled adaptation targets ESI treats the deployed agent as a composition \mathcal{A}_{r}=(\mathcal{T};P_{r},S_{r},\theta_{r}) at collection round r . Here \mathcal{T} is a fixed pretrained language model, P_{r} contains its system and task guidance, S_{r} defines the shared skill catalog and argument schema, and \theta_{r} contains the VLA parameters. Environmental interaction yields an experience buffer \mathcal{B}_{r} . The flywheel uses this shared evidence to update the prompt, skill catalog, and policy for the next collection round. These objects address different sources of coordination failure. Prompt evolution changes how the teacher plans, reviews action, and responds to difficulty. Skill evolution turns recurring operations into reusable descriptions that make the teacher’s intent explicit. Policy learning changes how the robot realizes that intent from the states encountered during collaboration. The skill catalog defines the shared language used by a single VLA policy; each entry describes an operation rather than a separately trained controller. The coupling is carried by subsequent interaction: changes in guidance and skill descriptions alter the operations collected, while policy updates change which operations still require teacher intervention. 3.2 Learning the interface between reasoning and action Let g denote the episode-level instruction, o_{t} the available observation including images and robot state, and h_{t} the interaction history. The two models communicate through language, action proposals, and environmental feedback.
