# Correct Robots Before They Make Mistakes: Proactive Human-in-the-Loop Intervention via Preference Learning

## Abstract
This paper presents Proactive Human-in-the-Loop (PHIL), a framework for proactive policy correction and preference-based post-training. PHIL enables operators to preview policy-predicted future motion and intervene before anticipated undesirable actions. During takeover, PHIL continues querying the policy and displaying its predicted trajectories in the physical workspace through an extended reality (XR) interface. PHIL pairs rejected policy predictions with corrective action chunks subsequently executed from the same physical state over the same horizon, and preference-based post-training encourages the policy to favor these corrections. This paper evaluates PHIL on four real-world manipulation tasks using a diffusion policy and a pretrained vision-language-action policy. With five correction episodes per condition, PHIL increases mean success rates across the evaluated tasks and demonstration budgets from 44.0% to 70.3% for the diffusion policy and from 57.8% to 76.9% for X-VLA, compared with HG-DAgger. Separately, policies trained with proactive corrections show fewer unnecessary recovery behaviors in successful episodes. Project page: https://intuitive-robots.github.io/PHIL-page/.

## I Introduction
Robot policies, including visuomotor policies and vision-language-action (VLA) models, have shown strong performance on a broad range of manipulation tasks. However, policies trained from offline demonstrations may still encounter states that are beyond the coverage of their training data. In such situations, the policy may produce undesirable motions or fail to complete the task. Improving performance in such cases requires additional task-specific data. Human-in-the-loop imitation learning addresses this gap by collecting corrective data during policy rollouts. During these rollouts, the policy may visit states that are absent from, or underrepresented in the offline demonstrations. Most existing human-in-the-loop methods collect correction data reactively. In reactive correction, the operator decides whether to intervene based on the robot’s currently observed execution, without a preview of the policy’s predicted future motion. It is straightforward to deploy and allows corrective data to be collected during policy rollouts. The resulting recovery actions can also be valuable when the robot must recover from an error and retry a task [ 11 ] . This approach has enabled effective policy refinement through interactive data aggregation, remote teleoperation, and corrective residual control [ 14 , 17 , 23 ] . When reactive corrections begin after an error has occurred, they include recovery actions in addition to actions that advance the task. The proportion of reactive correction data in the dataset can affect how often recovery motions appear in the policy output. A higher proportion increases the likelihood of producing recovery motions even when no recovery is needed. These limitations motivate proactive correction, in which the operator reviews the policy’s proposed motion and provides corrections before an anticipated error occurs. By visualizing the policy’s predicted future motion, proactive correction enables the operator to identify and correct potential errors before the robot enters safety-critical states. This predictive preview reduces the operator’s cognitive burden of continuously anticipating the policy’s behavior. It can also reduce the need for repeated reactive corrections as an error unfolds. Although proactive correction shows considerable promise, it has received limited attention. Existing work has considered simulated tasks in autonomous driving and robotic manipulation [ 2 ] , while its application to complex physical robot manipulation remains unexplored. Extending proactive correction to such tasks requires an interface that allows the operator to intuitively assess the robot’s proposed three-dimensional motion before an anticipated error occurs. Although a screen-based preview can overlay this motion on a camera view, judging its depth and spatial orientation from a two-dimensional display can be difficult. To address this challenge, we propose PHIL, a proactive human-in-the-loop imitation learning framework that uses an Extended Reality (XR) interface to visualize proposed policy action chunks in the physical workspace. Each action chunk is converted into a sequence of three-dimensional end-effector poses and spatially registered with the physical workspace. The operator can therefore inspect upcoming motion in three dimensions within its execution context while the robot executes the current action chunk. When the preview indicates that the proposed motion may lead to an error, the operator takes control and provides corrections before the anticipated undesirable action occurs. During the takeover, PHIL continues to query the policy at the unchanged query schedule and display updated action chunks in the XR headset. PHIL constructs contrastive pairs from the displayed policy action chunks and the corresponding executed corrective robot trajectories. For each retained policy query during intervention, the displayed action chunk is recorded as rejected, denoted by A_{t}^{-} . The next H executed robot actions starting at that query form the corresponding corrective action chunk A_{t}^{+} , which may include policy-controlled actions after release. Both action chunks in each pair start from the same physical state. When the operator judges the most recently displayed action chunk to be acceptable, they release control and the robot returns to policy control. After data collection, PHIL uses the contrastive pairs to fine-tune the policy, encouraging it to favor executed corrective action chunks A_{t}^{+} over rejected policy action chunks A_{t}^{-} from the same physical state. We evaluate PHIL on four real-world manipulation tasks, with experiments covering both diffusion and pretrained VLA policies. Compared with HG-DAgger, PHIL increases the average success rate from 44.0% to 70.3% for the diffusion policy and from 57.8% to 76.9% for X-VLA. ## III Method
PHIL post-trains an action-chunking robot policy using proactive corrections collected before an anticipated undesirable action occurs. As illustrated in Fig. 2 , the operator inspects the policy’s predicted future motion during execution. If an error is anticipated, the operator takes control of the robot to provide corrective actions. The policy continues to generate predictions on its fixed query schedule during the takeover. Retained predictions are paired with subsequent executed corrective action chunks from the same query state and used for preference-based post-training. III-A Problem Formulation Consider an action-chunking policy \pi_{\theta} operating in discrete time. At time t , its context is c_{t}=(o_{t},q_{t},\ell_{t}) , where o_{t} contains the current visual observations, q_{t} is the robot proprioceptive state, and \ell_{t} is the task instruction. This formulation covers both visuomotor and vision-language-action policies. Given c_{t} , the policy predicts an action chunk of horizon H , A_{t}=\left(a_{t}^{(0)},a_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,a_{t}^{(H-1)}\right)\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{t}), (1) where a_{t}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{A} is the action at offset k in the proposal generated at time t . Each action chunk is converted into a trajectory of absolute Cartesian end-effector poses for the operator to preview and assess. The policy is queried every B control steps, with B\leq H . Let \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{query}}=\{t_{0}+nB\mid n=0,1,2,\ldots\} (2) denote the fixed query schedule, where t_{0} is the first query time in a rollout. Policy queries occur only at the scheduled times, both during policy control and during human intervention. When the robot remains under policy control between consecutive queries, it executes the first B actions of the predicted chunk. The full H -step chunk is available for the operator to preview.
