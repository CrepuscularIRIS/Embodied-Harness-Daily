===ABSTRACT===
Frontier large language models (LLMs) have enabled vision-and-language navigation (VLN) through direct closed-loop perception and action. However, navigation performance depends strongly on the harness governing LLM-environment interactions, while existing harnesses largely rely on manually designed navigation tools, memory, and agent roles. We introduce NavRSI, a harness-centric recursive self-improvement framework that optimizes the navigation behavior of frozen LLMs through closed-loop experience. In each round, NavRSI iteratively collects navigation trajectories and failure diagnostics, employs a designer agent to rewrite the harness, and retains the revisions that prove effective, while the designer accumulates experience from previous revisions so that the harness and this experience co-evolve. Privileged simulator diagnostics guide only the designer and remain inaccessible to the navigating LLM, enabling deployment with a single RGB camera and primitive actions, without task-specific model training or additional navigation tools. Experiments demonstrate consistent improvements across LLMs from four vendors, with 85.64% success rate on the full R2R-CE val-unseen split with GPT-6-Astra, surpassing the previously reported best by 4.13%. Evaluations on RxR-CE, NavRAG-CE, and a real humanoid robot further demonstrate its generalizability.

===INTRO===
Vision-and-language navigation (VLN) requires a robot to follow language instructions and navigate to the target destination in an unseen environment [ 3 , 16 , 17 ] . Existing approaches either train specialized navigation models on large-scale embodied data [ 13 , 1 , 38 , 8 , 32 , 41 , 43 ] or construct explicit navigation workflows around general-purpose large language models (LLMs) [ 47 , 4 , 21 , 23 , 26 ] . Frontier LLMs such as GPT-6-Astra and Fable-5.2 have recently opened up a promising alternative, where the LLM directly perceives visual observations and executes primitive actions through closed-loop interaction [ 50 ] . This is enabled by a harness (such as Codex CLI and Claude Agent SDK), i.e., the program that connects the LLM to the environment and decides its context, tools, and feedback [ 46 , 48 ] . The design of this harness thus becomes an important factor in translating the capabilities of LLMs into successful navigation [ 20 , 7 , 44 , 25 ] . However, existing harness-based VLN methods [ 50 , 20 , 7 , 44 , 25 , 48 , 24 ] largely rely on human-designed assumptions about what an LLM needs to navigate successfully. They introduce auxiliary tools for depth estimation, waypoint execution, or learned navigation policies [ 20 , 7 , 48 , 9 ] , maintain explicit spatial or episodic memories [ 7 , 44 , 24 ] , or divide navigation into specialized agent roles [ 25 , 7 , 48 ] . Although effective, these designs prescribe how the LLM should perceive, remember, and act, rather than identifying its limitations from actual interaction with the environment. Importantly, the same LLM performs very differently under different harnesses. As shown in Fig. 1 (a), GPT-5.6-Sol achieves success rates of 56.7% and 58.7% with Codex CLI and Mini-swe-agent, but reaches 68.0% with our NavRSI harness on the same primitive actions. The harness itself can thus be a significant bottleneck, raising a fundamental question: Can we systematically improve the harness based on an LLM’s own closed-loop behavior, rather than relying on manually designed navigation mechanisms? Answering this question is challenging because navigation failures emerge from long-horizon interactions between perception, reasoning, and action. An agent may repeatedly attempt blocked movements, lose track of its heading, revisit explored locations, or stop before reaching the destination. Such failures are difficult to anticipate through static prompt design, and an overall success score gives limited guidance on how the harness should be revised. Moreover, the simulator diagnostics that explain these failures are unavailable to the deployed robot, so the optimization must produce a harness that operates without privileged information. Recursive self-improvement (RSI) has recently shown promise for optimizing agent code and execution scaffolds [ 36 , 40 , 18 ] , yet applying it to embodied navigation must account for the sequential, partially observable, and physically grounded nature of this interaction. In this paper, we introduce NavRSI , a harness-centric RSI framework that keeps the LLM frozen and iteratively rewrites the executable harness around it from closed-loop navigation experience, instead of modifying the model or adding navigation tools. As shown in Fig. 2 , NavRSI starts from a general-purpose Mini-swe-agent harness [ 50 ] and alternates between three steps: (1) running navigation episodes to collect closed-loop trajectories and failure diagnostics , (2) employing a designer agent to analyze them and generate revised harness candidates , and (3) evaluating the candidates to retain effective improvements. The designer also maintains a record of its optimization experience, including observed failure patterns, the hypotheses behind its revisions, and evidence of their effectiveness, so that the harness and this experience co-evolve across rounds. To bridge the gap between optimization and deployment, the designer, a separate LLM, reads execution logs and simulator diagnostics ( e.g., distance to the goal) from training episodes, whereas the frozen LLM, as the navigator, never sees these privileged signals and relies solely on RGB observations and action feedback. Through this process, NavRSI discovers a lightweight harness that provides action feedback, progress tracking, and arrival verification, without requiring task-specific model training, additional navigation tools, or specialized agent roles. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of NavRSI. On R2R-CE-100, the NavRSI harness consistently improves navigation success rate across four LLM families, outperforming their native harnesses by up to 11.3 SR ( Fig. 1 (b)). With GPT-6-Astra, NavRSI sets new SOTA on the full R2R-CE val-unseen without training (85.64 SR), +4.13 SR over NavGPT-3 , which pairs the same LLM with an 8B model additionally trained on 19.28M samples ( Fig. 1 (c), Tab. 1 ).
