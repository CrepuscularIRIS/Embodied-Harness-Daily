# Recursive Self-Improvement in Physical AI: Mechanisms, Evidence, and Evaluation

## Abstract
Embodied systems can update policies, world models, skills, and executable programs from deployment experience, allowing one interaction to benefit later tasks. When a system also revises and reuses its data-acquisition, feedback, or learning procedures, experience changes the process that produces capability, introducing recursive self-improvement. This paper surveys research in physical AI through the connection between persistent capability and its improvement process. We first explain the functional distinction between learner updates and improvers, then compare policy learning, imagined experience, memory and skill accumulation, and executable harnesses. These approaches connect to revisions of curricula, rewards, and research programs in different ways. Contact validity, recovery, retention, and resource costs determine whether the resulting gains accumulate during continued deployment. An L0-L3 rubric distinguishes task-local adaptation, persistent capability gain, inherited process revision, and verified improver gain. Representative benchmarks and within-study contrasts relate these distinctions to existing results. We further propose common-data and interactive comparisons that evaluate reference and revised improvers from shared learner starts, separating the use of experience from its acquisition. This perspective connects the diverse methods, physical operating conditions, and evaluation evidence, underpinning embodied RSI. A comprehensive list of studies in this survey is available at https://github.com/nkd-lkz/physical-ai-rsi-survey.

## I Introduction
Robots encounter new objects, contact conditions, and task combinations after deployment. A failed attempt exposes a capability gap and can also supply experience for the next improvement. Failed grasps can inform policy updates, human corrections can become reusable skills, and recurring failures can guide further practice. Self-improvement methods incorporate this experience into continued learning so that later tasks benefit from earlier interaction. RoboCat and RECAP use robot experience to update policies, while Learning While Deploying extends the reuse of deployment experience across robot fleets. [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] As foundation models generate programs, interpret failures, and organize experiments, the editable parts of a robot system extend beyond its action policy to the learning process. ASPIRE and SHAPER retain changes to skills and harnesses, while ENPIRE exposes policy-development programs to agent-generated revision. This enables a further form of self-improvement: experience changes data selection, feedback construction, or training code, and the revised process produces the next update. We use mechanistic recursive self-improvement (RSI) for such system-generated process revisions that are retained and used in subsequent learning. Experience thereby changes how the system acquires new capabilities. [ 4 , 5 , 6 ] Current work approaches self-improvement through policy training, world models, skill libraries, harnesses, and automated experimentation. The same terminology often refers to changes at different levels. Continued practice can improve a policy, revised reward code can change its learning objective, and a more effective experimental procedure can improve subsequent learning efficiency. Connecting these approaches requires distinguishing changes in capability, changes in the learning process, and the additional gains produced by those process changes. Embodied systems must also obtain valid feedback, recover from failures, and retain existing capabilities within limited robot time and human assistance. With robot learning and deployment as the main setting, this paper connects two strands of self-improvement in physical AI: how experience becomes persistent capability, and how it changes the process that produces capability. The first covers policies and values, world models, memory and skills, and executable harnesses. The second concerns revisions to experience acquisition, feedback construction, and learning procedures. Figure 1 summarizes their relationship. The L0–L3 evidence rubric connects advances in these mechanisms to the gains established by their evaluations. This paper makes four contributions: • Conceptual distinctions and evidence criteria. We distinguish task-local adaptation, persistent capability gain, inherited process revision, and verified improver gain. The L0–L3 rubric connects these claims to observable evidence for a specific system configuration and protocol. • A synthesis of persistent and recursive mechanisms. We organize methods by what experience changes, what is retained, and how the retained change influences subsequent learning. This functional analysis links updates to the learner with revisions to data acquisition, feedback, learning procedures, and update control. • Physical conditions and controlled evaluation. We connect contact validity, recovery, retention, and resource use to the interpretation of improvement. Common-data and interactive comparisons distinguish the use of experience from its acquisition when assessing reference and revised improvers. • Evidence-based research priorities. We derive directions for grounded feedback, lasting gains, reliable recovery, and efficient improvement from the gaps between implemented mechanisms and demonstrated benefits. Related surveys provide complementary perspectives on self-evolving agents, embodied learning, and world–action models. [ 7 , 8 , 9 ] Embodied self-evolution has been organized around memory, tasks, world models, embodiment, and model design, including changes to optimization and evaluation procedures. [ 10 ] Code-based robot-learning surveys distinguish execution feedback, persistent memory, and program search, [ 11 ] while continual-learning research develops retention and transfer metrics alongside practical robotics constraints. [ 12 ] Recent humanoid surveys connect post-deployment mechanisms and meta-updates to safety constraints and lifetime trajectory evaluation. [ 13 ] General RSI surveys also cover embodied applications and controlled comparisons of original and revised improvement mechanisms under matched budgets. [ 14 ] These surveys establish the components of self-evolution. This paper connects the technical approaches through the relationship between experience, retained artifacts, and subsequent learning: how policies, models, skills, and code acquire persistent value, and when those same components participate in revising the improvement process. ## II Foundations and Conceptual Framework
Better performance after interaction can arise from adaptation within the current episode, capability retained across tasks, or changes to the learning process itself. We distinguish these cases through the object of change, its retention boundary, and its later use. The functional distinction between learner and improver explains the mechanism; capability gain, mechanistic recursion, and improver gain identify the empirical claims; and the L0–L3 rubric connects those claims to observable evidence requirements. These distinctions also help interpret the convergence of several research traditions, providing a common basis for comparing their approaches. II-A Learner state and improvement process A robot learning to grasp provides an intuitive distinction between capability and the improvement process. Updating its grasping policy from failed trajectories changes its retained action capability. If the system also identifies missing slip experience, revises its sampling or feedback procedure, and reuses that revision in later training, it changes the process that produces capability. Policies, predictive models, and reusable skills constitute the learner state x . Procedures that select experience, construct feedback, produce updates, and accept changes constitute the improver \varphi . We use self-evolution for experience-driven, system-generated changes retained for later use; recursive self-improvement additionally involves revision of the improver. The second describes how revision operation R produces a version of the improver for later use. Fixed-process learning maintains \varphi_{t+1}=\varphi_{t} .
