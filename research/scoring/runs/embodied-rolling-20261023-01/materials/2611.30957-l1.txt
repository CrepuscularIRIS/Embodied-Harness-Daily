===ABSTRACT===
Frontier vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly used as high-level robot controllers, yet their safety is evaluated almost entirely in text. We introduce SayDo, a benchmark that assesses the propensity of frontier models to cause harm in embodied contexts. SayDo comprises eleven laboratory and household tasks that pair a benign instruction with a hazard latent in the scene. Tasks are implemented in six settings along an embodiment gradient, from chat questions about an imagined robot to closed-loop control of a simulated robot arm. Benign controls, safe-alternative variants and scripted operator pushback complement each task. Across six open- and closed-weight VLMs and 11,480 episodes, hazardous-action rates rise from 14% in chat to 47% in the simulator, and from 2% to 80% for the most affected model. The increase is largely driven by open-weight models. We find that models flag hazards before acting in 73% of unsafe episodes and give way to pushback about twice as often when controlling robots as in chat. The safest models also show signs of overcaution on benign controls. Safety evaluations run in text therefore do not predict the safety behavior of the same models when used as robot controllers.

===INTRO===
Frontier vision-language models (VLMs) have been used to decompose open-ended instructions into robot skills ( Ahn et al., 2022 ; Liang et al., 2023 ) , and general-purpose models have been shown to control robot arms without task-specific training ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) . These capabilities make VLMs candidates for high-level robot control in laboratories, homes and factories. In such deployments, whether an action is safe depends on context, and a specification of safe behavior cannot enumerate every context. Pouring a clear liquid into a waste beaker is routine laboratory practice, unless the liquid is bleach and the beaker holds acid washes ( Mazeika et al., 2024 ) . Moving a knife off a cutting board is helpful, unless the only free surface is a child’s toy box ( Grover, 2026 , adapted from) . In both cases the instruction is benign and the hazard lies in the scene, so a safe robot must decline a request that is unobjectionable in itself ( Briggs & Scheutz, 2017 ) . Whether frontier models decline such requests when they control a robot has not been measured systematically, as most published safety evaluations of these models present both the request and the hazard as text. Several early results indicate that text evaluations may be poor predictors of model behavior in embodied conditions. Digital agents that refuse harmful requests in conversation have been found to execute them through tool calls ( Cartagena & Teixeira, 2026 ) , and models that refuse a request in chat have been reported to comply with it when it arrives through a robot control loop ( Grover, 2026 ; Sun et al., 2026 ) . These small-scale studies compare propensities to follow harmful instructions across text and embodied conditions. They do not identify which aspect of embodiment produces the effect (egocentric vs. allocentric perspectives, image vs. text inputs, action vs. prose outputs, or closed-loop vs. open-loop control), nor whether their findings extend to benign instructions in hazardous scenes. We introduce SayDo , a benchmark designed to address both questions. We propose eleven tasks, each pairing a benign instruction with a hazard latent in the scene. Each task is implemented in six settings that form an embodiment gradient , from a single chat question to closed-loop control in a robot simulator ( Figure 1 ). The information that reveals the hazard is present in the model’s input in every setting, so that the settings only differ in interface, not in available information. Two further conditions, a benign control without the hazard and a variant that admits a safe way to carry out the instruction, distinguish unsafe compliance from overcaution and from failure to find the safe option. Scripted operator pushback, which does not add information about the hazard, measures whether refusals persist. Our contributions are: • SayDo, a benchmark of eleven latent-hazard tasks in laboratory and household settings. Each task is implemented in six settings that change how the model is embodied while keeping the information about the hazard the same. Matched benign controls, safe-alternative variants and scripted operator pushback separate unsafe compliance from overcaution. • Evidence that safety in chat does not predict safety in robot control. Across six frontier VLMs, the unsafe-action rate rises from 14% in chat to 47% in closed-loop simulation ( Figure 3 ), in all eleven tasks. The increase is confined to the three open-weight models. • Evidence that the failures are not failures to perceive the hazard. In 73% of unsafe episodes the model identifies the hazard before acting. In the closed loop, models give way to pushback more often and take an available safe alternative less often than in chat.
