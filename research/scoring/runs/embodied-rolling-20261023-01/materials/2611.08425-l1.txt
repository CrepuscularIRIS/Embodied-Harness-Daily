# Failure Prediction and Classification in Vision-Language-Action Models Via Calibrated Latent Probing

## Abstract
Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) enable general-purpose embodied intelligence, but their performance is brittle under distribution shift, where unanticipated failures can damage equipment or endanger people. Deploying VLAs in real-world systems therefore requires a runtime monitor that is timely, calibrated, and informative while being efficient enough to run on the edge. Existing methods fail to fulfill these requirements. We present VLA-Tug, a lightweight probe over the latent layers of a VLA backbone that improves early failure detection and bounds the missed-detection and false-alarm rates, with minimal inference overhead. We show that supervising on the pre-failure segment of a trajectory increases early failure detection at matched false-positive rates by up to 30% over the strongest learned baseline. Under shift to unseen tasks, the bounds on missed-detection and false-alarm rates remain valid in up to 86% of runs, versus at most 21% for baselines. Unlike existing methods, VLA-Tug predicts the failure mode to enable corrective actions, raising top-3 accuracy on unseen tasks by up to 44% over a majority baseline. VLA-Tug enables real-time monitoring where the well-timed and informative probe supports lightweight steering that improves task success. We release a dataset of 3,180 annotated manipulation episodes to support future work on latent failure detection in VLAs at https://vla-tug.github.io.

## 1 Introduction
Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) Physical Intelligence et al. (2025) ; Physical Intelligence et al. (2026) ; NVIDIA et al. (2025) are strong candidates for general-purpose embodied intelligence. By pairing a VLM backbone pre-trained on internet-scale data with an action expert fine-tuned on demonstration trajectories, they excel at complex dexterous tasks such as tightening screws or assembling cardboard boxes. Yet their performance is brittle and can degrade significantly under distribution shift, such as changes in lighting, novel textures, or rare object combinations Wang et al. (2026) ; Fei et al. (2025) . A VLA can therefore fail unexpectedly, especially as task and environment novelty increases, and such failures can be costly, damaging materials and robots, or outright dangerous to humans. This brittleness has slowed the adoption of VLAs in factories, homes, and public spaces. Among multiple solutions, one solution that the robotics community is exploring is VLA steering. The idea of steering comes from research on LLMs, where activations are selectively modified to nudge a model towards a desired output Turner et al. (2024) . Steering VLAs is particularly interesting because it can unlock behaviors that cannot be reached through prompting alone Buurmeijer et al. (2026) ; Swann et al. (2026) . Various steering methods have recently been proposed ( Li et al., 2026b ; Liu et al., 2026a ; Tan et al., 2026 ; Swann et al., 2026 ; Cui et al., 2026 ; Jeong et al., 2026 ; Li, 2025 ) , but they rarely use a calibrated probe to decide when to steer. Some steer continuously Li et al. (2026b) ; Liu et al. (2026a) ; Cui et al. (2026) , some rely on human-specified offline triggers Swann et al. (2026) ; Li (2025) , and others are gated by a one-sided calibrated trigger Tan et al. (2026) ; Jeong et al. (2026) . Overall, they lack an efficient and principled trigger that would make them deployable in real-world systems. Research on failure detection for VLAs is becoming increasingly mainstream Gu et al. (2025) ; Agia et al. (2024) ; Ma et al. (2026) ; Huang et al. (2026) . In a nutshell, these methods compute a failure score from signals available in the VLA, often its latent variables Gu et al. (2025) or predicted actions Agia et al. (2024) ; Huang et al. (2026) ; Ma et al. (2026) , that encode how likely the policy is to fail at the current task. A detection threshold is then calibrated to give probabilistic guarantees on the detector’s performance Gu et al. (2025) . However, these guarantees often cover only the false-positive rate (FPR), i.e. the rate of successes misclassified as failures, and not a target true-positive rate (TPR), i.e. the rate of correctly classified failures, which is arguably even more important in safety-critical settings where a single failure could harm a person or damage costly equipment. These methods are also seldom evaluated systematically against ground-truth failure times, which we argue is crucial to assess whether they can prevent failures before they happen and thus enable VLA steering. Finally, they cannot predict failure modes (grasping, pushing, opening), so steering cannot be targeted towards the root cause and would require human interventions. We argue that steering would be best supported by a probe that is (T.) timely , detecting failures before they occur; (I.) informative , outputting semantic information beyond a numerical score; (C.) calibrated , achieving the desired error rates; and (E.) efficient to train, calibrate, and deploy. We address this with VLA-Tug , a well-behaved probe designed to support VLA steering in the real world. We train VLA-Tug on the latent layers of two VLAs Physical Intelligence et al. (2025) ; NVIDIA et al. (2025) using 228 distinct tasks in two simulation environments and 8 real-world tasks, and evaluate it against ground-truth failure times. We show that supervision should focus on the pre-failure part of the trajectory to improve early failure detection, and that calibrating the detection threshold to guarantee both TPR and FPR simultaneously yields guarantees that hold far more often, including on unseen tasks. Building on these findings, we propose a real-time architecture that incorporates VLA-Tug to autonomously support the detection and prevention of VLA failures. Our contributions are as follows: • VLA-Tug : A timely, informative, and efficient probe for early failure detection. On unseen tasks, our probe improves the area under the early-detection curve by up to 30\% over the strongest learned baseline. VLA-Tug also predicts the failure mode, raising the top-3 accuracy on unseen tasks by up to 44\% over a majority baseline, at under 1 ms per step. • Guarantees on false alarms and missed detections. ## 3 Problem Statement
In this section, we start by giving several broad definitions used to describe how failure detectors for VLAs function. Next, we give a mathematical formulation of our problem and then introduce how VLA-Tug addresses it. 3.1 Definitions To facilitate comparisons across methods, we give general definitions for the concepts of failure score, detection threshold, detection time, failure time, and failure mode. • Failure score. The score s_{t}\in\mathbb{R} , defined at every timestep t , encodes the probe’s belief that the VLA is failing. It need not be a probability in the strict sense. • Detection threshold. The threshold h^{\star} over s_{t} separates failures from successes. • Detection time. The time t_{\mathrm{det}}=\min\{t:s_{t}\geq h^{\star}\} is the first time the score crosses the threshold. • Failure time. The time t_{\mathrm{fail}} , which we also call the failure onset, is when the policy makes an obvious mistake or its progress stalls to the point where an intervention, human or otherwise, is needed. • Failure mode. The label f_{\mathrm{mode}} is a high-level semantic category assigned to each failure, such as grasping failure, placement failure, or no progress. These definitions are general, and their exact form varies slightly across methods. The interpretation of s_{t} depends on the training strategy, but a higher score usually signals failure and a lower score success. The threshold h^{\star} may be fixed or vary with time, and it is often calibrated with probabilistic guarantees on a set of calibration rollouts {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}} . If s_{t}<h^{\star} for all t , the detection time t_{\mathrm{det}} is undefined, and the rollout raises no alarm. The failure time definition follows Gu et al.
