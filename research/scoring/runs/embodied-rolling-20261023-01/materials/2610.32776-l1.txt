# Predictive Long-Horizon Progress Modeling with Action-Aware Quantification

## Abstract
Quantitative and dense task progress feedback is essential for robotic manipulation across data curation, policy evaluation, and reinforcement learning. Existing progress annotations often rely on sparse keyframe markings derived from subjective manual judgments, limiting their scalability and cross-task consistency, particularly in long-horizon manipulation. To overcome these challenges, we propose action-aware quantification, a unified progress specification that decomposes long-horizon trajectories into object-level subtasks via a vision-language model (VLM) and assigns weighted action credits to capture fine-grained execution dynamics. Using VLM auto-labeling and human calibration, we construct an 800+ hour training video dataset and LOOM, a long-horizon progress benchmark containing 19,618 human-calibrated evaluation frames. To model these dense progress dynamics, we introduce NimCritic, a predictive progress critic trained via Joint Video-Progress Learning. We further incorporate Historical Progress Perturbation to mitigate sequential drift, and design a contrastive Video-Progress Alignment objective that bridges asymmetric expert streams by mutually grounding intermediate visual dynamics and denoised progress trajectories. NimCritic matches or outperforms state-of-the-art process reward models (PRMs) on the established Video Progress Benchmark (VPB) and LOOM, surpassing GVL (GPT-6-Sol) on VPB by about 65% in terminal success accuracy (TSA). On the large-scale RoboArena policy evaluation benchmark, it also retrieves human-selected successful rollouts with about 97% overall recall. These results establish action-aware quantification as a scalable foundation for dense progress feedback in robotic manipulation.

## 1 Introduction
Process reward models (PRMs) evaluate intermediate execution states from visual observations, supplying dense feedback for robotic data curation, policy evaluation, and reinforcement learning [ 33 , 42 ] . Consider a robot instructed to stack blocks in sequence: a completed placement advances the task, an attempted grasp earns temporary partial credit, and a dislodged block triggers revocation of its earlier placement credit. Faithful process supervision must track these state changes across hesitation, progress regression, and recovery. Recent methods draw on vision–language model (VLM)-based reward learning to bring task semantics to visual evaluation, and on video world models to supervise temporal dynamics through future prediction [ 27 , 36 ] . Current PRMs learn relative progress through trajectory comparisons [ 21 ] , interpolate across manually annotated task stages [ 7 ] , or assign VLM-generated subtask budgets [ 14 ] . Relative comparisons establish trajectory ordering while leaving cross-task progress magnitude unspecified, and elapsed-time interpolation varies with execution speed for the same achieved state [ 7 ] . Long-horizon feedback must also quantify what a partial attempt contributes and which credit is revoked when a later action destroys an earlier achievement. Scaling process evaluation thus requires both a unified absolute progress specification and a critic that tracks non-monotonic execution across subtasks. To overcome these challenges, we propose action-aware quantification . We first define a subtask as a sequence of operations on the same manipulated object. We then allocate a fixed local budget using the weights of executed actions and the necessary remaining actions. Action boundaries locate the curve breakpoints, and retained weighted action credit determines local progress within this budget. Forward effects receive full credit, engaged-only attempts receive temporary half credit, and subsequent revocations remove the credit of the referenced earlier occurrence. Finally, global progress tracks retained contributions across subtasks, including credit revoked by later destructive actions. VLM pre-annotation and human verification establish structured action records, from which rule-based post-processing computes dense progress for training and evaluation. We assemble a training dataset of 253,276 episodes spanning 800+ hours of robot and human video, together with LOOM (a LOng-hOrizon Manipulation progress benchmark ), comprising 300 cross-embodiment trajectories evaluated at 19,618 human-calibrated frames. The benchmark follows successive subtasks in long-horizon execution, covering different rates of advancement, hesitation, regression, recovery, and incomplete execution. Building on this foundation, we introduce NimCritic ( A Nimble Critic via Joint Video–Progress Learning ), a dual-diffusion-transformer world reward model with separately supervised progress and completion outputs. We term the model nimble because it uses predictive video learning during training and scores subtasks in parallel at inference [ 41 ] , and a critic because it evaluates task advancement and goal satisfaction. Joint Video–Progress Learning models how the observed state evolves while tying those transitions to advancement toward the specified subtask goal, complemented by Historical Progress Perturbation to regularize sequential history conditioning and contrastive Video–Progress Alignment to strengthen cross-modal correspondence. On the established Video Progress Benchmark (VPB), NimCritic achieves 0.77 overall PRC-F1 and 0.82 TSA (surpassing GVL with GPT-6-Sol by about 65%). On LOOM, it leads all external baselines across all four metrics in the overall and multi-task groups (0.86 and 0.95 PRC-F1), and on RoboArena it retrieves human-selected successful rollouts with 96.72% overall recall and 98.09% hit rate. We summarize our main contributions as follows: (1) We formulate action-aware quantification , which converts object-level action effects into dense progress through weighted credits, explicit revocation, and composition across complete and incomplete subtasks. (2) We construct an 800+ hour action-aware training dataset through VLM pre-annotation and human verification, and LOOM , a 300-trajectory benchmark with 19,618 human-calibrated evaluation frames for testing long-horizon progress through hesitation, regression, recovery, and incomplete execution. (3) We propose NimCritic , which combines predictive video learning with progress and completion supervision, using Historical Progress Perturbation and Video–Progress Alignment to support sequential estimates and connect visual features to task progress. (4) We demonstrate the highest overall PRC-F1 and TSA on VPB and LOOM, together with 96.72% successful-rollout recall on RoboArena, and analyze the roles of egocentric human videos, non-expert trajectories, and predictive supervision.

## 3 Predictive Long-Horizon Progress Modeling
3.1 Architecture Our framework comprises the action-aware quantification pipeline, a VLM front-end for subtask decomposition, and a predictive critic, together forming NimCritic (Figure 1 ). First, the action-aware quantification pipeline establishes a unified, scalable protocol that generates human-aligned dense progress and completion supervision for training and evaluation. Second, VLM-based subtask decomposition serves as the front-end module that partitions long-horizon multi-task videos into ordered, object-level subtask segments. Third, given each subtask goal and observed video window, the critic learns and predicts within-subtask progress and a dedicated completion output before duration-weighted global composition. 3.2 Progress Annotation Pipeline Procedural task annotations provide semantic subgoals and temporal structure for progress supervision [ 7 , 14 ] ; our action-aware quantification assigns numerical credit to the actions within those subtasks. Under this specification, our pipeline produces an 800+ hour action-aware training dataset and LOOM, a long-horizon progress benchmark of 300 trajectories and 19,618 human-calibrated evaluation frames spanning diverse embodiments and non-expert behaviors including hesitation, progress regression, and recovery. To achieve consistent quantification, our rule-based specification distinguishes an action’s physical class from its semantic task effect: the same motion can complete a subgoal, make an incomplete attempt on an object, or revoke credit from an earlier achievement. Specifically, we define a subtask as consecutive operations on the same manipulated object and use action boundaries to locate its temporal breakpoints. Each physical action class has a fixed weight w_{c} estimated from dataset-wide normalized action durations, while its effect on the task determines whether an occurrence earns or revokes credit. Within each subtask, local progress at video timestamp t is the retained action credit normalized by a fixed subtask budget B ; the subtask index is omitted below for clarity.
