# SCOPE: Scene-graph Construction Optimized by Plan Error-correction for Robot Long-term Planning Datasets

## Abstract
Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) stands as a fundamental challenge in robotics, essential for enabling autonomous agents to execute complex, long-horizon tasks in diverse environments. While Large Language Models (LLMs) introduce a new paradigm for TAMP by leveraging extensive common sense, their inherent reasoning limitations hinder direct application, and fine-tuning strategies to enhance these capabilities are currently bottlenecked by the scarcity of high-quality datasets. Existing automated generation methods often suffer from homogeneity, limiting model generalization, and unsolvability, where hallucinated scene graphs create physical mismatches with task goals. To address these issues, we propose Scene-graph Construction Optimized by Plan Error-correction (SCOPE), an automated framework designed to generate diverse and executable TAMP datasets. SCOPE incorporates multi-level diversity metrics to maximize the information entropy of scenarios. Crucially, it introduces a Plan Feedback Error-correction pipeline that utilizes planner execution failures as feedback signals to retroactively rectify logical inconsistencies in scene graphs, ensuring physical feasibility. Experimental results demonstrate that SCOPE exhibits greater robustness to erroneous scene graphs and generates higher-quality data. When utilized for LLM fine-tuning, SCOPE-generated data yield a downstream TAMP model that achieves an absolute 12.33% gain in success rate over its counterpart fine-tuned with data generated by DELTA, a state-of-the-art PDDL-based planner.

## I Introduction
Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) stands as a fundamental challenge in the field of robotics. Traditional approaches [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] rely primarily on rule-based designs and heuristic searches. However, these methods often lack sufficient generalization capabilities. The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) [ 4 , 5 ] has introduced a new paradigm [ 6 , 7 ] for addressing TAMP challenges. By leveraging their extensive common sense knowledge, LLMs demonstrate significant potential in handling complex TAMP tasks in different scenarios, enhancing the universality of planning systems. Current methods utilizing LLMs for TAMP can be broadly categorized into Symbolic Planning and Direct Planning. Symbolic Planning [ 8 , 9 , 10 ] employs the LLM as a translator that decomposes tasks based on hand-crafted rules to generate problem definitions for traditional planners. While this approach achieves a relatively high success rate, it relies heavily on manually defined domains and rules. Consequently, this paradigm exhibits limited error tolerance, making full automation difficult. Conversely, the Direct Planning methods [ 6 , 11 , 12 ] utilize the LLM directly as a planner. These methods exploit the common sense reasoning capabilities of the model and require minimal manual prior constraints, allowing users to accomplish a wide range of tasks through natural language. Nevertheless, the success rate of this approach is often limited by the inherent shortcomings of LLMs in temporal reasoning and their tendency towards hallucination. Ultimately, the performance of both paradigms is intrinsically constrained by the underlying planning capabilities of the LLMs. Therefore, fine-tuning LLMs with extensive data serves as an effective strategy [ 13 , 14 ] to internalize planning logic and enhance temporal reasoning capabilities. A standard TAMP fine-tuning sample consists of three core elements: the scene graph describing the environmental state, the instruction defining the task goal, and the plan serving as the solution path. However, constructing TAMP datasets currently requires balancing data quality and annotation cost. Manually annotated datasets [ 15 ] offer high quality yet incur prohibitive costs, making them difficult to scale for fine-tuning needs. While data constructed using LLMs [ 16 ] offers a low-cost automated alternative, it often suffers from low quality. This deficiency primarily manifests in two aspects. First, the generated content suffers from a lack of diversity. Due to the preference of LLMs, the scene graphs often exhibit high repetitiveness, resulting in insufficient information entropy within the dataset and limiting the generalization ability of the model. Second, the generated tasks frequently suffer from unsolvability because of the wrong scene graphs. Influenced by hallucinations, automatically generated scene graphs often contain logical flaws, such as missing key interactive objects or incorrect attributes. This mismatch between the environment and the task renders the generated problems physically unsolvable, thereby introducing a significant amount of noise into the training data. To address these challenges, we propose S cene-graph C onstruction O ptimized by P lan E rror-correction ( SCOPE ), an automated generation framework designed specifically for constructing high-quality TAMP datasets. To mitigate the homogeneity inherent in LLM-generated content, SCOPE incorporates multi-level Diversity Metrics during the Scene Graph Construction phases. Specifically, we monitor Global Diversity to ensure a broad coverage of task types while leveraging Local Diversity to enrich fine-grained variations in object attributes and spatial relationships, thereby maximizing the information entropy of the dataset. Second, to tackle the prevalent issue of unsolvability in automatically generated data, SCOPE introduces a Plan Feedback Error-correction pipeline. This pipeline establishes a closed-loop system for generation and verification, where execution results from the planner serve as feedback signals. This process allows the system to retroactively identify and correct logical inconsistencies within the scene graph while iteratively generating feasible planning sequences, effectively solving mismatches between the environment and the task. III-A Diverse Scene Graph Generation via Statistical Feedback and Dynamic Sampling To overcome the LLM’s biased preference for high-frequency, conventional room types, we introduce a room composition tracker that continuously monitors the statistical distribution of historically generated scenes. We define the set of room types as \mathcal{R} . At generation step t , let the cumulative usage frequency of room type r_{i} be u_{t}(r_{i}) . The empirical distribution of room types in the generation history can then be expressed as \hat{p}_{t}(r_{i})=u_{t}(r_{i})/\sum_{r_{j}\in\mathcal{R}}u_{t}(r_{j}) .
