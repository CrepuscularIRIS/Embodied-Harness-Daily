# Embodied Science Agents: Making Embodied Experimental Design Learnable

## Abstract
Recent advances in large models have accelerated AI for Science toward agentic science, where AI agents autonomously conduct scientific discovery. Realising this vision requires closing the loop between computational reasoning and physical experimentation, positioning self-driving laboratories (SDLs) as critical enablers. However, existing SDLs exhibit a fundamental paradox: foundation model integration has increased linguistic sophistication while severing physical grounding. By treating models as disembodied planners that output symbolic workflows for separate robotic systems, current architectures inherently block the development of embodied experimental intuition. We argue that overcoming this paradox requires embedding \emph{learnability} along three interdependent axes: cognitive learnability (learning what to investigate), experimental learnability (learning where to search), and embodied learnability (learning how to act). Building on this view, we introduce the Embodied Science Agent (ESA), which unifies digital intelligence with physical embodiment through three synergistic components: a Digital Agent that provides cognitive and experimental learnability via memory-based reasoning and Bayesian optimisation, an Embodied Agent that provides embodied learnability via generalisable instrument control, and an Embodied Unified Interface for seamless coordination. We validated ESA in autonomous wastewater treatment experiments, navigating over 200 million possible conditions. Through 15 continuous rounds, ESA raised the final solution transmittance from 82\% to 99.7\%, corresponding to a 98.5\% reduction in residual dye concentration and near-complete decolourisation, while its learned visuomotor policies generalised across varying instrument placements, a promising step toward next-generation SDLs for autonomous scientific discovery.

## 1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence is transforming scientific discovery, driving AI for Science (AI4S) as a pivotal frontier ( Jumper et al., 2021 ; Yang et al., 2023 ; Noé et al., 2019 ) . Within it, self-driving laboratories (SDLs) couple computational reasoning with robotic experimentation to actively test hypotheses in the physical world, closing the loop between modelling and empirical validation ( Burger et al., 2020 ; Tom et al., 2024 ) . The recent integration of foundation models has expanded SDLs from narrow, protocol-driven platforms into generalisable scientific agents that reason autonomously across chemistry, materials science, and biology ( Pyzer-Knapp et al., 2025 ; Choi et al., 2025 ; Guo et al., 2025 ; Menon et al., 2026 ; Hu et al., 2025 ) . Yet these advances mask a critical gap: today’s SDLs think about experiments, but do not experience them. Human experimental expertise is fundamentally embodied: through repeated sensorimotor interaction, chemists and biologists develop tacit sensitivity to cues such as colour gradients, reaction textures, or fluid resistance during pipetting, which enables techniques to transfer across reagents and instruments and to adapt when conditions deviate from written protocols. Current SDLs, by contrast, maintain a strict division between foundation models for reasoning and robotic systems for execution, precluding embodied expertise and yielding three systemic limitations: insufficient physical grounding (operations treated as symbolic actions devoid of material dynamics), limited adaptivity (no real-time feedback between planning and execution), and absence of sensorimotor learning (no accumulation of experimental intuition). Closing this gap requires embodied scientific agents that unify perception, cognition, and action to learn through physical interaction. To address these limitations, we argue that next-generation SDLs must embed learnability along three interdependent axes that together constitute embodied scientific intelligence: cognitive learnability , learning what to investigate by reflecting on past reasoning; experimental learnability , learning where to search by sequentially refining experimental designs from evidence; and embodied learnability , learning how to act by acquiring robust manipulation skills through interaction. These axes are deeply interdependent: genuinely autonomous discovery emerges from their co-evolution rather than from optimising any one in isolation (formalised in Appendix A ). Guided by this vision, we introduce the Embodied Science Agent (ESA), a new embodied self-driving laboratory paradigm that transforms SDLs from automated experimenters into adaptive, self-evolving agents. ESA couples digital intelligence with physical systems through three tightly coupled components: (i) a Digital Agent , the cognitive core (cerebrum) driving open-ended evolution via adaptive reasoning, Bayesian optimisation (BO), and continual learning from memory; (ii) an Embodied Agent (cerebellum) that unifies diverse robotic and scientific instruments through multiple embodied control modules for generalised, reliable operation; and (iii) an Embodied Unified Interface (EUI) providing a standardised, low-latency, safe communication protocol that bridges the two across heterogeneous instruments. Internally, ESA is realised as a distributed multi-agent system in which specialised agents—a Principal Investigator agent for planning, a Bayesian optimiser for experimental design, perception and GUI agents for instrument interaction, an embodied control agent, and a reporter agent—cooperate through the EUI to close the perception–reasoning–action loop. Grounding such distributed agents in real physical experimentation, and coordinating them safely and reliably, is precisely the class of problem central to distributed AI (DAI); ESA offers a concrete testbed where distributed cognition must be tightly coupled with embodied action. To evaluate this paradigm, we implemented ESA in a functional chemical laboratory that integrates conventional instruments with a dual-arm embodied platform. The system autonomously conducted a complete optimisation cycle for the Electro-Fenton degradation of Safranine T wastewater—planning, execution, and refinement without human intervention. Through iterative BO, ESA rapidly converged to near-optimal operating conditions, raising the final solution transmittance from 82% to 99.7%, which corresponds, via the Beer–Lambert law, to a 98.5% reduction in residual dye concentration and near-complete decolourisation. This validation shows the framework’s capacity for closed-loop, data-driven optimisation in a real laboratory. ## 3. Methodology
As shown in Fig. 2 , ESA is built on a hybrid cognitive–embodied architecture that tightly couples digital intelligence with physical experimentation. It integrates a cognitive reasoning core, the Digital Agent (analogous to the cerebrum), which handles hypothesis generation, planning, and memory-based inference, with embodied experimental systems, the Embodied Agent (analogous to the cerebellum), which translates plans into accurate physical execution; the EUI bridges the two so that abstract reasoning is grounded in reliable embodied action. Overview of Embodied Science Agent For a specific experiment, the workflow is an iterative cycle coupling digital decision-making with embodied execution. It begins with the Virtual Scientist Avatar , a multimodal (ASR/TTS) interface for transparent human–agent interaction and monitoring. When an experimental intent is identified, it is passed to the Principal Investigator (PI) Agent in the Digital Agent layer (the cerebrum), which performs deep research for external knowledge and retrieves relevant past cases from its episodic memory, shifting planning from static rules to adaptive, experience-driven reasoning. The resulting graph-based plan is dispatched to the EUI (the Executor in Fig. 2 ), which harmonises data and control via the Model Context Protocol (MCP), a device registry, and embedded safety checks, and drives the Embodied Agent to execute the plan through robotic systems and instruments.
