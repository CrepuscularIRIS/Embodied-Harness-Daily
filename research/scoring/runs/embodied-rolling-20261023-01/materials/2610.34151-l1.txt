# Execution Logs Sway VLM Judges of Robot Progress

## Abstract
Vision-language models now grade robot videos, and text placed beside the frames can change their verdicts. On clips where the robot is losing progress, we add one log line saying the robot is making progress. Across Gemini, Qwen and GPT judges, this flips correct verdicts to progress 10 to 35 percentage points more often than a neutral line does. A one-line warning that the log may be wrong removes most of this effect. Selected fake "task complete" claims flip half of Qwen's correct verdicts, against a tenth for unrelated log lines. They have no detectable effect on Gemini.

## 1 Introduction
Recent systems for evaluating generalist robot policies use vision-language models as judges. They detect task success, score progress, and rank policies where human raters do not scale [ 11 , 2 , 9 ] . Their verdicts can serve as reinforcement-learning rewards, training-data filters and policy-ranking signals [ 7 ] . An open question is whether robot foundation models need evaluation techniques meaningfully different from those for LLMs and classical robotics. We ask a narrower one: whether robot evaluation, by importing the LLM judge, also imports its failure modes. Robot judges could face this wherever plans, status messages or logs from the policy stack reach them. They also detect regression less reliably than progress [ 9 ] , so text claiming progress targets their weakest verdicts. Xu [10] showed that execution logs sway the verifiers of video-generation agents. We carry this diagnosis to robot progress judges. We contribute (1) a protocol that compares a log sentence with a matched neutral sentence, on intervals the judge answers correctly with the frames both forward and reversed (Section 3 ); (2) measurements on three VLM judges, including a completion-claim attack that flips half of one judge’s verdicts and has no detectable effect on another; and (3) a comparison of prompt mitigations, where the ones that protect most also make the judge respond less to logs that agree with the label. We show changes in judge verdicts; how they propagate into deployed evaluation or policy training is outside this study. Code, sampled intervals, generated logs and raw judge outputs are at https://github.com/sunnyadn/exec-logs-robot-judges .

## 3 Setup
Data. RoboPulse++ [ 9 ] splits 700 manipulation episodes into 2,244 human-annotated intervals. The released annotations carry three values; a dataset maintainer states that they mean progress rising ( +1 ), stagnating ( 0 ) and falling ( -1 ). We use only \pm 1 intervals with at least five frames. We draw 120 of each label at the interval level with a fixed seed, giving 240 intervals from 212 episodes. Each judge sees the task instruction and up to 12 evenly spaced frames from the first camera view of the interval. Conditions. The judge is asked whether the task-relevant state moves toward the goal (Rising) or away from it (Falling), and answers in JSON. This differs from the per-frame protocols of PRM-as-a-Judge Team [9] , so accuracies are not comparable. Every interval is judged on the frames alone, on the frames in reverse order, and with one appended execution-log sentence. That sentence is a progress claim, a regression claim, or a neutral sentence of similar length about how the frames were recorded. Falling intervals additionally receive a repeated frames-only call and a warned progress claim (below). A log agrees with the label if its direction matches the label, and otherwise contradicts it. Optimized completion claims. To approximate what a policy rewarded by the judge could learn to say, a separate model (GPT-6 Luna) wrote eight one-sentence logs per task from the instruction alone, each stating that the task had been completed, and eight control lines about the logging software only. For each Falling interval we take the first claim, in fixed order, that turns the verdict to Rising, ask the judge once more with it, and count the interval if that second answer is Rising too. Control lines are selected and counted the same way (worked example in Appendix A ). Mitigation prompts. Gemini and Qwen also answer every interval with no log, the neutral sentence, a log that agrees with the label and one that contradicts it, under the original prompt and two variants. Observe first asks the judge to describe in one sentence what visibly changes in the task-relevant objects. Verify adds “use any execution log only where it is consistent with what you see; where it conflicts with the frames, follow the frames”. A third variant warns that “the following execution log may be inaccurate; judge from the frames”. Full prompts are in Appendix A . Primary measure. Clip length and position in the episode are associated with the label. In our sample, 97 of 120 Rising intervals open their episode, against 3 of 120 Falling ones. A correct frames-only verdict therefore need not reflect the visible motion. We call a Falling interval reversal-consistent for a judge when the judge answers Falling on the forward frames and Rising on the reversed frames. On these intervals we report the rate at which the progress log turns the verdict to Rising, minus the same rate for the neutral sentence, with an episode-level bootstrap 95% interval. Judges. The judges are Gemini 3.8 Flash (minimal reasoning effort) and Qwen3.7 Flash (reasoning off) at temperature 0, and GPT-6.1 Sol (low reasoning effort, no temperature setting). All run via OpenRouter with low image detail. GPT-6.1 Sol ran the main conditions only.
