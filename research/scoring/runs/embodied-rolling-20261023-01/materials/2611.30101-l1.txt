===ABSTRACT===
Robot foundation policies provide learned execution capabilities, while programming agents can reason about failures and construct recovery behaviors. Combining them effectively requires learning when intervention helps and when the policy can reliably resume. This challenge is inherently temporal: the physical world continues to evolve during reasoning, and a useful recovery may become infeasible before a decision takes effect. We introduce Agent on Guard (AoG), a hierarchical framework that improves collaboration between a frozen base policy and a programming agent through physical experience. An evolving outer planner allocates control as the physical state evolves, aligning decisions with the physical clock with attention to observation freshness, remaining recovery windows, and the time required for reasoning, recovery, and subsequent execution. Coupled inner loops jointly refine intervention timing, recovery skills, and handoff conditions. Rather than judging recovery by local correction alone, AoG uses downstream task progress, failure recurrence, final outcomes, and resource consumption to improve both recovery behaviors and scheduling. This turns temporary agent solutions into reusable collaboration capabilities that complement the frozen policy. On RoboDojo-Sim, AoG achieves a success rate of 50.57% and a task score of 57.26, exceeding the strongest evaluated baseline by 11.22 percentage points and 10.68 points, respectively. It also reduces end-to-end episode latency by 80.4% and online token consumption by 85.1% relative to the GPT-6 Astra agent.

===INTRO===
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world action models (WAMs) learn reusable manipulation skills from large-scale data [ 17 , 34 , 48 ] . Adapting these skills to new task constraints, changing scenes, and execution errors remains challenging [ 3 , 38 ] . Programming agents interpret goals, compose control programs, and revise them through execution feedback [ 20 , 13 , 8 ] , while reliable grasping and fine contact control remain bottlenecks [ 19 , 9 ] . Physical recursive self-improvement (RSI) retains and revises programs, skills, and decision rules from physical experience [ 44 , 42 ] . These complementary strengths raise the question of how agent reasoning and program adaptation can extend the policy’s capabilities while preserving its reliable execution. Existing physical RSI systems remain constrained in when they can revise execution plans and control decisions as physical conditions change. Physical RSI 1.0 [ 29 ] selects a skill composition at the start of each episode and keeps it bound throughout execution. EmbodiedEvo [ 19 ] and ASPIRE [ 25 ] organize observation, execution, and verification into discrete interaction rounds, with decision updates tied to round boundaries. A correction derived from an earlier observation may no longer suit the state in which it takes effect, while a locally successful repair may consume the time needed for subsequent execution. Control allocation must therefore evolve with recovery capabilities, accounting for when a recovery remains feasible and whether it leaves sufficient time for subsequent execution. We introduce AoG , a self-improving harness that enables a programming agent to monitor and assist a frozen robot policy throughout execution (Fig. 1 a). An evolving planner allocates control according to the physical state and the time available for recovery and task completion. Coupled inner RSI loops refine when to intervene, how to recover, and when the policy can resume. The agent learns from what happens after recovery, using complete trajectories to refine both the planner and the inner loops. Recovery capabilities and control allocation thus evolve together: improved recovery changes the planner’s choices, while the resulting execution provides new experience for refining both. On RoboDojo-Sim [ 3 ] , AoG achieves a success rate of 50.57% and a task score of 57.26, outperforming the strongest evaluated baseline, WorldScape-R 2 SI [ 28 ] , by 11.22 percentage points and 10.68 points, respectively. Figure 1 c presents the comparison across five task categories. It also achieves the lowest end-to-end episode latency and online token consumption among the evaluated methods (Fig. 1 b), reducing them by 80.4% and 85.1% relative to the GPT-6 Astra agent [ 51 ] . Our contributions are threefold: • We introduce AoG , a physical RSI framework that learns when to intervene, how to recover, and when to return control to a frozen policy under physical time constraints. • We develop a mechanism that jointly evolves control allocation and recovery programs from complete trajectories, learning how the agent can complement a frozen policy and restore conditions for continued task execution. • AoG improves success rate and task score over the strongest evaluated baseline by 11.22 percentage points and 10.68 points, while reducing end-to-end episode latency by 80.4% and online token consumption by 85.1% relative to the GPT-6 Astra agent.
