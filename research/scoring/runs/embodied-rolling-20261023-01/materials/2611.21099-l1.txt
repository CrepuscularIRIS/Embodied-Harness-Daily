# ForceHarness: Turning Force into Action and Physics for Frozen Frontier Models

## Abstract
Frontier multimodal models can now control robots zero-shot from images, yet many manipulation tasks depend on contact forces that images cannot show. Bringing force to an API-only frozen model raises two challenges. First, contact evolves much faster than an API model can decide, so the system must close the loop on force beneath the model. Second, a frozen model cannot interpret raw force streams, so force must be returned in a form the model can reason about. Therefore, we present ForceHarness, a harness that lets frozen models use force without demonstrations or finetuning. After calibrating hand force from a wrist sensor or joint torques alone, ForceHarness addresses the first challenge with self-terminating force primitives that regulate force on their own and return at a contact event. To address the second challenge, a physics translation layer turns each force trace into physical quantities with uncertainty as the tool result. To evaluate ForceHarness against expert force controllers and learned policies under identical conditions, we also build HiddenPhys, a seven-task MuJoCo benchmark with hidden physical variables. On a real arm without a wrist force sensor, ForceHarness raises the zero-shot success rate of GPT-6 Astra from 3.3% to 90.0%. In simulation, it adds 17.5 percentage points on the public ForceBench suite and 34.3 percentage points on HiddenPhys, surpassing force-aware policies trained on demonstrations on both benchmarks and matching hand-written expert controllers on HiddenPhys. Ablations further show that smaller models make significantly better decisions when given physical quantities rather than raw force values.

## 1 Introduction
Frontier multimodal models can now control robots directly without task-specific training. For example, GPT-6 Astra runs behind an API with frozen weights and still controls a robot zero-shot from images through a pose interface [ 43 , 27 ] . Much of this progress comes from the harness that wraps the frozen model and provides semantic action units [ 5 ] , virtual camera views [ 32 ] , agent-written tools and code [ 13 , 17 ] , or low-level VLA skills [ 44 ] . However, existing harnesses mainly help the model see and move. Beyond seeing and moving, many manipulation tasks in the physical world depend on contact forces that images cannot show. For example, driving a screw requires pressing down while turning until the thread engages, and sorting identical-looking bottles requires comparing their weight. Existing work brings force into such tasks mainly through learning. Specifically, force-aware policies [ 39 , 18 , 15 ] and benchmarks [ 14 ] add wrist force as a network input to visuomotor policies [ 6 ] and vision-language-action (VLA) models [ 24 ] . However, this approach requires demonstrations for every task and updates model weights, so it cannot be applied to an API-only frozen model. Meanwhile, existing harnesses for frozen models that expose contact typically pass raw wrench and tactile values [ 7 , 29 ] or ask the model to generate force-control code [ 4 , 28 ] . Yet these designs still leave the model to parse raw sensor numbers or write low-level control without calibrated physical feedback. We therefore ask: can a frozen frontier model understand force and use it to complete manipulation tasks that cannot be done without it? Answering this question raises two challenges. First, contact evolves much faster than an API model can decide, so the system must close the loop on force beneath the model through general skills that regulate force on their own under the model’s direction. Second, a frozen model cannot interpret raw force streams or choose actions from raw numbers alone, so the system must return force in a form the model can reason about . Therefore, we present ForceHarness, a harness that lets a frozen frontier model use wrist force without demonstrations or training. As shown in Figure 1 , ForceHarness places three layers between the model and the robot. At the bottom, a force sensing and calibration layer estimates hand force from a wrist sensor or joint torques alone and removes sensor offsets. In the middle, self-terminating force primitives address the first challenge by turning common contact skills such as guarded moves, sliding, and insertion into callable tools. Once called, each primitive regulates force on its own and returns at a contact event. At the top, a physics translation layer addresses the second challenge by converting each primitive’s force trace into physical quantities with uncertainty estimates and status flags, such as payload mass, contact height, and friction. At each turn, the model calls a primitive, reads the returned physical quantities, and decides what to do next. Together, these three layers form a general set of building blocks that bridges a frozen frontier model and low-level force control. We evaluate ForceHarness on a real robot arm without a wrist force sensor and across two simulation benchmarks, as summarized in Figure 1 . On the real robot, we design six contact-rich tasks and find that ForceHarness raises the zero-shot success rate of GPT-6 Astra from 3.3% without force to 90.0%. In simulation, the same model with ForceHarness operates from RGB images on the public ForceBench suite [ 14 ] and improves from 70.0% to 87.5%, surpassing the force-aware policies trained on that benchmark. To further compare frozen models with classical controllers and learned force policies under identical conditions, we build HiddenPhys, a seven-task MuJoCo benchmark with hidden physical variables. On HiddenPhys, ForceHarness raises the success rate of GPT-6 Astra from 62.1% to 96.4%, matching hand-written expert force controllers and far exceeding Diffusion Policy [ 6 ] , \pi_{0.5} [ 24 ] , and ForceVLA [ 39 ] trained on about 200 demonstrations per task. Our contributions are: • We propose ForceHarness , a three-layer harness of force calibration, self-terminating force primitives, and physics translation that bridges an API-only frozen model and low-level force control without demonstrations or training. • We build HiddenPhys , a seven-task MuJoCo benchmark with hidden physical variables that includes expert force controllers and learned force policies evaluated on identical seeds. • Experiments on a real robot and two simulated benchmarks show that ForceHarness lets frozen frontier models make full use of force for contact-rich manipulation, outperforming both training-based methods and the same harness without force.

## 3 Method
3.1 Overview ForceHarness sits between a frozen model and a robot and has three layers, shown in Figure 2 . The bottom layer, force sensing and calibration , turns the robot’s raw signals into a calibrated estimate of the force that the environment applies to the hand. The middle layer is a menu of self-terminating force primitives , contact skills that regulate force on their own and stop at a contact event. The top layer, the physics translation layer , converts the force trace of each primitive into physical quantities with uncertainties. Because the model is reached only through an API and needs seconds for each decision, force enters the loop twice. Inside a primitive it controls contact at the rate of the controller, and in the tool result it informs the model’s next decision. None of the layers assumes a particular arm, and Sec. A of the supplementary material lists the constants of each robot and simulator. 3.2 Force sensing and calibration The bottom layer provides one signal to the layers above, the force F that the environment applies to the hand. A wrist force/torque sensor measures it directly. Many arms have no such sensor, so the layer can also recover the force from the external joint torques \tau_{\mathrm{ext}} that a torque-controlled arm estimates. It solves for the wrench w at the flange by least squares, then removes a zero point b and flips the sign, w=(J^{\top})^{+}\,\tau_{\mathrm{ext}},\qquad F=-(w_{F}-b), (1) where J is the flange Jacobian and w_{F} is the force part of w . Such an estimate has little noise, but its zero point drifts as the arm moves and changes pose, and no filter removes a bias that changes this slowly. The layer therefore controls when it reads rather than how it filters. It tares at rest , re-zeroing b before each empty-hand primitive so that the zero is taken close to the pose of the coming contact. After each primitive it lets the arm settle, then reads , relaxing the controller onto the measured pose and averaging the force over a short still window. Finally, it reports changes , giving each still reading relative to a reference from the same episode, such as the empty hand or the moment of the grasp, so that the model compares readings instead of trusting absolute values.
