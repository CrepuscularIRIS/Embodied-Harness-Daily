===ABSTRACT===
Weight-space programming is a process where a large language model reads and writes the weights of a neural network directly, guided by a natural language specification, without an optimiser or gradient descent. This study applies it to automatic tractor guidance, where a 253-parameter neural network steers along paths of lines and arcs and must be adapted when the machine changes, such as a slower steering actuator. GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 (also 5.2) edited the network for our research tractor using a small budget of simulated evaluations to repair a network trained for another tractor and write a controller into a network with zero weights. We compared them with an evolution strategy, gradient descent and pure pursuit in simulation and on the real tractor. Both models read the network and analysed the closed loop with control theory correctly, and Fable's repair without simulation succeeded only occasionally. With simulated feedback, both models repaired the trained network in single sessions and wrote a working controller from zero weights, whereas from zero the evolution strategy found none. The controllers written from zero were sparse and in a classical cascaded structure; one model edited the trained network while the other replaced it. Gradient descent reached higher accuracy with far more simulation, and all language model controllers steered the real tractor without intervention, with errors of the same order as pure pursuit. ===INTRO===

1.1 Weight-space programming Weight-space programming is a process where a general-purpose large language model (LLM) reads and writes the weights of a neural network directly, guided by a natural-language specification instead of an optimiser or gradient descent ( Takehi et al., 2026 ) . The MIT Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (CSAIL) introduced the term weight-space programming in a research blog post on 2 November 2026 ( Takehi et al., 2026 ) . This study employed the AMX G-trac tractor as the target machine (Fig. 1 ). To illustrate this concept, every weight of a network may be viewed as a knob (Fig. 2 ). A human engineer chooses the network architecture, the data or the reward, and the loss, and a numerical optimiser then tunes every weight by gradient descent. In reinforcement learning, the gradient comes from the reward, for example with proximal policy optimisation (PPO) ( Schulman et al., 2017 ; Andrychowicz et al., 2021 ) . The resulting controller works but remains a black box, where every weight is used and no one can explain why a single number has its value. A trained policy is tied to its plant, as a model-free policy learned for one actuator delay does not transfer to another delay ( Chen et al., 2021 ) . Therefore, adapting a trained network to a new machine means retraining it. GPT-6 Astra repaired a failing reinforcement-learning policy for a car in the Kinetix physics environment in one reply, which changed 3.5 % of the weights ( Takehi et al., 2026 ) . The model also wrote game-playing networks into all-zero weights, and these played better than networks trained with proximal policy optimisation for 100 million steps ( Takehi et al., 2026 ) . First follow-up studies verified discrete decision policies by model checking ( Gross, 2026 ) and debugged a robot policy in simulation ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) . However, all these results concern games, discrete tasks or simulation. This study addresses whether the same capability can serve the engineering of a controller for a physical machine, with feedback dynamics, limits and safety. 1.2 Tractor guidance Table 1: Eras of tractor guidance controllers by who writes the controller and in what form. In Era 1, a human writes a control law with a few hand-tuned parameters. ## 3 Materials and methods
3.1 Tractor and steering actuator model This study used the AMX G-trac research tractor (Fig. 1 ), which has four-wheel steering and whose rear axle was held straight. The simulation model is a kinematic bicycle with the reference point on the rear axle, a constant speed of 1 m/s, and a time step of 0.05 s. The steering chain consists of four stages, where the controller integrates a steering-rate command of at most 15 deg/s into a command angle clipped at 30 deg. A pure dead time of 0.45 s and a first-order lag with a time constant of 0.75 s follow, and the physical wheel limits are 34 deg and 26.5 deg/s. The dead time and lag are rounded to the time step of 0.05 s from the identification values of 0.43 s and 0.73 s. The path is cut into chunks of at most 4 m, and the network sees the current chunk and the next 3. Each segment of the path is split into the smallest number of equal chunks that are not longer than 4 m. The current chunk is the chunk on which the projection of the rear axle lies, so that the first token keeps its start point until the rear axle passes the end of its chunk. A fixed SE(2) layer without parameters turns each chunk into a token of 9 features in the vehicle frame. A token encoder 9-4-4 with tanh units and 60 parameters is shared by the 4 tokens. A head 22-8-1 with a tanh hidden layer and 193 parameters gives the output, which is a steering-rate command in the range from -1 to 1 scaled to 15 deg/s. The output has an exact mirror symmetry, u=\tanh(0.5\,(f(o)-f(\mathrm{mirror}(o)))) , which needs no extra parameters; therefore, the policy is odd and the output bias has no effect.
