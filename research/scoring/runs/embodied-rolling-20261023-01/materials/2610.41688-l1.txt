# When the Past Shapes Action: Understanding Embodied Memory

## Abstract
Recent advances in generative foundation models have expanded the capabilities of embodied AI, enabling increasingly general navigation, manipulation, and long-horizon task execution. However, effective interaction with dynamic environments requires more than only strong perception and reasoning: agents must act with partial knowledge and retain, retrieve, and update information from past observations, actions, and experiences. This survey examines embodied memory as a mechanism for preserving decision-relevant information beyond the current observation. We organize existing approaches along two complementary dimensions: six fundamental memory requirements, spanning world-state persistence, task progress, belief maintenance, and experiential learning; and eight architectural categories, including recurrent states, fast weights, visual and symbolic representations, spatial memory, and experience-based systems. We analyze their capabilities and limitations, and examine how existing benchmarks evaluate memory-dependent behavior, distinguishing genuine use of historical information from general task performance. Finally, we identify open challenges in selecting relevant experiences, maintaining consistent beliefs in changing environments, and consolidating knowledge across episodes. Our analysis provides a framework for understanding memory as an essential component of reliable, adaptive, and lifelong-horizon embodied intelligence.

## 1 Introduction
“All those moments will be lost in time, like tears in rain.” — Roy Batty, replicant (Blade Runner, 1982) Embodied intelligence depends on proper environment grounding and, critically, on information whose relevance outlasts its visibility. A robot may need to retrieve an object last seen inside a drawer, verify whether an earlier manipulation succeeded, or resume a procedure after an interruption. In each case, the current observation leaves decision-relevant variables unresolved, and successful action requires a state inferred from interaction history, z_{t}=f(o_{1:t},a_{1:t-1}) . This challenge extends beyond recalling what a camera once observed: actions affect the world, failures reveal hidden constraints, and previous experience may alter which strategy is appropriate. Memory must therefore maintain a usable relationship between past evidence and present decisions. This perspective connects embodied memory with state estimation under partial observability and with accounts of autonomous and spatial intelligence centered on constructing and revising internal world state [ 1 , 2 ] . While recent advances in generative foundation models have expanded the perceptual, reasoning, and planning capabilities of embodied agents, the underlying challenges of maintaining and exploiting historical information are longstanding. Earlier research on belief-state planning, spatial mapping, and episodic control established complementary approaches to preserving and using past experience, providing useful foundations for understanding the requirements and limitations of modern embodied memory systems ( e.g., [ 3 , 4 , 5 ] ). Recent work offers increasingly diverse mechanisms for carrying embodied history into action, but their diversity in approach and scope makes comparisons difficult. Recurrent policies compress observations into evolving latent states [ 6 , 7 ] ; event memories select sparse visual evidence [ 8 , 9 ] ; textual stores summarize task progress and constraints [ 10 , 11 , 12 ] ; spatial representations preserve entities across viewpoints [ 13 , 14 , 15 ] ; and experience libraries reuse trajectories or consolidate skills [ 16 , 17 , 18 ] . These designs preserve different information and introduce different failure modes. A compact state may retain a counter while losing an object’s appearance, whereas a detailed visual archive may preserve obsolete location information without recognizing that they may have been superseded. Consequently, architectural sophistication and storage capacity alone provide an incomplete explanation of when memory helps. The useful starting point is the historical variable that a future decision requires and the operations needed to keep that variable valid. This survey organizes embodied memory through two complementary axes: six task requirements and eight architectural design categories. The requirements describe why historical information matters: hidden world state, action-induced state change, task state, delayed-relevance evidence, active belief maintenance, and cross-episode experience. The architectural categories describe how systems retain and use such history: recurrent states, fast weights, persistent visual tokens, event or keyframe stores, textual or symbolic stores, graph or 3D stores, experience or skill representations, and hybrid compositions. These are different analytical levels with a many-to-many relationship; they are not competing counts of memory types. The architectural grouping is a practical comparison framework rather than a partition into mutually exclusive substrates: event selection can operate over visual entries, experience can be stored in text, and hybrids combine several mechanisms, for example. Moreover, evaluation of embodied memory approaches must establish which historical information actually matters in changing behaviour. Long episodes may remain locally solvable, while a brief episode can require memory when an early cue disappears before the decisive action. General embodied benchmarks measure valuable combinations of perception, planning, and control, yet their aggregate success rates do not identify the contribution of history. Dedicated suites such as RMBench [ 19 ] , RoboMME [ 20 ] , and MIKASA-Robo [ 21 ] create more explicit dependencies through counting, occlusion, and delayed recall. Even there, the observation interface, supplied demonstrations, and task-progress scaffolding determine what the agent itself must remember. We therefore examine concrete tasks, the historical variables they require, the outcomes they score, and the alternative explanations that remain. Our analysis distinguishes retaining evidence from revising stale state, and successful reuse of a specific past case from the formation of transferable knowledge. ## 2 What Counts as Embodied Memory?
The term memory is used broadly in embodied AI, ranging from short observation histories and recurrent hidden states to explicit episodic stores, spatial maps, and fast-weight states. These mechanisms all expose a policy to information beyond the instantaneous sensory input, yet they provide different forms of historical accessibility. In this survey, embodied memory is a task-relevant state, latent or explicit, that preserves information from earlier embodied observations, actions, outcomes, or demonstrations and allows it to influence a later decision when the originating evidence is absent from the current observation . We use a generic update-and-use formulation, M_{t}=\mathcal{U}(M_{t-1},o_{t},a_{t-1}),\qquad a_{t}\sim\pi(a_{t}\mid o_{t},g,M_{t}), (1) where M_{t} may be retained historical context, a latent state, a set of retrievable entries, a spatial representation, or an adaptive parameter state. Three properties characterize the resulting design space: persistence , or how long historical information remains behaviorally available; addressability , or whether a specific past event or state can be selectively accessed; and update dynamics , or how interaction changes the retained state. This section establishes the conceptual boundary of embodied memory; method-specific architectural comparisons are deferred to Sec. 4 . 2.1 Current-Observation Policies as No-Persistent-Memory Baselines A useful reference point is a policy whose decision is formed from the current observation and task specification without a dedicated persistent historical state. Generalist robot policies such as RT-1, Diffusion Policy, ACT, OpenVLA, Octo, and \pi_{0}/\pi_{0.5} [ 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 ] provide representative baselines in this regime. They may consume multiple camera views, exploit large pretrained representations, or generate temporally extended action chunks. Those capabilities can produce temporally coherent behavior without preserving task-relevant evidence after it leaves the observation interface.
