# UAV-SwarmAgent: Scalable Multi-UAV Reconnaissance and Task Allocation via Code Generation and Vision-Language Reasoning

## Abstract
Supervising a multi-UAV reconnaissance mission usually means plotting waypoints and watching every drone. We present UAV SwarmAgent, a hierarchical multi-agent system in which an operator states the mission in one natural-language sentence and a ground-station agent writes and executes code that turns it into per-UAV routes. The agent grounds inspection targets in an aerial map with a vision-language model, splits them among N UAVs by balancing route length, and dispatches worker agents that verify incidents with a compact onboard decision model. In simulation over 4 incident types and 3 foundation models, the swarm located the incident in 244 of 255 missions (95.7%). A paired scaling study with one to 5 UAVs shows that flight time follows an Amdahl-type law. The second UAV delivers half of the attainable saving and the speedup is bounded at 1.52x. We discuss what this predictability, and the false alarms we observed, imply for an operator who supervises the swarm, and outline the user study needed to test it.

## 1. Introduction
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) are routinely used for time-critical reconnaissance such as wildfire assessment, search and rescue, and environmental monitoring ( Su et al., 2023 ; Lyu et al., 2023 ) . Adding drones shortens the mission, but it also multiplies the work of the person in charge: each UAV needs waypoints, and each video stream needs attention. Research on supervisory control has long shown that the number of robots one operator can handle is limited by how long a robot can be neglected relative to how long it takes to service it ( Olsen and Goodrich, 2004 ; Crandall et al., 2005 ) , and that workload and situation awareness degrade as teams grow ( Chen and Barnes, 2014 ; Kolling et al., 2016 ) . Large language and vision-language models offer a different division of labour. They can read a satellite image, follow an underspecified request, and write programs that call tools ( Yao et al., 2023 ; Liang et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024 ) . Recent aerial systems use them to navigate from language instructions ( Liu et al., 2023 ; Lee et al., 2024 ) or to generate a single-drone mission from a map ( Sautenkov et al., 2025b ; Sautenkov et al., 2025c ) . What is missing for human-swarm interaction is the step from one instruction to a multi-drone mission whose duration the operator can anticipate. This late-breaking report presents UAV-SwarmAgent, a system in which the operator writes one sentence (“ I need to check the houses; one of them might be on fire ”) and receives the locations of detected incidents with the supporting camera frames (Figure 1 ). Our contributions are: • a two-tier agent architecture in which a ground-station agent plans by writing code and delegates incident verification to one agent per UAV; • a simulation study of 255 missions over 4 incident types and 3 foundation models, with mission-level success and frame-level false alarms reported separately; • a paired scaling study with one to 5 UAVs that yields a simple law for mission time, which an operator can use to decide how many drones to send. We did not involve human participants; the implications for operators in Section 6 are hypotheses for the study we outline.

## 3. System
3.1. Agents UAV-SwarmAgent is built on the open-source smolagents library and has two tiers (Figure 2 ). The Airspace Management Agent (AMA) runs at the ground station and works in a ReAct loop whose actions are short Python programs executed in a sandbox: it infers which structures or areas are worth inspecting, grounds them as waypoints on the archival map, shows them to the operator, and dispatches the swarm. One UAV agent per drone runs the onboard verifier on the camera frames collected along its route. We keep geometry out of the language model’s hands. Route allocation, flight simulation, and delegation are a single tool call, dispatch_swarm , rather than code the model writes itself. In a prototype where the model wrote this code, one model silently rescaled the waypoint coordinates before dispatch, which shifted every route. Fixed tools make the behaviour of the swarm independent of which model plans the mission. 3.2. Grounding inspection targets The AMA sends the map, letterboxed to a 1000\times 1000 grid, to a vision-language model and asks for waypoints. The prompt distinguishes two kinds of targets. Objects such as buildings or vehicles receive one point at their centre. Areas such as open water or forest receive an even spread of points, because one point in the middle of a lake does not make a drone survey it (Figure 4 ). Returned points are mapped back to map pixels; at most 15 are kept. Answers in which several object points lie on one image axis at equal spacing are discarded as fabricated. 3.3. Allocating routes Waypoints are assigned to N UAVs that start and land at one base station. Because the mission ends when the last drone returns, we balance the longest route rather than the total distance: waypoints are taken farthest-first and each goes to the UAV whose closed route stays shortest after receiving it; each UAV then visits its waypoints in nearest-neighbour order. Mission flight time is the makespan (1) T_{\mathrm{flight}}=\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,N\}}\frac{g\,L_{k}}{v}, where L_{k} is the length of route k in pixels, g=1.35 m/px is the ground sampling distance of the maps, and v=6 m/s is the cruise speed. 3.4. Verifying incidents onboard Each UAV carries a nadir camera with a 128\times 128 px footprint (about 170 m on the ground). A frame is scored every 16 m along the route by Valen-Preview-0923, a 2B-parameter vision-language decision model that returns the probability of “yes” for a scenario-specific question (for example, whether flames or smoke are visible) in a single forward pass, without generating text. At this size the verifier can run locally on the drone, on an embedded NVIDIA Jetson computer; in our experiments it ran on a laptop GPU. A frame is reported when the probability exceeds a threshold \tau set per scenario (0.55–0.74). Frames of all UAVs are batched on one GPU. Reported frames return to the AMA with their coordinates.

## 4. Evaluation Setup
Scenarios and maps. We use satellite maps of rural settlements derived from the UAV-VLA benchmark ( Sautenkov et al., 2025b ) , each covering about 1.4\times 1.4 km, and five coastal scenes collected from public web map imagery. Every mission pairs an archival reference map, on which targets are grounded, with a target map of the same area that contains the incident and supplies the camera frames (Figure 3 ); grounding never uses the target map. Incidents are synthetic and composited into the target map: flames on one building (structure fire, 30 maps), a burn scar with a flame front and a smoke plume inside a forest (wildfire, 5 maps), vehicles stopped at a junction (vehicle collision, 5 maps), and a dark slick on coastal water (oil spill, 5 maps). The operator request is one fixed sentence per scenario and never names a location. Models. The AMA and the UAV agents use one of 3 models through an OpenAI-compatible gateway: gemini-3.8-flash , gpt-6.1-sol , or qwen3.8-max . The onboard verifier is the same in all runs and executes locally on a laptop GPU (NVIDIA RTX 4050, 6 GB). Protocol. The scaling study runs the structure-fire scenario on all 30 maps with N=1,\dots,5 UAVs using gemini-3.8-flash (150 missions). The design is paired: every swarm size sees the same maps and the same base station, placed at a random point of the map border per map. The cross-model study fixes N=3 and runs every scenario with every model (135 missions, of which the 30 Gemini fire missions are shared with the scaling study). In total we report 255 missions. For the collision and oil-spill scenarios the verifier questions were later refined and the Gemini missions repeated; Table 1 gives the precision of that repeat and the mission counts of the first runs. Measures. A mission is successful when at least one reported frame shows the incident, that is, the incident centre lies inside the frame’s 128\times 128 px camera footprint. Wildfires and oil spills are larger than the footprint, so for them the frame only has to overlap the area changed by the incident. Precision is the share of a mission’s reported frames that satisfy the same criterion. Flight time is the simulated makespan of Eq. ( 1 ). Agent time is the wall-clock time of planning, simulation, and onboard inference. Total time is their sum, a conservative figure because inference would overlap with flight on a real drone. Analysis. Speedups are geometric means of per-map ratios T(1)/T(N) . Confidence intervals are 95% percentile intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of maps ( Efron and Tibshirani, 1994 ) .
