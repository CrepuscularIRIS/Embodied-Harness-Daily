# Alpha: Systematic Fault Injection and Robustness Evaluation for LLM-Driven Embodied Manipulation

## Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated into embodied robotic planning, yet standard benchmarks primarily evaluate end-to-end task completion in nominal environments, obscuring failure origins across the execution stack. We present Alpha, a systematic fault-injection and robustness-evaluation framework for LLM-driven manipulation in simulation. Alpha injects calibrated faults across perception (sensor lag), actuation (grip slip), kinematics (unreachable targets), and planning (planner-output corruption), alongside concurrent conditions. To separate physical vulnerabilities from planner sensitivities, we introduce a matched methodology pairing an LLM agent (Llama-3 via Ollama) against a deterministic Scripted Oracle planner across a balanced 150-episode matrix under identical seeds, geometries, and safety criteria. Across the matrix, the Oracle achieves 64.0% (48/75) success while the LLM achieves 44.0% (33/75) (paired McNemar p = 3.01 x 10^-4). Crucially, excluding planner-output corruption, performance across all physical and kinematic conditions is identical: 55.0% (33/60) for both planners with zero discordant pairs (McNemar p = 1.00). This demonstrates that physical actuator slip, kinematic reach boundaries, and perceptual staleness affect deterministic controllers and LLM planners equivalently. The performance gap is driven entirely by planner-output corruption (100% Oracle vs. 0% LLM), isolating the prompt-to-JSON serialization and validation interface rather than a cognitive reasoning deficit. We also audit 169 historical pilot attempts for transparency.

## I Introduction
Embodied artificial intelligence systems increasingly combine probabilistic large language model (LLM) reasoning with physical robotic execution stacks [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . In such systems, high-level task planners translate natural-language objectives and perceptual scene descriptions into structured action plans (e.g., approach, grasp, transfer, and release). While recent benchmarks have demonstrated impressive nominal task-completion rates across multi-step household and industrial manipulation tasks [ 4 , 5 ] , these evaluations overwhelmingly measure aggregate end-to-end success in nominal, unperturbed environments. Consequently, when an embodied agent fails, existing evaluation protocols rarely localize the failure to its causal origin: did the failure stem from noisy or delayed sensor observations, physical actuator slip, kinematically infeasible joint commands, syntax errors at the serialization boundary, or flawed semantic reasoning? In real-world deployments, autonomous robots routinely encounter hardware degraded states, communication latencies, grasp disturbances, and mechanical limits [ 10 , 11 ] . Understanding how embodied LLM architectures degrade under controlled, reproducible faults is essential before these agents can be safely deployed in safety-critical human environments. Controlled fault injection provides a principled methodology for answering these questions. In distributed software engineering and cloud infrastructure, chaos engineering and active fault injection systematically subject running systems to synthetic faults to observe failure signatures and evaluate automated containment boundaries [ 16 , 17 ] . However, translating this methodology to embodied robotic agents introduces fundamental challenges: physical simulation dynamics, closed-loop contact mechanics, non-deterministic language generation, and complex API interfaces must all be monitored synchronously without confounding the agent’s internal control logic. Furthermore, evaluating an LLM planner in isolation leaves open a critical counterfactual question: is a given failure caused by an intrinsic limitation of the language model, or is it an unavoidable physical consequence of the injected disturbance that would equally thwart any competent controller? Without a strictly matched baseline, environmental physics failures (such as a slippery block falling out of a gripper) are easily conflated with planner cognitive failures. To address these challenges, this paper presents Alpha , a reproducible fault-injection and robustness-evaluation harness for LLM-driven embodied manipulation in simulation. Alpha couples a modular middleware fault wrapper ( ChaosRoboticsWrapper ) with a high-rate validated safety monitor, structured telemetry logging, and an explicit counterfactual evaluation protocol. Alpha injects calibrated faults at four distinct boundaries of a manipulation pipeline: (1) sensor lag at the perception layer, (2) grip slip at the actuation layer, (3) unreachable inverse kinematics (IK) at the kinematic execution layer, and (4) planner-output corruption at the planning/serialization layer, in addition to multi-fault concurrent conditions. To decouple environmental dynamics from planner vulnerabilities, we introduce a matched LLM-vs-Scripted-Oracle methodology . Under this protocol, an LLM agent powered by Llama-3 [ 20 ] and a deterministic Scripted Oracle planner are evaluated across an identical balanced matrix of 150 episodes (75 per planner), sharing identical random seeds, scene spawn coordinates, physical parameters, fault formulas, and safety-incident thresholds. The primary empirical finding of this paper is that across all physical, kinematic, and perceptual perturbations (F1, F2, F3, and Concurrent F1+F2), the LLM agent and the Scripted Oracle perform identically : both achieve exactly 55.0% task success across 60 seed-matched episodes with zero discordant pairs. Physical actuator slip degrades both agents with identical monotonic decay, perceptual lag is accommodated within the grasp tolerance, and kinematic offsets prevent goal placement for both controllers. The overall difference between the two systems (64.0% Oracle vs. 44.0% LLM across the full 150-episode matrix) is driven entirely by F4 planner-output corruption, where text serialization breaks Pydantic schema validation. We show that this divergence isolates the serialization and schema-validation sensitivity of the prompt-to-JSON execution pipeline , rather than an intrinsic defect in LLM spatial or physical reasoning. Alpha structures autonomous robotic execution around a modular Brain/Body design pattern, instrumented by an external fault wrapper and an autonomous safety monitor, as illustrated in Fig. 1 . III-A Execution Pipeline and Information Flow The complete data flow of an execution episode proceeds across six sequential stages: 1. Scene Observation and Perception Hook: The physical simulator ( PyBullet DIRECT mode) instantiates table surfaces and manipulators. The ground-truth block positions are captured as a snapshot dictionary: \mathcal{S}=\{c:(x_{c},y_{c},z_{c})\mid c\in\{\text{red},\text{green},\text{blue}\}\}. (1) Before reaching the planner, this state is intercepted by wrap_observation() , which maintains an internal ring buffer of historical state snapshots. If sensor lag (F1) is active, a delayed historical snapshot \mathcal{S}_{\text{stale}} is returned instead of the fresh observation. 2. Planner Action Generation: The active planner receives a task specification \mathcal{T}=(\text{block},\text{place\_zone}) and the observation dictionary. The planner generates an action plan consisting of sequential sub-goals: MOVE_TO (hover position), MOVE_TO (block grasp coordinate), GRIP , MOVE_TO (lift hover), MOVE_TO (place zone), and RELEASE . 3. Planner Output Interception and Schema Validation: In the LLM pipeline, the generated plan is serialized as a JSON string. The intercept_planner_output() hook intercepts this text stream. If planner-output corruption (F4) is active, required keys are removed or syntax corruption is injected. The resulting payload is validated against a Pydantic schema ( ActionPlan.validate_raw_plan ). If schema validation fails, the episode aborts cleanly by yielding an empty action plan, preventing malformed targets from reaching the robot controller. The Scripted Oracle bypasses JSON serialization, generating internal domain objects directly. 4. Control Layer and Kinematic Interception: Validated action plans are executed by ControlService .
