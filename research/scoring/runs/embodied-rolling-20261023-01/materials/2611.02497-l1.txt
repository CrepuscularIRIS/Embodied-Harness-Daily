# RoboColosseum: Evaluating Physical Intelligence at Scale In-The-Wild

## Abstract
General-purpose robot policies must work across robots, tasks, and environments, yet the variation that makes physical trials informative also makes them difficult to reproduce. We introduce RoboColosseum, a distributed evaluation framework that combines unified policy serving and standardized protocols across a network of locally operated robots in diverse environments. Our initial study evaluates five vision-language-action (VLA) policies and three frontier AI agents across four embodiments (SO-101, Franka, Bimanual-YAM, and G1 Humanoid). The Open Track compares models without task-specific adaptation in blinded, pairwise trials under matched local conditions. The Fine-tune Track measures how well VLA policies adapt to 20 tasks across all four embodiments when each policy receives the same 100 demonstrations per task. Across 5,699 pairwise Open Track comparisons at 124 sites, frontier agents rival specialized VLA policies, while all systems struggle to complete open-world tasks reliably. After fine-tuning, GR00T N1.7 leads on SO-101 and pi0.5 leads on Franka and Bimanual-YAM. Policy rankings show weak agreement across embodiment-specific task suites (Kendall W = 0.24). Open Track rankings poorly predict relative performance after fine-tuning, with substantial rank reversals on all three embodiments shared between tracks. Measuring partial progress reveals differences that task success alone misses. On the G1 Humanoid, no policy completes any of the five tasks, yet average progress ranges from 13.3% to 23.2% across policies. Open Track rankings stabilize as trials accumulate, and active matchmaking achieves comparable ranking agreement with substantially fewer physical trials than uniform sampling. These results show how distributed physical evaluation can reveal where robot policies generalize, how well they adapt to new tasks, and how far they progress on tasks they fail to complete.

## 1 Introduction
You don’t know what you don’t know. — Socrates, Philosopher Recent advances in robot learning are shifting the field from policies tailored to individual tasks and platforms toward general-purpose models ( Fang et al., 2026 ; Team et al., 2025 ; Intelligence et al., 2025 ; NVIDIA et al., 2025 ; Lee et al., 2025 ) trained across diverse tasks, environments, and embodiments ( O’Neill et al., 2024 ; Khazatsky et al., 2024 ) . Although compelling demonstrations from leading robotics companies highlight the potential of these models ( Dyna Robotics, 2026 ; Sunday Robotics, 2025 ; Skild AI Team, 2025 ; Generalist AI, 2026 ) , establishing their reliability under real-world deployment conditions remains a fundamental challenge. In language and vision, large-scale benchmarks incorporating human preferences on real-world tasks have helped drive progress by providing a shared basis for assessing model capabilities relevant to end users ( Zheng et al., 2023 ; Chiang et al., 2024a ; Huang et al., 2024 ) . Robot policies, by contrast, operate through closed-loop interactions with the physical world, where each action shapes the conditions for what follows. Evaluating them reliably is therefore difficult, especially at scale and in the varied, uncontrolled settings of the real world. Measuring progress requires understanding what robots can consistently accomplish across these conditions. Such assessments are difficult to standardize because outcomes depend jointly on the policy, robot setup, initial conditions, and surrounding environment ( Vincent et al., 2024 ) . Even nominally identical tasks can differ in object placement, camera configuration, lighting, and physical properties, making it difficult to attribute performance differences to policy capability. Physical evaluation must therefore reconcile two requirements: sufficient control to support meaningful comparisons and sufficient environmental diversity to assess robustness beyond a particular setup. Simulation supports scalable evaluation through repeatable resets and controlled variation in tasks and environments ( James et al., 2020 ; Tao et al., 2025 ) . However, differences in sensing, calibration, dynamics, and visual appearance can limit how well simulated performance predicts real-world outcomes, making physical validation essential ( Li et al., 2024 ; Pumacay et al., 2024 ; Kim et al., 2026 ) . Achieving comparable scale with physical robots requires substantial labor for setup, resets, hardware maintenance, and trial execution. Consequently, real-world studies often rely on relatively few rollouts, leaving performance estimates sensitive to trial selection and experimental variability ( Kress-Gazit et al., 2024 ; Vincent et al., 2024 ) . Distributing evaluation across sites broadens environmental coverage ( Khazatsky et al., 2024 ; Atreya et al., 2025 ) , but efforts that depend on students and volunteers are difficult to sustain and scale. Running physical trials requires continued staffing, equipment maintenance, and coordination across sites, often alongside competing research commitments. Sustained evaluation therefore requires dedicated operators and shared physical infrastructure, supported by protocols for repeating trials, matching local conditions, and verifying whether measured performance can be reproduced. We introduce RoboColosseum , a distributed, multi-embodiment benchmark for evaluating general-purpose robot policies in the wild. It connects geographically distributed sites through unified evaluation and policy-serving infrastructure, with standardized hardware configurations, task definitions, and evaluation protocols. The benchmark initially supports four embodiments Bimanual-YAM, SO-101, Franka, and Unitree G1 and evaluates eight foundation models: five vision-language-action (VLA) policies and three frontier LLMs. Two complementary tracks assess adaptation and generalization. The Fine-tune Track measures performance on predefined semantic tasks after adaptation using a fixed budget of task-specific demonstrations. The Open Track evaluates zero-shot generalization across diverse real-world tasks and environments without task-specific adaptation. Together, they enable systematic assessment of physical intelligence across heterogeneous embodiments. With a growing fleet of over 100 robots across the United States, United Kingdom, Singapore, Malaysia, and China, operated by full-time evaluators, RoboColosseum aims to make real-world evaluation scalable and sustainable. This shared infrastructure provides a lens into the capabilities and limitations of robot foundation models, enabling the research community to investigate fundamental scientific questions through systematic physical trials. To broaden access, we pledge 10,000 hours of free evaluation to academic labs and partners. ## 3 RoboColosseum Benchmark
We present RoboColosseum , a distributed real-world benchmarking framework for systematically evaluating the generalization and adaptation capabilities of general-purpose robot policies across heterogeneous embodiments. A central contribution is the design of the physical infrastructure and operational protocols required to sustain large-scale, distributed robot evaluation. As illustrated in Figure 1 , the framework integrates geographically distributed robot evaluation sites, shared policy-serving infrastructure, and standardized evaluation protocols into a unified benchmarking system. RoboColosseum currently supports four robot embodiments and eight AI systems, comprising five vision-language-action (VLA) policies and three API-accessible AI agents. The benchmark comprises two complementary tracks: an Open Track for evaluation without task-specific adaptation and a Fine-tune Track for evaluation following controlled task-specific adaptation. Together, these tracks assess out-of-the-box capabilities and the ability to acquire new manipulation skills from demonstrations. 3.1 Physical Infrastructure and Operational Protocols RoboColosseum currently supports four robot embodiments spanning diverse costs, morphologies, and control complexities: a low-cost single arm, a bimanual system, a research-grade arm, and a walking humanoid ( Table 1 ). Evaluating the same model families across these embodiments tests whether findings generalize across hardware platforms, a question single-embodiment benchmarks cannot address.
