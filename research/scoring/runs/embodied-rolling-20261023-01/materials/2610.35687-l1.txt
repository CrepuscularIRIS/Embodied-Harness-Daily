# TrySee: Modular Perception-Enhanced VLM Grounding for Closed-Loop Robot Task Planning in Specialized Domains

## Abstract
Vision Language Models (VLMs) have shown impressive capabilities in high-level reasoning and task planning for robots. However, specialized domains remain a challenge for VLMs' image interpretation and grounding abilities. We introduce TrySee, a modular closed-loop robot task planning pipeline integrating general-purpose VLM reasoning with domain-specific perception and established robotics components. The system consists of an inherently reactive cyclic planning pipeline, augmented with domain-specific modular vision preprocessing, to provide online grounding. Finally, a dynamic preprocessing selection mechanism contributes to the scalability of the pipeline. The system requires neither VLM fine-tuning nor end-to-end demonstrations and adapts through replaceable perception modules and motion planning. The approach is evaluated on planning tasks across 5 widely different domains, including a real-world robot disassembly task. The field tests in a brownfield environment demonstrate the approach's practicality in integrating VLM planning into specialized robotic systems with limited domain-specific training data.

## I Introduction
The idea of controlling robots with natural language has long been a staple of science fiction. However, accomplishing even simple tasks, such as “Clean up the table.” , requires a complex understanding of the world and the robot’s capabilities, as well as drawing on many of the core challenges in robotics, such as perception, manipulation, and planning. With the recent advances in Vision Language Models (VLMs) [ 1 ] [ 2 ] , significant progress has been made in enabling robot reasoning and planning by leveraging vast amounts of real-world knowledge [ 3 ] [ 4 ] . While VLM-based systems have produced impressive results for tasks in everyday environments (such as offices or homes), they still struggle in more specialized domains, including industrial and scientific applications [ 5 ] [ 6 ] . This is attributable to these domains being underrepresented in the VLMs’ training data. The resulting performance gap is a barrier to the more widespread adoption of VLMs in these fields, in which data collection and annotation is often particularly expensive and time-consuming. A fundamental requirement for VLMs to generate effective robot task plans is the ability to ground their language understanding in the robot’s perception and action capabilities. In other words, the objects and features comprising the robot’s environment must be linked to the semantics used in the given task, and these, in turn, must be linked to the concrete movements and poses necessary to interact with them. General-purpose VLMs often fail at the first step for recondite objects, and their image-level outputs alone do not provide sufficiently reliable geometric targets for robot execution. A practical planning system therefore requires explicit interfaces connecting domain-specific perception, VLM-level semantics, and the robot’s action capabilities. In this work, we tackle the challenge of VLM grounding in specialized domains by presenting a modular pipeline for VLM-based robot task planning. Rather than fine-tuning the model or replacing the entire existing robot software stack, TrySee compensates the general-purpose VLM’s limitations in object recognition and robot pose generation by connecting its reasoning abilities with replaceable domain-specific perception modules and established motion-planning components. This allows the pipeline to be adaptable to different domains and tasks, through targeted perception models without requiring end-to-end robot demonstrations. In TrySee, the VLM incrementally generates and verifies short-horizon action sequences, while the perception modules provide semantic object labels, visual masks, and executable target poses. This provides online grounding that is continuously updated as the environment changes. For object-dense environments, the VLM can request preprocessing only for the information relevant to the current verification step. Our approach enables the VLM to generate task plans for a variety of domains. We evaluate the pipeline using tasks across 5 vastly different domains (see Fig. 1 ), including e-waste recycling, a workshop environment, and an agricultural setting. The focus is on complex, multi-step tasks. In order to underline the real-world applicability of our approach, we also implement the pipeline on a real robot and evaluate it in an industrial recycling environment.

## III TrySee
This work aims to improve the high-level task planning capabilities of robots for tasks from specialized domains based on instructions in natural language. In this context, a specialized domain is defined as comprising a specific set of objects, features, or components that are not found in general-purpose datasets and thus are unlikely to be recognized by the VLM out-of-the box. In particular, the focus is on improving the practical applicability of VLM-based robotic planning systems. An overview of the proposed approach is shown in Fig. 2 . The alternating use of these two modes forms the basis of the cyclic planning pipeline . On the one hand, the VLM is used to generate the next planning step based on the task at hand and the current state of the environment. To this end, it is provided with the task description, the list of actions the robot can perform (each of which represents a basic motion primitive, as seen in Table I , which can be combined with others to form more complex behaviors), current images of the environment, and the list of interactable objects and points of interest found therein. In turn, the VLM generates a Thought (a natural language description of the next step to be executed), a sequence of actions from the toolbox, as well as the goal it aims to achieve with the generated sequence (in natural language). The Thought provides an explicit rationale alongside the structured action output. The division of the overall plan into smaller steps is entirely self-directed by the VLM, which is instructed to generate only one major interaction with its surroundings (e. g., picking up an object) at a time. Additionally, the same VLM is also used to verify whether the generated plan was executed successfully. This is accomplished by comparing the goal generated in the planning step against current images of the environment as well as images from directly before the execution of the plan. The model first describes the images provided before generating an Observation stating whether or not the expected goal was achieved. The collection of past Observations comprises the memory of the VLM, which is also appended to its input in subsequent planning steps. The division of the pipeline into explicit planning and verification steps is a key design choice. It allows the VLM to focus on each step individually, without being overwhelmed by having to perform both of these complex tasks simultaneously. Separate prompts are used for planning and verification so that each invocation has a constrained objective and output format. For the purposes of the proposed pipeline, the VLM is treated as a black box, with the models not being fine-tuned or adapted to the specific task at hand. This separates domain adaptation from the choice of reasoning model and allows newer VLMs to be integrated without modifying the perception or execution interfaces. Additionally, while fine-tuning the VLM might improve the model’s innate scene understanding capabilities, external modules would still be required to provide the necessary action grounding.
