===ABSTRACT===
Foundation models can reason about complex physical tasks, yet translating this intelligence into reliable robot execution remains challenging. We argue that a critical, often overlooked factor is the robot-use interface (RUI), which determines how agents express and execute physical actions. To systematically study its impact, we introduce RUIBench, comprising 30 manipulation tasks across five suites spanning world knowledge, in-context learning, discovery, dynamic interaction, and contact-rich manipulation. We further develop RUIKit, a unified framework that crosses six action abstractions with two interaction forms, yielding 13 interfaces under shared perception and control. In simulation, changing only the RUI shifts a frontier agent's success rate from 45% to 81%, and that of a fine-tuned 4B model from 13% to 47%. Our analysis reveals that effective interfaces reduce unnecessary decisions while preserving essential feedback, with their benefits shaped by agent behavior and physical uncertainty. We further uncover a verification gap, where agents frequently declare success despite observable failures, revealing the need for task-level verification beyond individual actions. Together, these findings establish RUI design as a critical factor in embodied agent reliability.

===INTRO===
Foundation models are moving from understanding the physical world to acting in it [ 74 , 2 , 39 ] . While vision-language-action models learn to predict robot actions [ 7 , 43 , 6 ] , recent agentic systems enable general-purpose models to control robots through tool calls and executable programs [ 52 , 76 , 15 , 27 , 45 , 16 ] . This introduces a critical design choice beyond the model itself: how should an agent interact with a robot? We define the robot-use interface (RUI) as the actions an agent can command and how they are organized for execution. From low-level motion commands [ 3 , 4 ] and image-grounded points [ 25 , 57 ] to semantic skills [ 1 , 37 ] and executable programs [ 52 , 71 ] , the RUI determines what the agent must decide, what can be delegated to execution, and when control returns to the agent. This distinction becomes critical in complex physical tasks. These tasks require agents to gather missing information, react to dynamic events, and correct errors through physical feedback. An interface that demands a new model decision for every small movement can make execution costly and error-prone, while one that provides insufficient feedback may fail when the world deviates from the plan. Thus, reliable execution depends not only on what an agent knows, but also on how decisions and feedback are distributed between the agent and the robot. However, existing embodied benchmarks compare models under fixed interfaces [ 47 , 82 , 18 , 84 , 23 ] , while interface studies typically examine selected design choices [ 27 , 64 , 68 ] . Consequently, the impact of interface design remains poorly understood. In our experiments, changing only the RUI shifts Claude Code’s success rate from 45% to 81%, far exceeding the average performance gap between two frontier agents ( Fig. 1 a). This raises our central question: What makes a good robot-use interface? To answer this question, we introduce RUIBench, a benchmark combining 30 agentic manipulation tasks with a controlled evaluation protocol. These tasks go beyond simple pick-and-place, requiring agents to reason, acquire information through interaction, and adapt to changing physical conditions. They span five suites: world knowledge, in-context learning, discovery, dynamic interaction, and contact-rich manipulation. The protocol limits agents to RGB-D observations and robot states, keeps the environment evolving during model reasoning, and evaluates task success independently of the agents’ claims. We further develop RUIKit, a unified framework that disentangles two design choices: action abstraction , which determines what an agent can command, and interaction form , which determines how actions are organized and executed. RUIKit crosses six action abstractions with two interaction forms to instantiate 12 interfaces, plus a free-choice interface, all within a shared harness where only the RUI varies ( Fig. 1 ). Across frontier and small models in simulation and 650 real-robot episodes with frontier agents, we find that effective RUIs reduce unnecessary decisions while preserving essential feedback ( Fig. 1 b), which also makes them easier for small models to learn ( Fig. 1 d). Fine-grained interaction can fragment a clear plan into excessive model decisions, a failure we call execution fragmentation . Composing actions into programs reduces this burden when agents effectively exploit multi-step execution and intermediate feedback. However, real-world execution changes which interfaces are effective, as physical uncertainty demands more opportunities for local correction ( Fig. 1 c). We further uncover a verification gap : agents frequently declare success despite observable failures, while checking individual actions does not guarantee task completion. Together, these findings highlight the importance of allocating decisions, feedback, and verification at appropriate levels. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • Agentic benchmark. We introduce RUIBench with 30 manipulation tasks across five suites and a controlled evaluation protocol for systematically studying RUIs. • Unified framework. We develop RUIKit, spanning 13 interfaces across six action abstractions and two interaction forms under shared perception and control. • Empirical insights. We reveal how decision burden, physical feedback, and task-level verification shape embodied performance across agents and environments.
