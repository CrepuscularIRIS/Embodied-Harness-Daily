# When, Where, and What to Correct: Human-Guided Adaptive Residual Correction for Robot Policies

## Abstract
Imitation policies that reliably execute most of a manipulation trajectory can still fail in localized regions requiring last-mile precision. Updating the full policy or exploring residual actions everywhere treats this local failure as a global learning problem. We introduce Adaptive Residual Correction (ARC), a lightweight post-training module that uses reinforcement learning (RL) to learn a residual correction on top of a frozen policy while using sparse human corrections to structure autonomous exploration. Rather than treating human corrections as imitation targets, ARC uses pre-intervention states to determine 'when' residual control should become active and correction geometry to determine 'where' in residual action space autonomous refinement should explore; task reward determines 'what' residual is ultimately executed. Across three precision-manipulation tasks on a real robot, ARC improves average task success from 33.3% to 75.0%. The human corrections define task-dependent 2-3 dimensional subspaces of the seven-joint residual space that retain 96.0-97.7% of squared correction magnitude. Together, these results show that sparse human corrections can localize and constrain autonomous post-training without requiring the observed correction itself to serve as the final action target.

## 1 Introduction
Modern imitation-learning policies can acquire broad manipulation competence from demonstrations, including complex closed-loop visuomotor behaviors [ 14 , 3 ] . Yet even a policy that reliably reaches the relevant interaction can remain brittle to small execution errors and distribution shift, particularly when success depends on a short precision-critical segment of the task [ 14 , 2 ] . In this regime, collecting substantially more demonstrations or post-training the entire policy treats a localized failure as a global learning problem. Residual learning instead keeps the base policy fixed and learns only the additional control required to correct its remaining failures [ 2 , 13 , 1 , 6 ] . Residual learning is most useful when the correction itself is similarly targeted. Acting throughout the trajectory can degrade behavior the base policy already performs well, while arbitrary action-space exploration wastes interaction on unlikely motions. Sparse human corrections provide structure for both problems. During an otherwise autonomous rollout, the human intervenes only when needed by adding a residual adjustment to the base-policy action; we refer to these intermittent adjustments as human corrections . Human-in-the-loop methods show that sparse interventions can efficiently guide policy improvement [ 8 , 15 ] , while intervention timing itself carries information beyond the corrective trajectory [ 12 ] . At autonomous deployment, no human is present: the residual policy must recognize robot-controlled states in which a human would soon have corrected , rather than states already altered by a correction. Likewise, the coordinated multi-joint geometry of the correction reveals which residual directions the human actually uses. Together, these signals localize the temporal and action-space support in which residual learning should operate. The human correction itself, however, need not be the final action an autonomous policy should reproduce. Recent methods use corrective actions directly, or through latent representations, as supervision for learned steering policies [ 9 , 7 ] , but a correction is chosen to recover from the particular state encountered during intervention, and directly imitating it does not encode whether its downstream consequence leads to task success [ 15 ] . For example, a locally sensible placement correction may still disturb another object and cause the task to fail. This distinction motivates Adaptive Residual Correction (ARC) , a lightweight plug-in module for improving a frozen base policy. During otherwise autonomous rollouts, the human provides sparse residual corrections only when needed. From approximately 8–10 such rollouts per task, ARC learns a pre-intervention gate identifying WHEN residual control should become active and a low-dimensional basis of the seven-joint residual action space restricting WHERE autonomous refinement searches. These correction rollouts are used only to learn the gate and corrective basis and are not added to the RL replay buffer. ARC then collects separate autonomous experience within this learned temporal and action-space support, and reinforcement learning determines WHAT correction improves task outcome. The base policy remains frozen and its original demonstrations are not revisited. Relative to residual-RL methods [ 2 , 13 , 1 , 6 ] , ARC restricts both when the residual is active and where exploration occurs; relative to correction-supervised methods [ 9 , 7 ] , it uses human residuals to define the support of autonomous learning rather than as targets for the final residual actor. We evaluate ARC on three precision-manipulation tasks on a real robot: aligning and stacking industrial trays, inserting a marker into a narrow groove, and stacking cups into a fixed receiver. After two autonomous collect–reward–update rounds, ARC improves average task success from 33.3\% to 75.0\% . Experiments further show that pre-intervention states yield a gate that transfers to autonomous execution, human residuals define stable low-dimensional corrective supports, and directly predicting the human correction produces residual behavior different from reward-driven ARC even when supervision is restricted to the same corrective subspace.

## 3 Adaptive Residual Correction (ARC)
3.1 Problem Formulation Let o_{t}\in\mathcal{O} denote the robot observation and a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{7} the seven-joint arm action at time t ; the gripper remains controlled by the base policy. We study post-training of a pretrained policy \pi_{B} , with a_{t}^{B}=\pi_{B}(o_{t}) , using sparse human corrections while keeping \pi_{B} frozen. During otherwise autonomous rollouts, a human uses a SpaceMouse, a six-degree-of-freedom handheld controller, to add a residual adjustment to the base action rather than replacing it. If a_{t}^{H} is the resulting action, the human residual is r_{t}^{H}=a_{t}^{H}-a_{t}^{B} . A correction contains information beyond this instantaneous action: its onset indicates when the base trajectory is becoming unreliable, while its coordinated joint residual identifies useful corrective directions. ARC uses both signals to restrict autonomous learning instead of treating r_{t}^{H} as the final policy target. We write the adapted action as a_{t}=a_{t}^{B}+g_{t}r_{t} , where g_{t}\in\{0,1\} is a binary gate and r_{t}=B_{H}z_{t} is the residual action. Here, B_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times d_{H}} is a human-derived corrective basis and z_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{H}} is its coefficient vector. Thus, B_{H}z_{t} maps the low-dimensional coefficients back to the seven-joint residual action space. Residual components operate on x_{t}=[\phi_{B}(I_{t}),q_{t},a_{t}^{B}] , where I_{t} is the image observation, \phi_{B}(I_{t}) is the frozen ACT visual representation, and q_{t} is proprioception. 3.2 WHEN: Localizing Residual Refinement A classifier trained on states during an active human correction may rely on states already altered by the human. ARC instead predicts an upcoming correction from robot-controlled states. Using only the first state of each correction bout would provide one positive example per bout; we instead use a short preceding window to provide more positive examples and represent a small region where correction is imminent rather than one exact frame. For each correction bout beginning at t_{0} , the preceding L=5 states are positive examples; correction-active frames are excluded and sufficiently distant robot-controlled states form negatives. We train a binary gate classifier g_{\psi} that outputs p_{t}=g_{\psi}(x_{t})=P(y_{t}=1\mid x_{t}) , where y_{t}=1 denotes a correction beginning within the next L states. Episode-level splits prevent nearby frames from the same rollout appearing in both training and validation. At deployment, p_{t} is thresholded and passed through temporal hysteresis to produce the binary gate g_{t} used in a_{t}=a_{t}^{B}+g_{t}r_{t} . The probability threshold is chosen from leave-one-episode-out predictions using Youden’s J statistic, while the on/off durations are fixed from human correction timing statistics. These values are not tuned using autonomous deployment rollouts.
