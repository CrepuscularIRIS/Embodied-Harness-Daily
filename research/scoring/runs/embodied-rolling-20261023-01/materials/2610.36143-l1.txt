# Program-Level Adversarial Co-Adaptive Self-Play for Robotic Security

## Abstract
Coding agents can adapt robotic software by revising executable programs, but successful responses to an evolving opponent may not remain effective. We present a framework for program-level adversarial co-adaptive self-play in which coding agents generate attacks and defenses from tested opponent code and mission feedback, without model fine-tuning. Environment-specific interfaces support external programs for autonomous driving and SDK-level defenses for humanoid locomotion. Two eight-generation case studies reveal concrete software responses: a driving defender introduces direct-actuation recovery, while a humanoid defender revises incoming-state validation. These responses provide local task recovery but do not establish sustained robustness. The framework makes such adaptation and limitations observable through executable artifacts and task-level evidence.

## 1 Introduction
Robotic cybersecurity must protect physical behavior as well as software access. A disclosed Unitree Go2 vulnerability exposed a DDS programming interface without authentication or authorization, permitting a network-adjacent attacker to install code later executed through a controller binding [ 12 ] . Such risks motivate proactive testing: develop attacks and candidate defenses before malicious exploitation, then evaluate their consequences for an ongoing robotic task. Coding agents provide a way to study this interaction through executable programs. Prior work connects generated code to embodied control [ 6 ] , develops executable skills through environmental feedback [ 13 ] , and investigates code-mediated improvement and autonomous cyber co-evolution [ 9 , 7 ] . In this work, we study a system-level question: can agents attack an existing robotic stack and revise defenses effectively as the opponent adapts? Our framework implements program-level adversarial co-adaptive self-play . Attack-generating agents and a shared defense-generating agent are placed around a running robotic system. Programs submitted by agents are checked and frozen, executed on the robotic system, and returned with independently observed outcomes to inform subsequent revisions. Adaptation occurs in software and retained context rather than through arena-driven model fine-tuning. Environment-specific tasks and permissions determine what those programs may change. We study this process in Autoware-CARLA and Unitree G1-MuJoCo. The driving configuration uses fixed attacker roles; the humanoid configuration additionally uses an orchestrator, development-time tests against frozen opponents, and separate clean, attack-only, defense-only, and joint episode executions. The studies reveal concrete program revisions alongside recurrent failures, motivating evaluation beyond a favorable individual match. Our contributions are: • A robotic-security self-play framework with directly competing programs . Independent coding agents revise programs that execute together around an operating autonomy stack through robotic middleware and permitted software modifications. • A mission-grounded evaluation protocol. The humanoid evaluation combines clean, attack-only, defense-only, and repeated joint trials to distinguish attack effectiveness, defense viability, and normalized task recovery. • Two eight-generation case studies of adversarial adaptation. Driving and humanoid experiments trace how agents develop countermeasures and counterattacks in response to each other, revealing targeted recovery alongside persistent failures.

## 3 Methodology
Figure 1 summarizes the program-generation and evaluation loop. A fixed mission focuses adaptation on the opponent’s software. One shared defender faces each attacker separately, so it must accommodate different forms of interference with the same submission. 3.1 Attack-Defense Self-Play Loop Before the self-play loop begins, the arena verifies a clean mission as baseline and captures exposed interfaces for agent context. At each generation, attacker (Red) and defender (Blue) agents develop concurrently in private workspaces. Each receives role-specific context describing the rules, robotic software, retained history, and previous execution feedback. Each Red sees its own preceding evidence and the tested defender; Blue receives the preceding attackers and their match records. Current development workspaces are hidden from opponents. After development, the arena checks submissions and permits up to two automated repairs. Middleware access is assumed, and direct manipulation of the simulator or referee is outside the interaction policy. Environment-specific permissions define the allowed interventions. For driving, both sides use external ROS 2 programs, and Blue may issue direct actuation commands. For the humanoid, Red uses DDS messages, while Blue supplies a monitor and permitted SDK replacements that process inputs before the fixed locomotion policy consumes them. The monitor can also publish commands. Accepted programs are checked, frozen, and evaluated, with one attacker paired with the same generation-specific defense in each joint episode. They execute without live language-model calls or mid-trial edits. An independent referee determines outcomes from simulator ground truth while the results and tested opponent code are returned for subsequent revision. The robot resets between trials. 3.2 Attacker Allocation and Evaluation The driving configuration assigns five fixed specializations: perception, failsafe, planning, control, and chained attacks. In the humanoid configuration, an orchestrator proposes five roles before each generation using the available interfaces, task, and prior feedback. Role identities preserve their histories; unused attackers remain available to Blue. The orchestrator is instructed to retain effective pressure and explore other directions when roles become ineffective or well recovered. Shorter humanoid episodes and up to sixteen parallel slots make more testing practical than the sequential driving setup with per-match Autoware restarts. This supports both unscored development sparring, where agents test candidate programs alone or against frozen prior opponents, and more formal evaluation episodes. Each generation runs one clean, one defense-only, five attack-only, and fifteen joint episodes (three per attacker): 1+1+5+15=22 formal evaluations. 3.3 Mission-Grounded Evaluation Driving reward and revision feedback. Blue’s terminal reward is +1 for reaching the goal, -1 for collision or off-road departure, and 0 for timeout or no progress; Red receives its negative. Timeouts are draws regardless of how far the car travels. Terminal rewards do not capture partial mission recovery, so a separate quality score exposes differences in progress and speed for revision. Let L be the start-to-goal distance and d_{\max} the greatest recorded progress, giving the route fraction p=\text{clip}_{[0,1]}(d_{\max}/L) , set to one on goal completion. Let v_{\max} be peak vehicle speed and v_{\mathrm{ref}} the clean-baseline peak, giving a=\text{clip}_{[0,1]}(v_{\max}/v_{\mathrm{ref}}) . Driving quality is q_{D}=p(0.5+0.5a) , with L=60 m and v_{\mathrm{ref}}=4.5 m/s. Progress rewards task completion, while retained speed discourages a slow defensive takeover and encourages preserving Autoware’s nominal driving behavior. This is a lightweight proxy. Quality informs revision but is not added to the terminal reward. Humanoid quality and reward. A faster fall should not outscore a completed walk, so humanoid quality separates partial progress from completion: q_{H}=\begin{cases}0.5+0.5a,&\text{goal reached},\\ 0.5p,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} (1) Here, p is progress along the 3 m route and a is peak body walking speed relative to the clean baseline. Failed episodes are rewarded for progress; speed distinguishes successful walks. Unlike driving, humanoid evaluation includes attack-only and repeated joint episodes, allowing reward to measure how much attack-induced loss Blue restores. Let q_{C} be the quality in a clean episode, q_{A,k} be the quality in an attack-only episode, and q_{J,k,j} quality in joint episode j : \rho_{k,j}=\text{clip}_{[0,1]}\left(\frac{q_{J,k,j}-q_{A,k}}{q_{C}-q_{A,k}}\right). (2) Thus, \rho=0 means no improvement over attack-only and \rho=1 restores clean quality. For gaps below 0.05, recovery falls back to goal completion: 1 if reached and 0 otherwise. Blue’s reward is the median of the three joint-episode recovery scores; Red receives its complement. The median limits the effect of one lucky success or one failed episode. A matchup is eligible when the attack disrupts the undefended mission, the defense preserves the defense-only mission, and the joint episodes are valid. Blue’s lowest eligible reward guides revision toward its weakest defense.
