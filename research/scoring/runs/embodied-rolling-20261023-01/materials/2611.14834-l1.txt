# Learning Low-Latency Action Verification for Frozen Vision-Language-Action Models

## Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) models have made significant progress in general-purpose robotic manipulation. However, sampled action sequences can be ineffective, while evaluating alternatives with computationally expensive verifiers adds latency at each policy query. In this work, we propose HDH, a lightweight framework that uses hindsight from a frozen VLA's own deployments to improve action verification. The framework has three stages. First, we deploy the policy and collect its autonomous rollouts. We then train a compact action verifier to assign each action chunk a predicted task-success score. Finally, the frozen VLA generates action candidates, and the verifier selects the candidate with the highest score for execution. Across four LIBERO-10 tasks, HDH achieves an average success rate of 82.3\% and on three real-world manipulation tasks, HDH improves average success over the baseline policy by 22.6\%.

## I Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models have achieved state-of-the-art performance on general-purpose manipulation, mapping visual observations and language instructions into robot actions [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] . In deployment, however, the common practice is to sample a single action from the VLA, and execute it on the robot. Existing deployments, thus, do not exploit a fundamental property of generative policies, where the same observation and instruction can yield a full distribution of candidates with substantial variation in execution quality. To exploit this distributional generation capability, a simple test-time solution is to sample multiple candidate action chunks and select the candidate with the highest estimated quality. The question of how to select that action is, however, an open problem. Existing work has considered several heuristic selection strategies, including random selection and candidate evaluation using large vision-language models [ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] . However, we believe that instead of relying on very large external models, the policy’s own deployment experience can provide explicit supervision for this choice. We therefore investigate whether terminal success and failure provide sufficient supervision for candidate ranking and whether a compact verifier can achieve selection performance comparable to that of substantially larger verifiers. To address these questions, we introduce Hindsight Decision Model with History ( HDH ), a lightweight verifier trained on terminal outcomes from autonomous policy rollouts, without expert comparisons, synthetic preferences, or predicted futures. Each training example consists of the current observation, the executed action prefix, and the episode’s terminal outcome. To provide context about task progress, the verifier additionally receives recent observations and executed actions. At deployment, HDH assigns a predicted eventual task-success score to each candidate sampled from the frozen VLA, and the robot executes the highest-scoring prefix. We evaluate HDH in simulation and in the real-world in four dimensions. First, does a verifier trained only from terminal outcomes improve the closed-loop success of a frozen VLA? Second, does it produce candidate rankings that improve selection performance, beyond randomly selecting from the same set? Third, does history-conditioned scoring improve candidate discrimination by revealing task progress? Finally, how does such a compact verifier compare with verifiers based on large vision-language models on task success and inference latency? We find that hindsight supervision alone substantially improves closed-loop manipulation. Across four LIBERO-10 tasks, HDH achieves an average success rate of 82.3%, outperforming both the frozen policy and random selection from the same candidate set. On three real-world manipulation tasks, it raises average success from 44.7% to 67.3% within a real-time control budget. Furthermore, history-conditioned scoring improves candidate ranking, and the compact verifier achieves selection performance comparable to that of much larger verifiers with substantially lower inference cost. Our contributions are thus threefold: • Problem setting. We frame VLA deployment as real-time candidate selection supervised only by the frozen policy’s own rollout outcomes. • Verifier and selection. We introduce a lightweight, history-conditioned verifier for ranking native VLA candidates without updating the policy. • Evaluation. We show in both simulation and real-world experiments that hindsight-supervised selection improves closed-loop manipulation with substantially lower inference cost than large verifiers.

## III Learning Action Verification from Policy Rollouts
An overview of our method is shown in Fig. 2 . First, we collect rollouts from a frozen VLA and use their terminal outcomes to supervise the recorded actions. We train a Hindsight Decision Model (HDM) to score candidate actions using the current observation and proprioceptive state. Our full model, the Hindsight Decision Model with History (HDH), additionally incorporates recent observations and executed actions. During closed-loop control, the frozen VLA samples multiple action candidates, and the verifier selects the candidate with the highest predicted task-success score. III-A Action Chunk Success Estimation Let o_{t} contain the camera observations and proprioception at decision t , and let \ell denote the instruction. A chunk A_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{a}} is generated by sampling from the frozen policy \pi_{\theta} using these inputs and flow-matching noise (the frozen policy’s action-generation noise). The robot executes only U_{t}=A_{t}[0:H] , after which the system samples a new plan using the resulting observation. The verifier context x_{t} is defined to include the current observation and, for the history-conditioned configuration, information from prior observations and executed actions recorded before decision t . The quantity of interest is the probability of eventual task success after U_{t} is executed and subsequent actions are generated by the baseline policy: Q^{\pi_{\theta}}(x_{t},U_{t})=\Pr(y_{e}=1\mid x_{t},U_{t};\pi_{\theta}\text{ thereafter}), (1) where y_{e}\in\{0,1\} denotes terminal episode success. We approximate this quantity as Q_{\phi}(x_{t},U_{t})=1-\sigma(f_{\phi}(x_{t},U_{t})) , where f_{\phi} is a failure logit. Thus, Q_{\phi} represents the predicted eventual task-success score learned from observed rollout outcomes. III-B Learning from Terminal Outcomes Each retained example consists of a decision context, its actually executed prefix, and the terminal outcome of that episode. With failure label y_{e}^{\mathrm{fail}}=1-y_{e} , the simulation objective is \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MC}}=\frac{1}{N_{q}}\sum_{(e,t)}\operatorname{BCEWithLogits}\!\left(f_{\phi}(x_{t},U_{t}),1-y_{e}\right). (2) The same episode return is assigned to all retained queries from that episode. The dataset is partitioned at the episode level, rather than by randomly assigning queries from the same trajectory across splits. Under the current preprocessing convention, queries whose recorded executed length is below H are excluded. This convention prevents supervision from being applied to action rows that were not executed, especially at terminal queries. III-C Action Verifier Architecture The core attention-based scoring path is shown in Fig. 2 . In HDM, frozen backbone features are extracted from two image views (early-layer outputs of the backbone’s SigLIP image pathway) [ 31 , 4 ] . After region pooling, the representation contains an 8\times 8 grid for each view, yielding 128 visual tokens of feature dimension 2048. These features are projected to width 128, with spatial and view embeddings. An eight-dimensional proprioceptive state is projected into one additional token. A separate verifier is used for each task, with a constant task token. The scorer input does not include language-prompt tokens, although the policy remains instruction-conditioned. The scorer input includes the H\times 7 executed prefix, typically H=8 from a length- 10 predicted chunk. Action tokens are formed using a linear action projection and learned temporal embeddings. The architecture applies four-head action self-attention, followed by cross-attention from the action tokens to the observation tokens [ 32 ] , with residual connections, normalization, and a feed-forward network. The failure logit is computed by mean pooling over the action tokens followed by an MLP of widths 128\rightarrow 64\rightarrow 1 . We convert this logit into the task-success score Q_{\phi}(x_{t},U_{t})=1-\sigma(f_{\phi}(x_{t},U_{t})) for candidate selection.
