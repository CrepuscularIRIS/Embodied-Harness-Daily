# When the Simulator Fails, Not the Policy: Auditing Contact Artifacts in Simulated Robot Benchmarks

## Abstract
Simulation benchmarks score robot policies by success rate, and every failed episode counts against the policy. Many of these failures are the simulator's fault, not the policy's; we find the cause and fix it. Testing a vision language action model and a world action model in simulation, we find that the policy often places the object and the simulator then ejects it, and that the benchmark's own scripted demonstrations fail the same way. We present SimuGuard, an auditing plugin for LIBERO, RoboTwin, RoboCasa and RoboDojo. It leaves the evaluation unchanged. At every physics step, it records the state and flags an object that starts moving at a contact faster than the robot's contact or gravity account for. It replays a recorded episode bit for bit, and it reruns the episode with one simulation parameter changed. With it we trace these episodes on RoboTwin to the collision detection of the physics engine: for geometry one to two centimeters apart, the engine reports a penetration of about two centimeters, and the solver throws the object out at several meters per second. The cause is one constant: the distance algorithm discards triangles below a fixed area threshold, which valid triangles fall below at the centimeter scale. With that constant changed and nothing else, episodes with an artifact fall from 12 in 100 to none, and the success rate on five container tasks rises from 89% to 98%. Success rates in simulation thus count failures that the simulator causes. Benchmarks on PhysX 5.3.1 or earlier should rerun in a repaired engine. Where the engine cannot change, they should report the success rate together with the range it spans when every flagged episode fails or passes: 75.8% to 98.5% around a reported 83.1% for LingBot-VA.

## 1 Introduction
Simulation benchmarks give a repeatable way to compare robot policies, from vision language action (VLA) models to world action models ( Brohan et al., 2023a ; Brohan et al., 2023b ; Octo Model Team et al., 2024 ; Kim et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2024 ; Björck et al., 2025 ; Gemini Robotics Team, 2025 ; Li et al., 2026 ) . LIBERO ( Liu et al., 2023 ) , RoboCasa ( Nasiriany et al., 2024 ) and RoboTwin ( Mu et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025 ) fix the tasks and the protocol and treat the simulator as faithful physics. Their success rates record which episodes failed, not why. A failure caused by the simulator’s treatment of contact enters the same count as a failure caused by the policy. Testing a world action model and a VLA in simulation, we find that such failures are frequent: the policy places the object, and the simulator throws it out. In an episode of RoboTwin 2.0’s Place Can Basket, LingBot-VA ( Li et al., 2026 ) places a can inside the basket, resting against its wall. The gripper releases it. After 0.28 s, within a single 4 ms physics step, PhysX reports a piece of the can 2.75 cm inside a piece of the basket wall, although the two pieces are 2.1 cm apart, and the can’s speed rises from 0.2 to 22 m/s while no robot link touches it and the robot is nearly still; the basket ejects it, the can lands 1.65 m away, and the benchmark records a failure ( Figure 1 ). Nothing in the recording supplies the 2.4 J the can gains: no robot contact in that step and no drop. Replayed with the same recorded actions and the reported depth removed over several physics steps instead of one, the episode passes the benchmark’s own success check ( Figure 3 ); in the engine with one constant of its distance algorithm corrected, the contact is never reported ( Section 4.2 ). We call a contact response that sets an object moving faster than the robot’s contact or gravity account for a contact artifact. The impulse that starts one lasts a single physics step and falls between two camera frames; a video shows the can flying, not what launched it, and the success check cannot tell whether the policy or the simulator decided the episode. SimuGuard is an auditing plugin that leaves the evaluation unchanged ( Figure 2 ). An adapter attaches it to the benchmark’s own evaluation script; we provide adapters for LIBERO, RoboTwin, RoboCasa and RoboDojo. At every physics step it records the actuation, the object states and the contacts, and flags an object that starts moving at a contact faster than the robot’s contact or gravity account for. It replays a recorded episode bit for bit by rebuilding the seeded scene and applying the recorded actuation from the first step. A rerun is a replay with one simulation parameter changed, with the recorded actuation and the success check fixed. Prior work adds metrics to the success rate ( Wang et al., 2025 ; Snyder et al., 2026 ; Jiang et al., 2026 ) or measures how solvers depart from physics ( Erez et al., 2015 ; Le Lidec et al., 2023 ) ; neither connects an anomalous contact in a rollout to the outcome of that rollout. In short, many failures in simulated robot benchmarks are the simulator’s fault, not the policy’s; we find the cause and fix it. Our contributions are: • A finding and its cause. Simulated benchmarks contain frequent episodes in which the policy has placed the object and the simulator then ejects it, so the benchmark scores a failure of the policy. On five RoboTwin tasks that place objects into containers with thin walls, 40 of LingBot-VA’s 44 failures and 6 of HyVLA’s 10 contain such an ejection, and the benchmark’s own scripted demonstrations, which act on ground truth object poses, fail the same way. We trace it to a fixed threshold in the distance algorithm of PhysX 5.3.1, which makes the engine report a penetration between pieces that do not touch; with that constant set to its value in PhysX 5.4.0, the ejections are gone. • A tool. SimuGuard monitors every physics step, replays a recorded episode bit for bit, and reruns it with one simulation parameter changed, so that a single contact report can be traced to the outcome of its episode and the trace verified by anyone. It attaches to the benchmark’s own evaluation script without changing it, on LIBERO, RoboTwin, RoboCasa and RoboDojo. • An evaluation practice. Where the cause of the artifacts can be removed, we measure the policy in the repaired engine: LingBot-VA succeeds in 98 of 100 closed loop episodes on the five tasks against 89 in the benchmark’s engine, with no artifact against 12 episodes. Where a benchmark has to keep its engine, we give the estimates it can compute from its recordings, by ignoring, excluding or replaying the flagged episodes, each under one stated assumption, and compare them with the measurement on the same runs.

## 3 SimuGuard
SimuGuard has three requirements: it must not change what it measures, it must reproduce an episode exactly so that a difference after an intervention belongs to the intervention, and it must run with the benchmark’s own evaluator so that its findings apply to the reported numbers. 3.1 Setting A benchmark evaluates a policy on a sequence of seeds. Each seed fixes the scene, and the evaluator runs the policy’s actions through the simulator until a scripted success check passes or the action budget is spent. The simulator advances in physics steps of a few milliseconds (4 ms in RoboTwin, 2 ms in LIBERO and RoboCasa); one policy action spans tens of them. Of N scored episodes, S succeed, and the benchmark reports S/N . A contact artifact is motion that starts at a contact and that neither the robot’s contact nor gravity accounts for; in this paper the term names what the detector of Section 3.3 flags, and Section 4.5 measures its precision against human judgment. Let A be the set of scored episodes that contain one. Figure 2 shows the five steps that follow. 3.2 Recording without intrusion SimuGuard wraps the physics step of the running scene, so the evaluator, the benchmark and the policy run unmodified in their own process. A thin adapter per engine provides the wrapper, around the step function of SAPIEN (RoboTwin), Isaac Sim (RoboDojo) or robosuite, which runs LIBERO and RoboCasa on MuJoCo. Before each step, SimuGuard records the actuation: joint drive targets and forces in PhysX; control inputs, applied forces and mocap poses in MuJoCo. It also records any state that benchmark code writes between two steps, which it must replay ( Section 3.4 ). After each step, it records the pose and velocity of every body and the contacts, merged per pair of bodies, with impulse and penetration depth. A task specification names the objects a task manipulates and the containers they go into. The evaluator, the benchmark and the policy code are untouched, and with the monitor on or off, all 59 tracked object trajectories in our check are identical; the monitor adds 7.5 to 11.6 ms to each 4 ms step in SAPIEN ( Section C.1 ). 3.3 Detecting contact artifacts Detection has three stages, with the same threshold
