===ABSTRACT===
Vision-language models (VLMs) demonstrate broad generalization across tasks and domains. However, comparable generalization in robot manipulation remains challenging. Heterogeneity in robot embodiments, task formulations, geometric conventions, and action interfaces fragments interaction experience across benchmarks and hardware. We rethink robot skill learning through alignment: Can heterogeneous interactions across benchmarks and embodiments jointly improve one shared skillset for robot manipulation? We propose UniCraft, a framework for learning robot manipulation skills by aligning heterogeneous settings. Specifically, UniCraft represents observed scene geometry using consistent coordinate conventions and aligns sensing and control interfaces across robots. It organizes tasks from different benchmarks in a canonical format, allowing interaction feedback to jointly refine shared skills and the orchestration policy that guides their selection and composition, without any demonstrations or model weight updates. We interleave tasks from 3 benchmarks and 6 embodiments, collecting 2,700 learning rollouts per agent model. During training, UniCraft improves pooled task success on unseen scenarios and tasks by 9-28 percentage points on LIBERO-PRO, 10.8-13.3 on RoboTwin 2.0, and 3.1-14.4 on RoboCasa365 (Epochs 1-3). After training, with the learned skills frozen, UniCraft demonstrates zero-shot transfer across 4 new robot embodiments and 5 additional agent models on robosuite and RoboDojo.

===INTRO===

Large language models and vision-language models have demonstrated broad generalization across language, image, and video tasks [ 4 , 1 ] . Natural language and images provide a unified interface for representing information and specifying tasks across domains. Aligning heterogeneous data through this shared interface supports training at scale on diverse sources, allowing models to acquire knowledge that extends to new concepts, instructions, and reasoning tasks [ 12 , 16 ] . At inference, the same interface enables zero-shot transfer to new tasks without task-specific examples or parameter updates [ 43 , 41 , 25 ] . However, comparable generalization in robotics remains challenging. One of the main reasons is heterogeneity (Figure 1 (a)). Robot embodiments differ in their kinematics, observation spaces, and action representations; even the same numerical action can produce different physical effects depending on the robot and its controller [ 33 , 47 ] . These embodiment differences also shape how interaction experience is collected and represented, while datasets introduce further differences in coordinate conventions and annotation practices [ 23 , 47 ] . These differences fragment experience across platforms, making it costly to collect and difficult to transfer to new environments and embodiments. These challenges also affect vision-language-action models, which extend pretrained vision-language models to robot control by learning to predict actions from task-specific demonstrations [ 53 , 23 , 3 , 2 , 49 ] . Their improvements on manipulation benchmarks do not consistently translate into generalization beyond training conditions: changes in scenes, instructions, or embodiments can cause sharp performance drops [ 13 , 47 , 50 ] . Qwen-RobotManip advocates aligning heterogeneous manipulation data in representation, motion, and behavior before VLA training [ 47 ] . Another line of work learns and evolves robot control policies through iterative interaction with the environment, reducing reliance on task-specific trajectory demonstrations [ 7 , 17 , 27 , 29 , 48 ] . In this setting, large language models or vision-language models write executable policy code and continually revise it using interaction feedback [ 26 , 29 , 48 ] . Successful executions and validated repairs are distilled into programmatic skills, accumulating cross-task knowledge and enabling agents to solve diverse tasks through skill composition. [ 17 , 29 , 48 ] . This line of work represents a pragmatic middle ground between learning from demonstrations in predefined settings and open-ended continual learning. Its demonstrated scope, however, remains bounded by task definitions, hardware configurations, and benchmark suites. For example, GaP formulates policy evolution for variational automation tasks, where a specified task is repeated within a fixed workcell with bounded shape and pose variations [ 7 ] . SHAPER evaluates evolved skills under fixed execution interfaces, leaving cross-embodiment transfer underexplored [ 42 ] . Skill2Real learns programmatic skills separately on LIBERO-90 and robosuite [ 17 ] . These boundaries and misalignments constrain the experience generalization across tasks, scenarios and embodiments. Naturally, they motivate three research questions. (i) Can alignment enable unified learning of manipulation skills from interaction across heterogeneous tasks, benchmarks, and embodiments? (ii) If so, does such learning improve generalization to unseen tasks and scenarios? (iii) Can the learned skills also transfer to unseen benchmarks and new embodiments? Inspired by the alignment first, then scale principle [ 47 ] , we present UniCraft , a unified framework designed to align heterogeneous settings (e.g., embodiments, environments, and benchmark cases) for learning robot manipulation skills through iterative interactions without trajectory demonstrations or updates to model weights. We realize this principle by introducing (i) a unified environment representation that describes observed scene entities as typed Objects and exposes robot and camera capabilities through a shared Python package; (ii) composable skills that use reusable library functions and declare the action interfaces required for execution, with adapters providing their hardware-specific implementations; (iii) a learnable orchestration policy that guides the agent model to write and execute programs in response to observations and rollout outcomes; and (iv) a canonical case representation that consistently specifies task instructions and success conditions, embodiments, scenes, and initial states across various benchmarks. During the skill learning stage, mixed rollouts of these cases provide aggregated feedback for jointly optimizing the shared programmatic components and the orchestration policy. ## 3 Method
Here we describe alignment, joint refinement of skills and their orchestration policy, and online skill composition. 3.1 Aligning Heterogeneous Manipulation Settings Aligned geometry. Translating high-level cognitive plans into real-world manipulation requires grounding abstract semantic relations in metric physical space. Although vision-language models can identify interaction candidates and reason about spatial relationships, their predictions may require additional grounding in metric geometry and the robot’s current state for reliable closed-loop execution [ 5 , 46 , 30 ] . We therefore build on object-centric manipulation [ 36 , 18 , 21 ] and represent observed scene entities as typed Objects carrying image bounding boxes and the measured position and extent of their visible regions, with every measurement, target, and tolerance expressed in one episode-local frame. Each episode fixes this frame by its origin and a fixed upward z -axis, and the camera adapter reports intrinsics K together with the capture-time rotation R_{t} and translation \mathbf{t}_{t} . A pixel (u,v) with measured depth d_{t}(u,v) is back-projected into this frame as \mathbf{p}^{\,w}_{t}=R_{t}\!\left(d_{t}(u,v)\,K^{-1}[u,v,1]^{\top}\right)+\mathbf{t}_{t}, (1) so that a region selected in an image becomes a point a skill can reason about in meters. A shared metric frame is necessary but not sufficient, because two devices can agree on where a target is and still disagree on how to touch it. We therefore fix the orientation convention of the tool frame as well. Writing \hat{\mathbf{z}}_{a} for the approach axis and \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{g} for the jaw-closing axis, we define R^{\mathrm{tcp}}=\big[\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{g},\;\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{g},\;\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{a}\,\big],\qquad\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{g}=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{a}\times\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{g}}{\lVert\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{a}\times\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{g}\rVert}, (2) so that the approach and grasp directions are carried by the target itself rather than implied by the executing device. The convention is inherited where a benchmark already defines a tool site, and otherwise recovered from the device’s finger geometry at assembly, not supplied as a per-device rotation matrix. Scene objects thereby provide geometric arguments to shared skills; Figure 2 (a) shows these representations and skill calls, in which a camera observation supplies a target the agent passes to \mathprogram{robot.pick(target)} . Execution contracts. Deploying manipulation policies across heterogeneous robotic platforms poses a trade-off: high-level operational behaviors such as grasping and placing are largely embodiment-agnostic, while their physical instantiation is governed by disparate kinematics, coordinate frames, and native low-level controllers. We resolve this tension by decoupling procedural task logic from embodiment-dependent execution through a modular, capability-based interface, in which the execution hierarchy comprises four components: (i) a skill s\in\mathcal{S} is an executable procedure whose control flow combines library computations, action calls, and outcome checks, without internal LLM/VLM calls; (ii) a library function \ell\in\mathcal{L} performs reusable computation on supplied observations and device parameters without issuing device commands; (iii) an action interface a\in\mathcal{A} specifies a sensing or control operation; and (iv) an adapter \alpha binds each supported interface a to a native implementation \alpha(a) . An interface a=\big(\sigma_{a},F_{a},\varphi_{a}\big) consists of a signature \sigma_{a} of arguments and results, a frame and unit convention F_{a} for those arguments, and a completion predicate \varphi_{a} evaluated on the device’s own measurements. A Cartesian motion completes when the measured tool pose lies within a position and orientation tolerance, and fails otherwise.
