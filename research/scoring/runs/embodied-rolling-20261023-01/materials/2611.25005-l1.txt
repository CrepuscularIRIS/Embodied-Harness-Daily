===ABSTRACT===
Recent work on human-scene interaction (HSI) generation moves from single interactions to long-horizon interaction, where a language-model agent plans and executes a sequence of interactions in a 3D scene under one instruction. However, existing agents struggle in challenging everyday scenarios (e.g., picking up a toy and throwing it into a basket) because they lack spatial control over the motion and quantitative feedback on its execution, and they often fail to complete the interaction sequence. To address these gaps, we present Inhabitant, a training-free agent framework in which the agent specifies each motion through positions, paths and poses, measures how the executed interaction deviates from its intent, and revises the control specification accordingly. For an extensive evaluation in such scenarios, we build Inhabit, a benchmark of 75 household tasks in 31 furniture layouts of an apartment, in which every task chains one to three goals on movable objects and is carried out by the whole body, excluding fingers. On Inhabit, Inhabitant completes 98.7\% of the tasks with Claude Opus 5.5, and 93.3\% and 82.7\% with Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon, respectively. Ablations confirm that without spatial control the agent fails most tasks, and without quantitative feedback its motion becomes less precise. Code is available on the project page.

===INTRO===

Human-scene interaction (HSI) generation, which synthesizes how a person moves through a scene and acts on its objects, is a core problem for computer graphics and embodied AI [ 38 , 7 , 8 , 29 ] . Early work typically focuses on isolated, short-horizon interactions: a single action involving one target object, such as sitting down on a chair, standing up from a chair, or picking up an object [ 61 , 48 , 65 , 18 , 14 , 25 ] . Recent work has shifted towards a more challenging setting of long-horizon interaction, where a language-model agent plans and executes a sequence of interactions in a 3D scene under one instruction, for example, “put the cube in the basket, kick the ball under the table and put the cushion back on the sofa”. These scenes are often simulated environments that respond physically to the body [ 52 , 51 , 43 , 12 , 1 , 60 , 37 ] . However, existing agents often fail to complete such a sequence when every interaction must be executed through full-body motion (Fig. 1 a). In the example above, the agent first tries to pick up the cube, but the hand does not reach close enough to grasp it and closes a few centimetres away. The agent cannot correct this failure: a text prompt cannot specify the required hand position precisely, and rendered frames do not quantify the spatial error. We investigate such failures by adapting three representative agents to these scenarios (Sec. 5.2 ) and attribute them to two causes, both concerning the interface between the agent and the generated interaction. First, the control is mostly high-level: the agent conditions the human motion only on a skill with one parameter [ 37 ] , a text description of each step [ 49 , 60 ] , or a few contact or goal positions [ 52 , 12 ] . As a result, it cannot specify the motion at the level of precise positions, paths and poses. Second, the feedback is absent or qualitative: most agents plan the interaction once and never observe its outcome [ 52 , 51 , 43 ] , including the few that write poses for a motion generator [ 30 , 1 ] . Other agents observe only rendered frames [ 37 , 60 ] , which may show that an interaction failed but cannot indicate precisely how far the executed interaction deviates from its intent. We present Inhabitant , a training-free agent framework that turns long-horizon human-scene interaction into a closed-loop spatial control problem (Fig. 1 b). The key novelty is to expose two interfaces that existing agents lack: spatially grounded control over generated whole-body motion and quantitative feedback on its physical execution. For control, the agent directly writes the control specification of a pretrained motion generator, Kimodo [ 32 ] , and specifies the motion by positions, paths and poses rather than only high-level skills or text prompts. For feedback, each motion is executed in a scene simulator with half-physics [ 36 ] ; the agent then queries measurements of the body and the scene, such as positions, distances and contacts, to quantify how the executed interaction deviates from its intent. It revises the control specification using the measured deviation. The two interfaces close a loop: the agent proposes a motion, executes it, measures the outcome, revises the specification and commits the result as a short motion segment only when the measurements match its intent. By composing such verified segments, Inhabitant builds a complete interaction sequence segment by segment. For an extensive evaluation in such challenging everyday scenarios, we build Inhabit (Fig. 3 ), a new benchmark of 75 household tasks in 31 furniture layouts of the ReplicaCAD apartment [ 39 , 56 ] , simulated with half-physics [ 36 ] in line with HumanCLAW [ 37 ] . Each task states one to three goals, such as placing an object, throwing it or moving it with a foot; ten tasks additionally combine a goal with a body posture, such as sitting, lying or kneeling. The goals are designed to require coordinated use of the hands, the feet and whole-body posture. Inhabit differs from previous benchmarks in two respects. First, HSI benchmarks typically evaluate a single interaction with a static target at a time, such as walking to a piece of furniture and sitting on it [ 48 , 37 ] . In contrast, Inhabit chains multiple interactions with movable objects under a single instruction, so the outcome of one interaction, including its errors, becomes the initial condition for the next. Second, embodied household benchmarks also contain long-horizon tasks, but agents usually solve them by calling predefined skills such as navigating, picking and placing [ 3 , 56 ] . In Inhabit, every step must instead be realized through generated whole-body motion, excluding fingers, so the benchmark tests whether the body can reach, hold and move an object through physically executed motion. ## 3 Inhabitant
Inhabitant is a training-free agent framework with three components. The agent is connected to the other two components by two interfaces, which are the core of the framework: spatial control , through which it specifies the motion for the generator by positions, paths and poses, and quantitative feedback , through which the simulator reports the executed result as measurements. We first state the problem and then describe the components one by one. 3.1 Problem Formulation An interaction sequence is a fixed-frame-rate sequence of states \Sigma=(s_{0},\dots,s_{N}) , where each state s_{t} comprises the poses of the body, all objects and the articulated furniture at frame t . Here, N denotes the number of frames. The agent does not act frame by frame but through segments : a segment \tau_{k}=(s_{t_{k-1}},\dots,s_{t_{k}}) is a contiguous, few-second portion of the sequence that is generated and executed as a unit. The full sequence is the concatenation of K segments, \Sigma=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{K}) . The input is a natural-language instruction I and the initial state s_{0} . Task completion is evaluated by a success rule G , which checks the states of the sequence and returns 1 for success and 0 otherwise. The rule is task-specific, defined together with its instruction (Sec. 4 ), and hidden from the agent. The objective is to produce a successful interaction sequence \Sigma such that G(\Sigma)=1 . 3.2 Agent The agent \pi is a language model that builds the interaction sequence segment by segment. The prompt of the agent contains the instruction I , the state description \phi(s_{0}) of the initial state, the documentation of the generator, the dimensions of the simulated body, and the request for person-like motion that neither collides with the scene nor slides its feet (Supp. A.2 ). At each step it issues one tool call a [ 57 ] , \displaystyle a=\pi\big(I,\phi(s),H\big)\in\{ \displaystyle\textsc{try}(c,e),\,\textsc{measure}(q), (1) \displaystyle\textsc{commit},\,\textsc{rollback}(j)\}, where the state description \phi(s) lists, in text, the positions and sizes of the body and of the objects in the state s where the preceding segment ended. The agent can re-read the \phi(s) at any time. H stores previous tool calls and their results. The agent chooses both the call type and its arguments. The framework prescribes neither the order of the calls nor their content. Next we define the four call types. • \textsc{try}(c,e) requests a motion for the next segment. The control specification c states what the body should do, for how long, and where specified joints should be at specified frames; This is how the agent exerts spatial control (Sec. 3.3 ). The interaction events e state which hand grasps or releases which object, and when. The simulator executes the generated motion from the state at the end of the last segment, and the call returns qualitative feedback o(\tau) on the resulting candidate segment \tau , i.e., the sequence of states that the execution produces (Sec. 3.4 ). A candidate segment is not added to the sequence until it is committed; a subsequent try replaces it, allowing the agent to revise a motion by trying again from the same state. • \textsc{measure}(q) asks the simulator for a measurement and returns its value M(q,\tau) . The agent chooses the query q : what quantity to measure, between which entities, and at which frame or interval. For example, it may ask for the distance between a hand and an object at a given frame of the last try. These measurements are the quantitative feedback , and the agent receives only the measurements it asks for. The available queries, which may refer to an earlier segment or to the current state, are given in Sec. 3.4 . • commit appends the candidate segment of the last try to the interaction sequence, \tau_{k+1}=\tau , and returns the state description of the new state. • \textsc{rollback}(j) restores the state s_{t_{j}} at the end of an earlier segment j
