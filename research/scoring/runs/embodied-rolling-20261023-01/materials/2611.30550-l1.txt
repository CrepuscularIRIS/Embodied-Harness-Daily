===ABSTRACT===
Vision-language-action (VLA) policies can execute individual skills but fail to complete tasks that require combining several of them. In our audit of Xiaomi-Robotics-1, an estimated 43\% of failures involve choosing the wrong target or losing track of task progress. Wrapping a frozen VLA in a vision-language model (VLM) harness is a natural remedy. Existing harnesses can gain substantially from task- and policy-specific preparation, raising the question of how much of that gain transfers to a new task or policy. An ideal harness should (i) need minimal preparation for a new task, (ii) need no redesign for a new policy, and (iii) preserve standalone capabilities the policy already has. To the best of our knowledge, our \ours{} is the first harness built this way. Its premise is that choosing targets and tracking progress are easy for a VLM such as GPT-6 without task preparation, while the frozen VLA already handles continuous, contact-rich motion. The frozen VLA therefore executes continuous control while the VLM plans zero-shot and monitors execution; when progress stalls or targets drift, the VLM first rephrases the instruction and, only as a fallback, executes short generated code over generic motion primitives before returning fine control to the VLA. The supervision rules stay fixed across policies, requiring only a thin interface adapter and an automatically profiled skill configuration per policy. On 200 RoboCasa365 scenes, \ours{} with GPT-6 Astra raises the success rate of frozen Xiaomi-Robotics-1 from \textbf{\color{blue}57.5\%} to \textbf{\color{blue}70.5\%} and preserves \textbf{\color{blue}90.4\%} of its standalone successes (104/115) while rescuing 37 of 85 failures. On a shared 50-scene subset, the same rules add 8 to 10 percentage points to each of four other frozen policies, while existing preparation-based harness systems often fail to transfer to a new policy.

===INTRO===
A vision-language-action (VLA) policy can have the motor skills a task requires and still fail to finish it ( Hu et al., 2026 ) . In some RoboCasa365 ( Nasiriany et al., 2026 ) episodes, a robot acted on the wrong object, turning the wrong burner knob or taking the wrong item from a drawer (Figure 2 a,b). Even when it chose the right item, it could move on too soon. Asked to put two pieces of broccoli in a pot and stir them, it added one and began stirring while the other was still on the counter. Across failed RoboCasa365 episodes with Xiaomi-Robotics-1 ( Xiaomi Robotics Team, 2026b ) , we estimate that 43% of failures arise from choosing the wrong target or losing track of which steps remained (§ 3.1 , Table A.2 ). A natural response to these decision errors is to put a vision-language model (VLM) in charge of tracking the task while the VLA executes it. Existing harnesses improve performance by learning how to guide the robot in a particular setting. In its few-shot RoboCasa365 evaluation, Harness VLA ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) finds a successful sequence of subtask instructions on one reference scene per task and reuses it in new scenes. EmbodiedRSI ( Song et al., 2026 ) runs robot trials to decide which changes to its code and skills to keep, while AdaHVLA ( Tang et al., 2026 ) revises its prompting and recovery code separately for each robot and policy. How much of the resulting gain survives when the task set or the policy changes? Although Harness VLA gains 29.2 percentage points on RLDX-1, transferring its released task memories to Xiaomi-Robotics-1 on our 50-scene benchmark solves 29 scenes, compared with 30 for Xiaomi alone ( -2.0 percentage points). We argue that an ideal harness should (i) need minimal preparation for a new task, (ii) need no redesign for a new policy, and (iii) avoid breaking what the policy already solves . We introduce RoboHarness-Zero , a VLM harness that gives a frozen VLA the full task and monitors progress within each substage during execution, without interfering while the policy advances. When progress stalls or the robot acts on the wrong target, RoboHarness-Zero first revises the instruction and, if needed, briefly takes over the arm via VLM-generated code over generic motion primitives before handing control back. Our core insight is that the instruction and the scene alone determine which object the robot should act on, what must happen next, and whether the current step is complete, so a VLM can judge them zero-shot and identically across policies. This allows RoboHarness-Zero to supervise different tasks and frozen VLA backbones during execution with a single fixed set of rules; onboarding a new policy requires no task-specific demonstrations or scripts—only a thin interface adapter and a one-time automatic calibration (§ 3 ). Our method builds on the premise that a VLM can interpret an instruction, plan its steps, and detect execution drift zero-shot, while the frozen VLA provides reactive, contact-rich motor control without needing task-specific scripts or skills. When asked to place a suitable tool for peeling a cucumber on a cutting board (Figure 2 b), RoboHarness-Zero gives the frozen VLA the full instruction while the VLM forms its own step-by-step plan from the robot’s camera views. Judging progress against the active step, the VLM sees that reaching the drawer completes the approach, but the grasp stalls while the peeler remains inside. It first intervenes through language—“Place the green-handled peeler on the cutting board”—and only when the VLA grasps and delivers the knife instead does the harness invoke generic motion primitives via generated code on the fly to position the gripper at the peeler, before returning control to the VLA to finish (§ 3.5 – 3.6 ). Task-level completion criteria can be shared across policies, whereas execution reliability and timing vary. For example, if a task requires two pieces of broccoli in a pot, placing only one leaves the goal incomplete regardless of the policy (Figure 2 c). Automatic profiling estimates each policy’s skill success rates and completion times, allowing the same supervision rules to accommodate different execution speeds. Across 200 RoboCasa365 evaluation scenes spanning 50 tasks, RoboHarness-Zero with GPT-6 Astra raises the success rate of frozen Xiaomi-Robotics-1 from 57.5% to 70.5% (Figure 1 ). It preserves 104 of the 115 episodes the policy solves on its own (90.4%) and rescues 37 of its 85 failures, for a net gain of +26 episodes; multi-step tasks account for +23 of this net gain, including +13 on previously unseen task compositions. On a shared 50-scene subset, the same rules add 8 to 10 percentage points to each of four other frozen policies, from \pi_{0.5} (16% to 24%) to RLDX-1 (40% to 50%). Recovery remains a bottleneck: in a separate trajectory audit, 22 of 35 failed skill attempts still do not reach their goal after the harness intervenes (§ 4.7 ).
