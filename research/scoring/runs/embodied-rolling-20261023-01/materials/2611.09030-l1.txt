# A Controlled Study of Models, Skills, Feedback, and Curriculum in Agentic Robotics

## Abstract
Agentic robotics asks coding agents to implement and iteratively improve robot policies from simulated and real-world physical interactions. We study the self-learning loop of agentic robotics through a controlled simulation study spanning nine manipulation tasks, 631 different experiment configurations, and 28K coding agent rollouts, systematically varying model capability, skill priors, feedback structure, and curriculum. The experiment data suggests that the bottleneck is often not the access to sufficient information, but interpreting it into the right policy update. Across these factors, a successful self-learning loop depends on how effectively agents attribute failures, reason task-relevant spatial and geometric structure, and selectively restructure code to improve policies.

## 1 Introduction
Recent coding agents, such as GPT Astra ( OpenAI, 2026a ) and Claude Fable ( Anthropic, 2026a ) , have become increasingly capable of generating and iteratively repairing executable programs, motivating a growing class of Agentic Robotics (AR) systems in which LLM- and VLM-based agents write code that composes perception, planning, and control skills into robot policies ( Liang et al., 2023b ; Singh et al., 2023 ; Fu et al., 2026 ; Chen et al., 2026a ) . However, self-improvement in robotics differs fundamentally from software debugging. In software, failures, such as compiler errors, are often exposed through symbolic feedback that is already closely aligned with the program; but in robotics, the evidence to improve a policy is instead embedded in physical interactions: object geometry, spatial relationships, contact, motion, and changes in the scene over time. As illustrated in Figure 1 , a failed placement can be attributed to an earlier incompatible grasp and a collision requires reasoning about the geometry of an object after it becomes part of the moving robot. In this paper, we ask: what determines whether an agent can interpret physical interaction and convert it into effective robot-policy improvement? We design a controlled simulation study of agentic robot self-learning across five contact-rich VABAR manipulation tasks Xie et al. (2026b) and four longer-horizon MimicGen tasks Mandlekar et al. (2023) . Holding the overall self-learning harness fixed, we systematically vary four components of the learning loop: model capability, skill prior, feedback structure, and curriculum, as illustrated in Figure 2 . Across these studies, we find that self-learning is limited less by the availability of useful information than by the agent’s ability to turn the information into effective policy updates. More execution feedback is not necessarily more useful: feedback is most effective when aligned with semantically meaningful policy decisions rather than reported at the finest temporal resolution. Similarly, world-frame states contain all the information needed for manipulation, yet agents often fail to recover task-relevant object-relative and functional relationships unless these are made explicit. Even when agents identify local geometric errors, they often struggle to translate them into coherent collision-aware motion plans. We find that this limitation can also affect curriculum design: agents tend to preserve and patch policy structure inherited from earlier stages rather than fundamentally reorganize it, so privileged learning can either provide a useful initialization or constrain later learning when the inherited policy depends on privileged precision or brittle manipulation strategies. Our results further suggest that progress in agentic robot self-learning comes from the better structures of skills, feedback, and state representations and the learned physical reasoning ability from the underlying models. This paper makes three contributions: (1) A controlled study design for Agentic Robotics that isolates four important components of simulation-guided self-learning, (2) Experiment data from 631 implemented experiments with 28 thousand simulation rollouts on Agentic robotics policies, (3) Six findings on model capability, skill priors, feedback structure, and curriculum.

## 3 Problem Formulation
Agentic robotics for manipulation. A robot manipulation task class can be defined as a tuple \mathcal{T}=\langle\mathcal{L},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{J}\rangle : a natural-language instruction \mathcal{L} , a fixed workcell \mathcal{E} with a known robot and sensor configuration \mathcal{R} , a bounded set of objects \mathcal{O} with known models, a state space \mathcal{X} of robot configurations and object poses, a belief space \mathcal{B} giving the distribution over initial object poses, and a reward \mathcal{J} , here the per-object success indicator \mathbb{I}(\text{success}) . A task instance \tau_{i}=\langle o_{i},x_{i}\rangle is drawn from \mathcal{O} and \mathcal{B} . The agent produces a policy \pi represented as executable source code composed from reusable robot skills. Following the graph-based policy representation of GaP ( Chen et al., 2026a ) , we view \pi as a computation graph whose nodes invoke skills and whose edges define their execution and control flow. The policy is constructed over a skill library \mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{0}\cup\mathcal{S}_{g}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\tau}\cup\mathcal{S}_{A} , where \mathcal{S}_{0} contains basic robot and sensor primitives; \mathcal{S}_{g} contains reusable general-purpose skills; \mathcal{S}_{\tau} contains task-specific skills provided by a human expert; and \mathcal{S}_{A} contains task-specific skills synthesized by the agent. Given \mathcal{S} , an LLM agent A constructs \pi by selecting, parameterizing, and composing available skills, while implementing new skills when the existing library is insufficient. On a task instance \tau_{i} , the resulting policy \pi(a\mid\mathcal{I}) maps observations \mathcal{I} to robot actions a in closed loop, and the resulting episode is evaluated by \mathcal{J} . Given a task class \mathcal{T} and skill library \mathcal{S} , the agent seeks to construct a policy that succeeds across the belief space: \pi^{*}=\arg\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}\sim\mathcal{B}}\left[\mathcal{J}\big(\pi(a\mid\mathcal{I})\big)\right], using only \mathcal{L} , \mathcal{S} , and the outcomes of its own policies. Policy improvement may therefore modify both the composition of the policy graph and the implementation of its constituent skills. Self-learning loop. Unlike reinforcement learning, feedback in the self-learning loop is not restricted to a scalar reward. At iteration k , the current policy \pi_{k} runs in simulation on the training instances \{\tau_{i}\} . A feedback function F turns these runs into a textual (and optionally visual) report f_{k}=F(\pi_{k},\{\tau_{i}\}) . The agent then edits its own policy, conditioned on the report and on its edit history \pi_{k+1}=A\big(\pi_{k},\,f_{k},\,\{(\pi_{j},f_{j})\}_{j<k}\big) . The loop runs for K iterations. Success is reported on a held-out set of instances for the initial policy \pi_{0} (zero-shot, k{=}0 ) and for the iterate with the best validation score, \pi_{k^{*}} with k^{*}=\arg\max_{k}\mathrm{val}(\pi_{k}) . Held-out success at every iteration gives a learning curve for each setting and task. Each study below varies one component of this loop: the agent A , the skills \mathcal{S} , the feedback F , or the curriculum over training instances. Assumptions and Scope. We assume access to a reliable physics simulator. Training and evaluation remain within the same manipulation task class, with a fixed robot and sensor configuration and bounded scene variation. We evaluate generalization to held-out instances of these task classes and do not study sim-to-real transfer or generalization to new embodiments.

## 4 Study Design
Task Setup. We evaluate five simulated manipulation tasks from the Variational Automation Benchmark for Agentic Robotics (VABAR) Xie et al. (2026b) : (1) Tool Hanging , (2) Syringe Packing , (3) Gear Meshing , (4) USB-C Insertion , and (5) Cable Routing . For the curriculum study, we evaluate four multi-step tasks from MimicGen Mandlekar et al. (2023) using the Medium (D1) layouts: (1) Kitchen , (2) Coffee , (3) Threading , and (4) Mug Cleanup (Figure 3 ). Skill Setup. We use open-robot-skills provided by Chen et al. (2026a) as task-agnostic skill library \mathcal{S} . The library contains a set of 30 skills in the Agent Skills format, each with a natural-language SKILL.md describing when the skill applies and conditions. The library covers perception, grasping and motion planning primitives. The full list of skills can be found in Appendix B . Metrics and Evaluation scale .
