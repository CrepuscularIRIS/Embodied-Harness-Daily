===ABSTRACT===
Recent vision-language models (VLMs) can act as robot-use agents that understand diverse scenes and control robots through policy code and tools. However, they predict 3D gripper poses poorly even with extended reasoning, struggling with precise manipulation. Vision-language-action (VLA) and world-action (WAM) models are the opposite: they execute fast, smooth control on trained tasks, yet fail under domain shifts or when tasks require long-horizon memory and reasoning. Agentic robots therefore combine VLM reasoning and perception with precise VLA/WAM control through a policy harness. Yet harness design spans thousands of configurations, shifting success by tens of points with the same models. Moreover, testing even one design takes thousands of rollouts at high API and GPU cost, so prior works often commit to a single design chosen from few alternatives. To guide harness design, we evaluate over 470,000 rollouts across eight simulation benchmarks, six VLMs, and 15 VLA/WAM checkpoints. Seven controlled studies show effective harnesses must adapt to both VLM and VLA/WAM capabilities. Our findings specify what structure the VLM's policy code needs, where VLA manipulation needs help from tools and policy code, and when to involve the VLM at test time. The reference recipe X-Harness shows that these findings compose without weight updates, lifting base policies up to $+42.8$ points across memory, reasoning, and domain shifts.

===INTRO===
Recent vision-language models (VLMs) have emerged as robot-use agents [ 16 ] that understand diverse scenes, recall past events, and—building on programmatic robot control [ 23 , 14 ] —control robots through policy code and tools [ 11 , 12 ] . However, they struggle with precise manipulation: they predict 3D gripper poses poorly even with extended reasoning and motion planners (§ 4.1 ), and calling them in the loop stalls the robot. Vision-language-action (VLA) [ 3 , 2 , 19 ] and world-action (WAM) [ 1 ] models are the opposite: they execute fast, smooth control on trained tasks, yet fail under domain shifts or when tasks require long-horizon memory and reasoning. For instance, \pi_{0.5} [ 2 ] exceeds 95\% on standard LIBERO [ 24 ] but drops to about a quarter once LIBERO-PRO [ 51 ] shifts object positions. Agentic robots [ 32 ] therefore combine VLM reasoning and perception with precise VLA or WAM control, alongside motion primitives (functions that move the gripper). The interface that connects them is the policy harness (§ 3.1 ): it specifies what context the VLM receives, whether it acts in-loop or writes policy code (free-form Python or symbolic templates), and when it is called. Yet harness design spans thousands of configurations, shifting success by tens of points with the same VLM and action policy (§ 4 – 5 ) . Moreover, testing even one design takes thousands of rollouts at high API and GPU cost, so prior works often commit to a single harness [ 23 , 15 , 12 , 50 ] or restrict their ablations to a single regime and benchmark [ 42 , 13 ] . As a result, how to build effective harnesses for agentic robots remains unclear. To answer this question, we evaluate over 4,000 settings and 470,000 rollouts across eight simulation benchmarks (267 tasks), six VLMs, and 15 VLA/WAM checkpoints (Figure ). Controlled simulation enables one-factor-at-a-time ablations on matched seeds, complemented by supplementary physical validation on a bimanual robot (Appendix J ). Starting from VLMs (§ 4 ), we study (1) action level : 3D poses versus VLA sub-goals , 2D visual prompts, and 3D target code; (2) context and perception : video history, task notes, pre-coding detections, and depth quality; (3) call timing : in-loop VLM calls versus closed-loop policy code; and (4) code structure : free-form Python versus symbolic templates. Starting from VLAs (§ 5 ), we study (5) where harnesses help across tasks and base policies; (6) pre-test evolution : revising policy code or a shared tool bank from training failures under regression checks; and (7) test-time VLM involvement : Offline does not call the VLM, Semi-online calls the VLM on failure signals, and Full-online calls the VLM per sub-goal. Across these seven studies, we find that effective harnesses must adapt to both VLM coding strength and VLA/WAM capability rather than following one fixed design. To provide open baselines and practical guidelines for researchers stepping into agentic robotics, our contributions are: • Large-scale benchmark study and open baselines (§ 3 ), evaluating over 4,000 configurations across eight benchmarks under one protocol, releasing our evaluation suite, policy code, and 470,000+ rollout logs. • Seven empirical takeaways as a design guideline (§ 4 – 5 ; Table 1 ), specifying perception grounding, code structure, VLA-vs.-primitive routing, code evolution, and test-time VLM scheduling. • X-Harness, a reference recipe (§ 6 ) showing that these guidelines compose without weight updates to lift base policies under domain shifts, on memory tasks, and in precise manipulation.
