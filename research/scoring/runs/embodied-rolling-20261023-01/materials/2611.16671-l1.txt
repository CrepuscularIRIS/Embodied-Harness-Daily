# NSDL: Neuro-Symbolic Division of Labor for Multi-Robot Task Planning

## Abstract
Planning for a heterogeneous robot team from a scene image and an instruction involves two kinds of decisions. Some must be judged from the scene: which goals are already met, how the task splits, and which robot does what. Others follow from these and can be computed: prerequisite actions, handoffs between robots, and timing. We argue that each kind should go to the component suited to it: a small vision-language model learns the first, and a deterministic solver computes the second. In NSDL (Neuro-Symbolic Division of Labor), the trained model writes its judgments as a plan sketch, which lists the goals that still need action and the intended steps in order, each with a robot. The solver expands the sketch into an executable joint plan. The model calls two tools, a detector for evidence and the solver, and is trained with the solver in the loop. We evaluate on the public VIKI-L2 benchmark and on LOCUS-Bench, a new benchmark that grades the two kinds of difficulty separately. NSDL outperforms end-to-end planners and formalize-then-plan baselines on both benchmarks and with three backbones from 3B to 8B; the 7B model reaches 97.0% out-of-distribution accuracy on VIKI-L2. Ordering and scheduling errors nearly disappear once the solver writes the structure; the remaining failures are misjudged states, and most goals the model misses are ones it never lists. Code, model weights, and LOCUS-Bench are publicly available at https://github.com/Xincheng-Tan/NSDL.

## I INTRODUCTION
Multi-robot systems combine robots with complementary capabilities for tasks that no single robot completes alone. Planning for such a team from a scene image and an instruction has become a task for vision-language models (VLMs). The planner receives the instruction, one image, and the capabilities of each robot, and returns a joint plan that says which robot does what and in what order [ 1 , 2 ] . Large language models (LLMs) brought broad reasoning to this setting [ 3 , 4 , 5 ] , and VLMs connect it to raw visual observation [ 6 , 7 , 2 ] . Trained planners for this task build on small VLMs [ 2 ] , whose capacity is limited. Which decisions should such a model learn, and which should a solver compute? The question applies to language-model planning in general [ 8 , 9 ] . The decisions in this task fall into two kinds. State judgment covers whether each goal is already satisfied in the scene, how the task decomposes into subtasks, and which robot takes which. No fixed procedure computes these from the input; they require image evidence and semantic understanding. Structural planning covers the prerequisites and the scheduling that follow once intent and assignment are fixed, such as opening a cabinet before stowing an item and respecting end-effector capacity. Given a fixed action model, a deterministic solver computes them correctly. Fig. 1 shows one failure of each kind on one scene, made by two planners: the untrained Qwen2.5-VL-7B and VIKI-R 7B, an end-to-end planner trained on the public VIKI-Bench. Both mark a plate that is already on the table as still to be moved, and both stack picks onto a robot without a free end-effector. We argue that each kind should go to the component suited to it: the model learns the state judgment, and a deterministic solver computes the structural planning. The model then spends its capacity only on the decisions that need the scene, and a 7B model plans better than planners that learn both kinds. Existing planners draw the line elsewhere: end-to-end planners [ 2 , 10 , 6 ] learn both kinds, and formalize-then-plan methods [ 5 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 ] have an untrained model write the input of a classical planner, which then produces the plan (Sec. II ). NSDL (Neuro-Symbolic Division of Labor) implements this division (Fig. 2 ). The trained model writes its judgments as a plan sketch: the goals that still need action and the intended operations (ops) in order, each assigned to a robot. A deterministic solver \Phi expands the sketch into the executable plan: it adds prerequisite actions and handoffs, breaks each op into primitive actions, and schedules them. The model works in one multi-turn dialogue (a trajectory) with two tools. detect returns the objects and spatial relations it observes, and schedule runs the solver; when the solver rejects a sketch, schedule returns the reason, and the model revises the sketch. The model is trained by supervised fine-tuning and then by reinforcement learning with the solver in the loop. We evaluate NSDL on the public VIKI-L2 benchmark, the task-planning level of VIKI-Bench [ 2 ] , and on LOCUS-Bench, which we build because public benchmarks fold both kinds of failure into one score. LOCUS-Bench sets state-judgment and structural-planning difficulty on two separate five-level scales, and its out-of-distribution splits raise the difficulty of one kind, the other, or both. On both benchmarks and with three backbones from 3B to 8B, NSDL outperforms end-to-end planners and formalize-then-plan baselines; the 7B model reaches 97.0% out-of-distribution accuracy on VIKI-L2. Our contributions are as follows. • NSDL. A framework for embodied multi-robot task planning in which a trained model makes the decisions that depend on the scene and writes them as a plan sketch, and a deterministic solver expands the sketch into the executable joint plan. • LOCUS-Bench. A benchmark that sets state-judgment and structural-planning difficulty on separate five-level scales, so that a failure can be traced to either kind. • Findings. Once the solver writes the structure, the runtime almost never rejects a plan for its ordering or scheduling, and these errors return when the model is trained without the solver. The remaining failures are misjudged states. Most goals the model misses are ones it never lists rather than ones it misreads, and ground-truth evidence does not reduce their number.

## III NSDL
NSDL lets a trained model make every state-judgment decision inside one agentic trajectory and hands the resulting plan sketch to a deterministic solver that expands it into the submitted plan (Fig. 2 (A)). The model reads the instruction, the image, and the robot team and writes, in a fixed order after a think segment, [GOAL] (a skip or active verdict for each goal with a one-sentence reason), [ASSIGN] , and [PLAN_SKETCH] , calling detect first in a separate turn when it needs evidence. A finished sketch is always handed to schedule , which runs the solver \Phi and returns the expanded plan or a rejection with a structured reason; once \Phi accepts, the expansion is the submitted plan. III-A Problem and the Plan Sketch A task instance gives an instruction, one scene image, and a robot team with capability labels and end-effector counts, and no symbolic world state: x=(I,o,R) . Executability is defined by the action model \mathcal{M} (end-effector capacity, holding, closed containers isolating their contents, per-step concurrency) over the eight primitives \mathcal{A}=\{ Move, Reach, Grasp, Place, Open, Close, Push, Interact \} of the official VIKI-R implementation, shared by both benchmarks; we re-encode \mathcal{M} in STRIPS style with a typed entity catalog E , used by \Phi and by our runtime validator (one difference, a Move demanded before each deposit, is described in Appendix B ). The output \pi=(O,\rho,\prec,\tau) , comprising ops, an assignment, a directed acyclic dependency graph, and a primitive schedule, is scored through \tau against a ground-truth initial state s_{0} (of which o is the rendering) and a reference plan \bar{\tau} visible only to scoring: \begin{split}\mathrm{Sol}(\pi;x,s_{0},\bar{\tau}):\Leftrightarrow\;&\mathrm{Exec}_{\mathcal{M}}(\pi,s_{0})\\ &\wedge\,s_{T}(\pi,s_{0})\models G(I,s_{0})\,\wedge\,|\tau|\leq|\bar{\tau}|\end{split} (1) (i) executable under \mathcal{M} from s_{0} ; (ii) goal-achieving, where G itself depends on s_{0} , since which goals require action varies with the scene; and (iii) within the reference length.
