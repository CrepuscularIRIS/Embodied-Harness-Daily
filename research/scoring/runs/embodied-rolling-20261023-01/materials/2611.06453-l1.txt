# Beyond Infraction Scores: Failure-Boundary Profiles for Closed-Loop Evaluation of Driving and Robot Navigation Agents

## Abstract
Closed-loop driving benchmarks in CARLA rank agents by a driving score that multiplies route completion by an infraction penalty. This score does not distinguish an agent that stops in front of an obstacle from one that pushes past it. In a corridor closed by two parked cars, for example, one agent that drives through with contact averages 52.2 on the blocked widths, while the agents that stop average between 37.8 and 40.6. We propose failure-boundary profiles, an evaluation in which each scenario family varies one measured factor, such as corridor width or the time gap in oncoming traffic, and each variant is certified from measurement as one where the agent should proceed or one where it should stop. Along this axis we estimate two curves per agent, commitment (the probability of attempting the passage) and competence (the probability of passing without contact), and locate where each crosses one half, with intervals that account for the simulator's run-to-run variance. The certified labels also give a summary metric, decision accuracy, which credits clean passes on passable variants and stops on blocked ones. We evaluate seven driving agents on seven CARLA families. The rule-based expert reaches a decision accuracy of 0.74 and TFv6, the highest-scoring learned agent, 0.60. Agents with similar driving scores fail in different ways, and agents that reliably stop at a blocked corridor still rarely wait for a gap in oncoming traffic. We also apply the method, unchanged, to a warehouse robot in Isaac Sim. The navigation policy we test stops at blocked gaps in only one of three families, and two fine-tuned versions of it pass more gaps cleanly but no longer stop at all.

## 1 Introduction
Recent CARLA-based benchmarks such as Bench2Drive and Fail2Drive [ 10 , 7 ] evaluate agents with the Driving Score (DS), which multiplies an agent’s route completion by an infraction penalty. The route is predefined for each scenario, and the infractions cover collisions, traffic-rule violations and blocking. The intent is that a single number captures good driving. These benchmarks probe a wide range of behaviors through scenario construction: merging, obeying traffic signals, and deviating from the usual route geometry. Bench2Drive observed that long routes entangle different failure modes and make aggregate metrics hard to interpret, and moved to short routes with one scenario each. We argue that even a per-scenario driving score cannot explain why two agents differ, because it mixes two separate questions: whether the agent attempts a maneuver, and whether it carries it out without contact. We therefore evaluate agents along controlled axes instead of fixed routes. A scenario family varies one measured factor, such as the free width of a corridor or the time gap in oncoming traffic, and holds the rest of the scene constant. Each variant is certified from measurement: a corridor is passable or blocked according to its geometry, and a gap in oncoming traffic is usable or one to wait for according to the traffic that actually occurs. Along each axis we estimate two curves per agent. Commitment is the probability that the agent attempts the passage, and competence is the probability that it passes without contact. The point where a curve crosses one half is a failure boundary , which we report with an interval reflecting the simulator’s run-to-run variance. Where commitment is higher than competence, the agent keeps attempting a passage it can no longer complete, and the width of that region differs from agent to agent. Since every variant carries a label, each run can also be scored as right or wrong, a clean pass where passage is possible and a stop where it is not. We call the resulting summary decision accuracy . We apply this to seven driving agents on seven families, producing curves that show each agent’s commitment-competence gap, and we analyze the failures behind it. The same curves and the same decision accuracy carry over to robot navigation without modification. We test a world-model-based navigation policy in simulation, producing similar failure-boundary curves, and demonstrate the effects of a simple fine-tuning strategy. Our contributions are: 1. a pipeline that turns natural-language requests into scenario families whose variants differ in one measured factor and are certified passable or blocked, with the spawned geometry checked in the simulator; 2. failure-boundary profiles, i.e. commitment and competence curves along a certified axis, with boundaries and intervals that account for the simulator’s run-to-run nondeterminism; 3. decision accuracy, a summary metric that credits a clean pass on variants certified passable and a stop on variants certified blocked; 4. an evaluation of seven driving agents on seven CARLA families and of a warehouse robot on three Isaac Sim families. Agents with similar driving scores fail in different ways, within a family the driving score can rank an agent that never stops above agents that stop correctly, and learned agents rarely wait for a gap in oncoming traffic, including those that stop at a blocked corridor. 5. the ten scenario families, the measurement chain and the records of all seventeen campaigns in a public repository , and the simulator evidence the scores were based on in a dataset on the Hugging Face Hub .

Finally, in Section 3.4 we illustrate the steps of the SCL scenario generation pipeline. 3.1 A Case Study in Failure Profiling We consider a constructed scenario in which route completion and infraction score become opposite objectives. In this scenario the ego vehicle must pass between two cars parked on the two shoulders of the road. Neither car impinges upon the drivable surface, and thus the vehicle should drive straight through. We then systematically reduce the spacing between these two vehicles, thereby reducing the drivable corridor. Figure 1 shows the two ends of this scenario. By repeating evaluations of the agent at each reduction in corridor width, we obtain a curve over the variations in agent performance. Figure 2 shows two baseline agents (TFv6 [ 14 ] and BridgeDrive [ 11 ] ) varied over corridor width. We measure two quantities across repetitions: the CARLA Driving Score, and a new measure detailed further in Section 3.2 , the probability that the agent attempts to continue along the route. Figure 2 illustrates the tension between route completion and infraction score: the agent can win a higher driving score by trading infractions for progress. Looking at commitment, we can see a clear boundary at which point the TFv6 [ 14 ] agent refuses to attempt the gap. The BridgeDrive [ 11 ] agent meanwhile attempts the gap no matter the width.
