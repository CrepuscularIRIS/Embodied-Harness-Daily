# Consensus Within, Competition Between: Decentralized LLM Teams for Perimeter Defense

## Abstract
The use of large language models (LLMs) to coordinate multi-agent teams raises a fundamental architectural question: whether task allocation should be centralized in a single LLM controller or distributed across autonomous LLM agents, particularly when communication is unreliable. In a deliberately simplified perimeter-defense simulation against LLM-controlled intruders, we compared a central commander, an auction-based consensus team and independent agents under five network conditions. Breach rates were 15% for the consensus team, 32% for the commander and 29% for independent agents, and of the different network conditions, only limited radio range changed a team's breach rate, raising the commander's. In consensus team, giving a low-cost LLM more authority over the bid computation raised breaches by 38 percentage points. With birds and civilian drones in airspace, we added a photograph for each to the input text state. Only the near-frontier model treated them apart from intruders. Intruders disguised as existing taxidermy bird drones were mistook as birds by both low-cost and near-frontier LLMs, going unpursued in 59-61% of near-frontier decisions (1-16% before). However, a one-sentence forewarning in the prompt lowered it to 0-25%. We find that LLM team with a dedicated external conflict resolution protocol results in fewest breaches. ## 1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) increasingly act as the decision-makers of multi-agent teams, from software agents that solve tasks together Guo et al. (2024) ; Shen et al. (2023) ; Zhan et al. (2026) ; Hong et al. (2024) to robot teams that divide work and plan joint actions Kannan et al. (2024) ; Mandi et al. (2024) . In robotics, this division of work is studied as multi-robot task allocation, the question of which robot should execute which task Gerkey and Matarić (2004) . Many LLM teams allocate tasks through a central component, such as an orchestrator that delegates work or a single LLM that assigns subtasks to the whole robot team Yang et al. (2025) ; Kannan et al. (2024) . Central coordination brings scalability bottlenecks and a single point of failure, which leaves the team exposed when the coordinator fails or becomes overloaded Yang et al. (2025) . Decentralized multi-agent LLM systems remove this component. Each agent decides from its own local information and communicates directly with its peers Tran et al. (2025) . Without an orchestrator, the agents of AgentNet Yang et al. (2025) reached higher accuracy than centralized multi-agent baselines on mathematics, logical question-answering and function-calling benchmarks, and robot arms with one LLM agent each proposed sub-task plans and paths through dialogue Mandi et al. (2024) . The evidence is not one-sided. In multi-robot planning tasks, Chen et al. Chen et al. (2024) compared four designs for coordinating the LLMs of a robot team. The robots in the study cooperated without opponents, and each LLM agent was assumed to know the full environment. In the decentralized architecture, agents relay messages through a peer-to-peer network and achieve consensus via sequential debate rounds—iterative challenge-response exchanges designed to verify reasoning steps and organically converge on consistent information. This setup had the lowest success rate, below a central planner, while hybrid designs did best and the central planner scaled worst as robots were added. In a separate study, Kim et al. Kim et al. (2025) evaluated 260 agent-system configurations and found that independent agents, which do not communicate, amplified errors more than centrally coordinated ones, and whether coordination helped depended on the task. Network degradation and agent attrition are unavoidable in distributed multi-agent systems, so we directly compare centralized and decentralized LLM coordination under these conditions. We study this problem in an adversarial game that serves as a simple abstraction of a cyber-physical system, aiming toward applications where an LLM in a hierarchical control setting decides and allocates tasks as an intruder approaches. In perimeter defense, a team of defenders must intercept intruders before they reach a protected region Shishika and Kumar (2020) , and the intruders score for each one that gets through Shishika et al. (2020) . We call an intruder’s entry into the protected region a breach. Perimeter defense belongs to the broader family of pursuit-evasion and reach-avoid differential games Jaleel and Shamma (2020) , in which a team of intruders attempts to reach a target region while defenders attempt to capture or neutralize them Chung et al. (2011) ; Weintraub et al. (2023) ; Weintraub et al. (2020) ; Zhou et al. (2025) . Game-theoretic analyses build team strategies by decomposing the game into local games between small groups and assigning defenders to intruders Shishika et al. (2020) , and learned policies imitate a maximum-matching assignment from each defender’s local perception Lee et al. (2023) . In scenarios such as counter-drone defense, defenders must detect intruders with limited sensing Park et al. (2021) , share observations over a communication network Maity and Pourghorban (2024) , and cope with message loss over non-ideal channels Otte et al. (2020) . Assigning defenders to intruders is therefore an allocation decision made under partial and evolving information, where the contextual reasoning of LLM planners can be beneficial. Acharya et al. Acharya et al. (2026) propose a hierarchical architecture in which a per-team LLM planner assigns tactical roles to robots under partial observability, with execution delegated to low-level model predictive controllers. ## 2 Results
We first describe the testbed, the three defender teams, the five network conditions and the outcome we measure. The next three subsections compare these three teams, all run with the low-cost model on the same 30 seeds. They ask how often each team was breached, how well it divided its tasks among its defenders, and how it responded to message loss, falsified reports and limited radio range. We then run the consensus team at high authority, and run the commander team and the consensus team at high authority with a near-frontier model as well. Last, we add birds and civilian drones to the airspace, show the defenders’ LLMs a photograph of every contact and then disguise the intruders. 2.1 Teams, conditions and outcome We built a testbed in which four defenders protect a region against two or four intruders, and every agent on both teams uses the same low-cost LLM, Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite, once per second, in decision epochs of 1 s (Fig. 7 ; Section 4 ). A defender’s task is to pursue an intruder, hold one of four stations around the region or scout part of the arena, and an intruder’s task is to attack the region or evade a defender. Model predictive control Mayne et al. (2000) turns each agent’s task into motion every 0.1 s, and a barrier filter adjusts that motion to keep agents apart except for when a defender is pursuing to neutralize an intruder, in which case the barrier constraint of defender is switched off for only the target being chased. Each agent sees opponents only inside a narrow sensor wedge, so it acts on its beliefs. A belief holds an opponent’s last observed position and velocity, from the agent’s own sensor or a teammate’s message, projected forward based on the velocity and time of observation. The three defender team configurations considered in this study differ how their beliefs are turned into tasks (Fig. 1 , Supplementary Videos 1–3). In the commander team, the defender with the lowest index (by default defender 0) is assigned the commander role, and its LLM assigns a task to every defender, including itself. In the consensus team, each defender scores every task it could take, and this score is its bid for the task.
