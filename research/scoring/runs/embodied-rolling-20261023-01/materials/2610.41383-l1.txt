# BioAgent: Self-Directed Skill Acquisition and Capability Growth for Long-Horizon Laboratory Protocols

## Abstract
Executing an unfamiliar laboratory protocol requires a robot to identify missing capabilities and determine whether each operation remains valid as the experiment progresses. This letter presents BioAgent, an embodied agent that addresses these coupled challenges of capability sufficiency and experimental validity. It matches human-reviewed requirements to validated skills and task packages, selects tool-based implementations for missing behaviors, and requests human teaching when needed during learning. Learned skills undergo sequential task-package validation to check outcomes and compatibility between successive operations. During execution, precondition, outcome, and experimental-state checks govern progression, autonomous recovery, or termination. Execution evidence guides tool selection, while accepted experience supports offline policy updates and task packaging. On the second task, BioAgent reuses 17 skill types unchanged, revises two existing implementations, and acquires five new types. On two dual-arm laboratory tasks with 20 and 31 ordered skill invocations, BioAgent achieves complete-task success rates of 66\% and 58\% with autonomous recovery over 50 runs per task. Across 25 atomic skills evaluated over 50 autonomous trials each with recovery disabled, success rates range from 84\% to 100\%, with 17 skills achieving 50/50.

## I Introduction
A robot prepared for one laboratory protocol is not necessarily ready for the next. New protocols can require operations or skill combinations beyond its finite repertoire. Even when the required skills are available, earlier steps or failures can change the conditions under which they can be used. Executing an unfamiliar protocol therefore poses two coupled challenges: capability sufficiency , whether the robot has the capabilities required by the task, and experimental validity , whether the current state permits execution under the specified constraints. Automated laboratory systems carry out routine handling operations as part of multi-step experimental workflows [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . More recent systems use protocol instructions to guide robotic planning [ 4 ] and vision–language–action (VLA) models to execute laboratory manipulations [ 5 , 6 ] . However, translating a protocol into a plan does not ensure that the robot can perform every required operation. Even when most of the workflow is supported by existing skills or their compositions, a single missing behavior can prevent its completion. Guided demonstration and continual skill learning provide ways to acquire additional capabilities [ 7 , 8 ] . The challenge is therefore to identify which protocol requirements remain unsupported and direct learning toward those gaps while retaining validated skills for reuse. Capability sufficiency alone does not establish experimental validity. Each operation acts on the state produced by preceding steps. After a partially completed liquid transfer, for example, the robot may still be able to repeat the motion, but doing so may no longer satisfy the experimental requirements. The decision to continue depends on the current sample, container, and liquid states and the protocol’s timing constraints. A missing capability calls for acquisition; an unmet but restorable precondition calls for preparation or recovery; a confirmed validity violation requires termination. This letter presents BioAgent, an embodied agent system that enables robots to execute unfamiliar laboratory protocols by closing only the capability gaps that prevent valid execution, while retaining validated capabilities for future reuse (Fig. ). BioAgent matches human-reviewed requirements to validated skills and task packages, then refines unsupported requirements to distinguish reusable compositions from missing atomic behaviors. For each missing behavior, it selects or combines tools and requests human demonstrations or corrections only when needed during learning. Newly learned skills then undergo sequential task-package validation, which checks both their outcomes and the conditions linking successive operations. During execution, precondition, outcome, and experimental-state checks determine whether to proceed, retry, restore preconditions, or terminate. A missing recovery skill identified during development returns the affected requirement to learning. Execution evidence guides subsequent tool choices, while accepted demonstrations, execution traces, and corrections support offline policy updates and task packaging. Validated candidates enter the reusable repertoire, linking task execution to capability growth in subsequent experiments. Our contributions are threefold: 1. We develop a targeted capability-acquisition procedure that matches task requirements to validated skills and compositions, identifies missing behaviors, and directs tool-based skill construction and, when needed, human teaching toward those gaps. 2. We develop a recovery mechanism that couples experimental validity with capability acquisition . BioAgent uses observed partial effects to identify admissible recovery, composes validated skills or requests targeted teaching for missing recovery behaviors during learning, and reassesses sample, container, liquid-state, and timing constraints before resumption. 3. We present an acquisition–execution–reuse mechanism that separates skill learning from sequential task-package validation, uses execution evidence to guide tool selection, and converts accepted experience into candidate policies and task packages for validation and reuse. Experiments on two dual-arm laboratory tasks demonstrate BioAgent’s ability to close capability gaps selectively while retaining validated skills for reuse. On the second task, BioAgent reuses 17 skill types unchanged, revises two implementations, and acquires five new skill types, expanding the validated repertoire from 20 to 25 types. Validity-aware recovery improves complete-task success by 12 and 18 percentage points on the two tasks, respectively.

## III Method
BioAgent closes capability gaps through targeted learning and sequential task package validation (Fig. 2 ; Algorithm 1 ). The Analyzer derives requirements, the Planner acquires and reuses skills, and the Monitor checks execution and updates tool use experience. The validation process identifies necessary skill and transition revisions. III-A Problem Formulation and Capability Representation a Experimental requirements Given a laboratory request and protocol instructions P , the Analyzer uses its knowledge to form ordered operation–object–outcome requirements: Q(P)=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}),\qquad q_{i}=(o_{i},b_{i},g_{i}). (1) Here, o_{i} denotes an operation, b_{i} its objects and parameters, and g_{i} its required outcome. Each requirement specifies preconditions and constraints on samples, containers, liquid states, and timing. Human review confirms their meaning and order. Capability matching refines Q(P) into skill-level requirements. A task is considered successful only if all mandatory outcomes are achieved in the specified order and all constraints are maintained throughout execution. b Skills, packages, and tools BioAgent maintains tools \mathcal{T} and persistent validated skill and task package libraries \mathcal{K},\mathcal{B} with initial \mathcal{K}_{0}=\mathcal{B}_{0}=\varnothing . An atomic skill specifies an operation, bindings, preconditions, and a verifiable outcome; its record links these to an implementation, development stage, and validation evidence. Implementations may contain multiple motions or tool calls. Task packages retain versioned skill references, bindings, order, endpoint requirements, and inter-operation constraints. The toolbox comprises execution backends and pipelines, a device interface, and perception tools (Table I ). Skill implementations use VLA policy inference, reviewed trajectory replay with visual or object pose guided adaptation when needed, or instrument command execution. c Learning and validation stages Learning constructs or revises skills with human teaching when needed. Once all required skills are learned or validated, validation checks their ordered composition and registers passing candidates in \mathcal{K},\mathcal{B} .
