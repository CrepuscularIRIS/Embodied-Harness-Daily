# What Makes a VLA Reactive? A Study of Execution Strategies for Dynamic Manipulation

## Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) models predict chunks of future actions, and when objects move, those chunks go stale before they are executed. We study what makes a pretrained VLA reactive, using \pihalf{} on two dynamic simulation benchmarks and three real-robot tasks. We vary the prediction and execution horizons, compare synchronous and asynchronous inference, test methods that deliver actions sooner or keep successive chunks continuous, and adapt the policy with motion cues and latent-space reinforcement learning. The best prediction horizon is about one second, and the best execution horizon depends on the checkpoint. Short horizons help more with asynchronous execution, and methods that keep the trajectory continuous outperform those that only reduce latency. Adapting the policy repairs targeted failures, but on the conveyor task changing the execution horizon gained as much. Reactivity depends on the execution pipeline as much as on the policy, so both should be evaluated together.

## 1 Introduction
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models predict action chunks to support coherent motion with fewer inference calls. When objects move during inference and execution, these predictions can become stale. Shorter execution horizons allow more frequent replanning, but increase inference demand and can introduce discontinuities between chunks. Dynamic manipulation therefore requires balancing observation freshness, computational cost, and execution stability. Recent approaches address these challenges through faster action generation, asynchronous execution, trajectory continuity, and temporal information [ 1 , 11 , 14 , 5 ] . We used \pi_{0.5} [ 9 ] on DOM [ 14 ] , DOMINO [ 5 ] , and three real-robot dynamic tasks. In this work, we investigate: (1) How do prediction and execution horizons affect performance? (2) How do scheduling and trajectory continuity affect the benefits of frequent replanning? (3) Can temporal information and reward-guided adaptation, including DSRL [ 13 ] , improve dynamic manipulation? Our results show that frequent replanning alone does not ensure better performance: its benefits depend on the execution pipeline and the learned policy.

## 2 Study Design
Problem Setup Given visual observations o_{t} , language instruction \ell , and proprioceptive state p_{t} , the policy predicts an action chunk A_{t}=(a_{t\mid t},\ldots,a_{t+H-1\mid t})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid o_{t},\ell,p_{t}) , where H is the prediction horizon . The execution horizon s\leq H specifies how many actions are executed from a predicted chunk. Thus, prediction length and execution commitment can be varied separately [ 1 , 11 ] . In synchronous execution , the controller executes the selected actions, then waits for the next prediction. Asynchronous execution overlaps inference with ongoing motion, reducing this waiting time. Inference latency \tau corresponds to a delay of d action steps. Asynchronous execution must account for motion during this delay when selecting or conditioning the next chunk. Switching between independently generated chunks can also introduce discontinuities in the commanded trajectory [ 1 ] . We therefore study execution horizons, inference scheduling, and continuity, and whether temporal information or policy adaptation can compensate for these effects. Experimental Setup We evaluate \pi_{0.5} -based configurations on DOM [ 14 ] and DOMINO [ 5 ] simulation tasks and three real-robot tasks using a Franka Panda: moving-cup placement , rolling-can grasping , and conveyor-belt picking . The conveyor task uses three cubes with a red target cube under stationary, slow ( 2.5\,\mathrm{cm/s} ), and fast ( 5\,\mathrm{cm/s} ) belt conditions. Left, Middle, and Right denote the target’s position along the stationary belt or its relative position within the moving three-cube sequence. Training, hardware, control rates, task configurations, and evaluation protocols are detailed in the appendix. 2.1 Intervention Selection and Implementation We select approaches that address complementary limitations of dynamic manipulation: temporal commitment, delayed or discontinuous execution, and insufficient motion information or behavioral adaptation. Together they decide how old the observation behind each executed action can be. We vary H during fine-tuning and s at deployment with a fixed policy. A fixed s treats every moment the same, so we also test whether the policy can choose s itself with Adaptive Action Chunking (AAC) [ 10 ] . AAC samples several candidate chunks, measures the entropy of the predicted actions, and ends a chunk early when the entropy is high, subject to a minimum action-magnitude constraint in Cartesian space. Scheduling and Continuity Interventions A shorter s only helps if new actions arrive in time and join the old ones smoothly. We therefore compare synchronous and asynchronous execution across execution horizons, and evaluate methods that address each requirement as separate \pi_{0.5} configurations: FASTER and DynamicVLA make actions available sooner, RTC and ABPolicy keep the trajectory continuous across chunks. Our DynamicVLA adaptation uses continuous inference and latency-aware action skipping, adding a minimum execution window while retaining the \pi_{0.5} architecture and inputs [ 14 ] . With FASTER, we get near-term actions earlier through horizon-aware sampling, action streaming, and early stopping [ 11 ] . To test whether continuity-aware chunk transitions improve dynamic performance, we evaluate RTC and ABPolicy. RTC uses inference-time inpainting to align new predictions with overlapping actions from the previous chunk without additional policy training [ 1 ] . ABPolicy combines bidirectional prediction with continuity-constrained spline refitting. We adapt it to Cartesian actions and train the policy to predict spline control points [ 16 ] . Motion Information and Policy Adaptation The interventions above change how the policy is executed, the last two change the policy itself. PUMA gives it information about how the object moves, and DSRL adjusts what it does with that information. PUMA incorporates historical optical flow and future object-centric feature supervision during training [ 5 ] . DSRL trains a latent-space actor and critic while keeping the underlying VLA frozen [ 13 ] . We use DSRL-SAC with a short visual history aggregated by a temporal convolution for the actor and critic, while the VLA retains its current-observation input.

## 3 Experimental Findings and Discussion
We organize our evaluation around three research questions: RQ1: How do prediction and execution horizons affect dynamic manipulation performance? RQ2: How do inference scheduling and trajectory continuity affect the benefits of frequent replanning? RQ3: Can temporal information and reward-guided adaptation improve dynamic manipulation? The best prediction horizon is 25 steps on DOM and 30 steps on DOMINO. At their control rates of 25 and 30 Hz, both are one second of motion (Fig. 2 a). With longer chunks, the robot keeps executing a plan made for an object that has since moved on. Deployment: the best execution horizon depends on the checkpoint, not on replanning more often. The 1k checkpoint works best at s{=}5 and the 5k checkpoint at s{=}15 (Fig. 2 b). The 5k policy barely moves during the first steps of each chunk. If we replan sooner, the robot sees almost the same scene, predicts the same slow start again, and never gets going. Replanning helps only when the new prediction is different from the old one. Adaptive horizons do not help. AAC never beats the best fixed horizon and fails on the static and fast belts (Fig. 2 c). It samples several candidate chunks to measure the entropy of the predicted actions, and this extra inference delays the next chunk. Entropy is the wrong trigger here.
