===ABSTRACT===
LLM agents have made remarkable progress in robot manipulation, yet they suffer from expensive inference, dense step-by-step control, and tool bottlenecks. We introduce SOLVE, a training-free, versatile and efficient agent policy: a multimodal LLM agent writes a sparse, ordered sequence of 6-DoF end-effector keyposes per call, observes the result, and writes the next, while a motion planner interpolates between them. The agent decides the number and spacing of keyposes for both fast long-range motion and fine-grained manipulation. This granularity matches the sparsity of teleoperated manipulation: most demonstrations in DROID can be compressed into a few keyposes instead of dense actions. SOLVE grounds keyposes with sparse depth queries answered by noisy sources, such as multi-view triangulation, stereo, or monocular depth, and copes with that noise itself. It also improves from execution feedback, reflecting on its own rollouts and distilling a few rules per task. SOLVE reaches state-of-the-art performance of 74.5% on RoboDojo and 84.5% on RoboLab, with only $1.7 per episode on average, 12x less than previous dense-control agent policies. On a real robot, the same agent achieves an 88% success rate (75 of 85 episodes) over 16 challenging zero-shot tasks. Our project page is at https://serenos.github.io/solve-website.

===INTRO===
Robot manipulation policies have progressed from small task-specific networks trained with reinforcement or imitation learning [ 51 , 26 , 71 , 14 ] to vision-language-action (VLA) models [ 3 , 22 , 35 , 1 ] and world-action models (WAMs) [ 27 , 7 , 25 ] , yet all remain data-hungry: they need large teleoperation corpora for each robot and benchmark, and transfer poorly beyond them. Frontier multimodal LLMs offer a training-free alternative and can now control robots directly [ 64 , 56 , 69 , 32 ] , but current agents sit at one of two extremes. Step-wise agents [ 69 , 56 , 64 ] issue one small step per model call, so every step costs seconds of latency and money, and errors compound. Code-as-policy agents [ 40 , 55 , 19 ] instead generate programs over a primitive library, which becomes a bottleneck: uncovered tasks require new primitives, written by hand or searched offline [ 19 , 39 ] , and feedback arrives only when the program returns, too late to correct a failed step. This raises a key question: what action granularity is appropriate for an agent policy? We compress 57,534 successful DROID episodes [ 34 ] into end-effector keyposes joined by straight lines ( Fig. 1 b), and find that over 91% of the travel lies on segments longer than 5 cm, and that a grasp or release needs only 6 keyposes instead of 96 recorded actions. We therefore hypothesize that, for an agent policy, a sequence of sparse end-effector keyposes suffices for manipulation, in place of a dense stream of actions. We present SOLVE, a training-free framework in which a multimodal LLM agent writes sparse, ordered end-effector keyposes, and a motion planner [ 57 ] executes straight-line motions between them ( Fig. 1 c). Each keypose is an absolute 6-DoF grasp-point pose with a gripper command; one call specifies up to five keyposes, roughly one primitive such as approach, grasp, and lift, and the gripper actuates only upon arrival. The agent decides how many keyposes to use, how far apart, and how fast: a single keypose for a long free-space motion, and short, slow ones near contact. After each sequence, the agent inspects the outcome before predicting the next one, so visual feedback enters its reasoning at every primitive rather than only at the checks a program anticipates. Because each sparse keypose is an absolute commitment, it must be metrically accurate. SOLVE therefore grounds keyposes through sparse metric queries : the agent specifies the few pixels on which its next keypose depends, and a tool returns their 3D positions, each annotated with its measurement modality. In RoboDojo and RoboLab simulation, which provide no stereo cameras, the tool triangulates each queried pixel across multiple views by epipolar matching, and falls back to monocular depth estimated by Depth Anything 3 [ 42 ] , scaled by the known table height, when no multi-view match is found; on the real robot, it directly uses depth from FoundationStereo [ 58 ] on a stereo infrared camera. Over multiple rounds of keypose reasoning, the agent adapts to the resulting depth noise by itself. On difficult tasks, the agent further improves from its own execution feedback without training: it reflects on rollouts from layouts disjoint from evaluation, and consolidates the notes from several rounds into at most five bounded rules per task, which are provided to it at test time. Since no component of SOLVE is trained, and keyposes live in end-effector space while the planner resolves embodiment-specific execution, the same agent deploys on different robots without modification. SOLVE achieves 74.5% on the dual-arm RoboDojo under the leaderboard’s metric ( Tab. 1 ) and 84.5% on the single-arm Franka RoboLab [ 63 ] . On a real UR12e, it completes 75 of 85 episodes across 16 challenging zero-shot tasks ( Tab. 4 ), including table cleaning, peg-in-hole, sketch drawing, and playing Gomoku against a human. It does so at $1.7 per episode on average, with one quarter of the model calls and 12 \times lower cost than a step-wise agent ( Fig. 1 d). Our contributions are as follows: • We show that teleoperated demonstrations compress into a small number of keyposes and, building on this finding, propose SOLVE, a framework in which an LLM agent writes sparse, ordered keyposes for direct manipulation. • We propose sparse metric queries, combining cross-view epipolar matching with depth estimation to ground keyposes accurately and efficiently in 3D from RGB in simulation, and use stereo depth on the real robot. • We enable the agent to improve from rollouts, and show that SOLVE transfers without modification across robots and benchmarks, achieving state-of-the-art results on RoboDojo, RoboLab, and a real robot at 12 \times lower cost.
