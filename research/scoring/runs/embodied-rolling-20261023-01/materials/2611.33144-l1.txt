===ABSTRACT===
Learning for dexterous manipulation is limited by the scarcity of high-quality demonstrations: retargeting from human video is imprecise, teleoperation gives no force feedback to the operator, and exoskeleton-based collection is hard to scale up. We present PyRUA-Dex, a framework that enables coding agents to develop dexterous manipulation through programmatic policy, without the need of demonstrations. The programmatic policy is developed in two stages: during exploration, multiple agents explore in parallel for task skills and ways to compose them, and a distilling agent condenses the successful attempts into a task library and one program. During refinement, this program is improved through randomized layouts for enhanced robustness and execution efficiency. In simulated environments, PyRUA-Dex achieves SOTA on Bench2Dex with a score of 72.9 and reaches 81.0% success on DexJoCo under full randomization, ahead of every trained policy on both benchmarks. In real world experiments on a bimanual platform with two 22-DoF hands, PyRUA-Dex learns to play piano, write calligraphy, and other tasks efficiently: learning a new piece from sheet music takes as little as 30 minutes. These results suggest that PyRUA-Dex is an effective framework for developing dexterous manipulation policies with coding agents in the absence of high quality dexterous manipulation demonstrations.

===INTRO===
Dexterous manipulation is learned today mainly from demonstrations. Imitation learning [ 51 , 10 ] , vision-language-action models [ 5 , 18 , 3 , 4 , 25 ] and world-action models [ 13 ] are all trained on teleoperated episodes, and data generators [ 24 , 17 ] expand a few such episodes into many. For multi-fingered hands, however, high-quality demonstrations are scarce. Retargeting human video to a robot hand is imprecise, teleoperation gives the operator no force feedback during contact, and exoskeleton-based collection is hard to scale. The data are also tied to the hand they were collected on, so a new hand requires new data and retraining. Policies trained on such data remain fragile. On Bench2Dex, which evaluates bimanual tasks on 12 arm–hand embodiments [ 49 ] , the strongest trained policies succeed in little more than half of the episodes [ 19 , 2 ] , and all of them degrade when a layout’s appearance or object placement is perturbed. Coding agents offer an alternative to collecting demonstrations. A programmatic policy represents behavior as a program rather than as network weights, so it can be read, tested and verified [ 39 , 40 ] . Language models already write programs that control robots [ 20 , 34 , 16 ] and improve them from environment feedback [ 41 , 23 , 32 , 45 ] , and general coding agents run, test and debug software for hours [ 48 , 26 ] . Robot programs, however, are typically written once from a prompt [ 20 ] or found by search in a small domain-specific language [ 38 , 21 ] , and are not refined for robustness across layouts. For dexterous hands, the agent has so far stayed in the loop instead: it observes and acts in every test episode [ 31 ] , consumes millions of tokens per episode, and still fails most episodes within the benchmark’s step budget. We ask whether a coding agent can learn a dexterous task once, during development, and produce a program that then runs on its own reliably without per-episode cost. Learning such a program poses two distinct problems. The first is discovering a solution: the grasps, the ordering of the two hands and the motions that complete each stage of the task. This is a search problem that requires many attempts on a few layouts, and it proceeds faster when parallel agents share their progress. The second is making the solution robust. A dexterous task consists of consecutive stages, and its success rate is the product of the stage success rates , the probability of completing each stage given that the preceding stages were completed. A program that succeeds on one layout can therefore fail on most others: five stages at 80% each give an overall success rate of only 33%. In our experiments, most failed runs end with steps to spare, at a stage that was not completed (Section 4.3 ), so failures come from unreliable stages rather than from the step budget. Because the overall rate cannot exceed the lowest stage rate, robustness requires measuring every stage over many layouts and improving the least reliable one. PyRUA-Dex addresses these two problems in two stages (Figure 2 ). In the exploration stage (Stage 1), multiple coding agents work on one task in parallel in simulation. They write and run code, rewind the simulator to retry a stage, use the number of completed stages as feedback, and share their progress with one another. A distilling agent then condenses the successful programs into a task library and one program. In the refinement stage (Stage 2), an agent refines this program over many randomized layouts. It measures the stage success rates, improves the least reliable stage, and keeps a revision only if it performs no worse on layouts that no agent has seen. Agents and programs control every robot through the same codebox , a layer of hand, arm and sensing calls built from the robot’s description, so the procedure applies to any hand the codebox can model. The resulting policy is general Python and runs frozen. It takes the inputs of an ordinary policy (calibrated cameras, joint positions and fingertip touch), calls no language model, and runs at the robot’s control rate. Because it measures the scene in every episode, it remains reliable when a layout is perturbed. On Bench2Dex, the frozen programs reach a score of 72.9, a new SOTA ahead of every trained policy, and at the benchmark’s step budget they succeed 2.4 times as often as the same model, GPT-6 Astra, acting online (Sections 4.2 and 4.4 ). On DexJoCo, a second benchmark with Allegro hands [ 42 ] , they succeed in 81.0% of episodes under full randomization, against 40.3% for the strongest trained policy, and are above each trained policy on every task (Section 4.5 ). On a real bimanual platform with two 22-DoF hands, the same two stages run in a calibrated simulation that is updated with the robot’s feedback after each round, and the frozen programs then run on the robot (Section 5 ).
