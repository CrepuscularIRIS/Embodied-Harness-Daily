# Time-Indexed Skill Success Models for Latency-Aware Skill Selection and Timing in Dynamic Manipulation

## Abstract
Robots that manipulate moving objects act on observations that are outdated when the chosen skill starts, and the best skill can change within a few hundred milliseconds. Skill competence models, however, predict success for a launch at the moment of observation. We propose time-indexed skill success models, which predict whole-task success when a skill is launched after a given delay. Trained on rollouts branched at a grid of launch delays, they let a receding-horizon planner select both the skill and its launch time under fixed or random latency. In a MuJoCo study in which a UR5e captures a sliding puck with three skills, the planner exceeded a static competence model by 2.8 to 6.2 percentage points at 100 to 300 ms of latency, including when that model was evaluated on point or sampled forecasts of the launch state. Across five complete runs (three training seeds and two distribution shifts), all 80 paired 95% intervals against four baselines excluded zero. On delayed launches, the time-indexed model was calibrated (expected calibration error 0.009 against 0.239), and pausing the world during latency overstated success by up to 23 points. On a real UR5e that captures a rolling ball after 0.2 s of injected latency, a static model trained in a hardware-matched simulator predicted 99% success on a prespecified subset of 35 delayed launches that it rated at least 0.8. Of these, 63% succeeded (95% interval 46 to 77%), and the time-indexed model predicted 56%. In this single-skill, predictable regime, a matched physics-based forecast was also well calibrated.

## I Introduction
Robots act on information that is already old. Between the exposure of a camera frame and the first motion of a skill, the system transfers the image, estimates the state, runs inference and sends the command. For current vision-language-action policies, this end-to-end latency is about 100 to 300 ms [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . When the object is at rest, the delay costs only time, but when it moves, the robot acts in a world that differs from the one it observed. For example, a puck sliding at 0.5 m/s travels 5 cm in 100 ms, and an interception that was feasible at the moment of observation may no longer be feasible when the skill starts. Skill-based and task-and-motion planners select skills with learned competence models, such as initiation classifiers, affordance values and success discriminators, which map the current state to the probability that a skill succeeds [ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] . These models estimate the success of a skill that starts immediately, which is not possible under latency. The planner needs the probability that the task succeeds if skill k starts after a delay \tau , which includes the unavoidable latency and any extra wait that the planner chooses. Waiting is a real option in dynamic tasks. A puck that is too fast to grasp may become graspable once friction has slowed it down, while an interception that is possible now may soon expire (see Fig. 1 ). The standard remedy for delay is prediction, as in delay-compensated control [ 9 ] and in robot policies that condition on the state rolled forward to execution time [ 10 , 11 ] . At the skill level, the equivalent is to evaluate a static competence model on a forecast of the launch state. We show that this is exact only in expectation over the forecast distribution and that the error of a point forecast grows with the forecast spread relative to the tolerance of the skill. With several skills, even sampled forecasts recovered only part of the loss in our experiments. In this letter, we therefore learn skill competence as a function of the launch delay. A time-indexed skill success model G_{k}(z,\tau) predicts the probability that the whole task succeeds when skill k is launched \tau seconds after the observation of the decision state z . The letter makes three contributions. First, we formulate skill competence as a function of the decision state and the launch delay, and relate it to static models evaluated on forecasts, which shows when a forecast is sufficient (Section III ). Second, we introduce branched rollouts that give exact training labels for delayed launches, and a receding-horizon planner that selects the skill and its launch time under fixed or random latency (Section IV ). Third, we compare the approach with static, forecast-based and rule-based planners in a simulation whose clock keeps running during latency, with replication over seeds and distribution shifts, a calibration analysis and a frozen-clock comparison (Sections V and VI ), and we test the predicted calibration gap on a real UR5e (Section VII ). Unlike delay-aware continuous policies, the approach works at the level of skill selection and decides which skill to launch and when from an estimate of whole-task success.

## III Problem Formulation
A robot observes a dynamic scene and, at decision times t , selects one of K skills k\in\mathcal{K} . Once launched, a skill runs in closed loop until it terminates, and fixed follow-up skills then complete the task. Let z_{t} denote the decision state estimated from the observations available at time t . In our tasks, z_{t} contains the estimated position and velocity of the object, quantities derived from them, and the time left until a deadline, which we call the slack s . A decision taken on z_{t} cannot take effect before t+\ell , where the latency \ell includes perception, computation and communication delays. The planner may also wait. It selects a skill k and an extra delay \delta from a finite set \Delta\ni 0 of candidate waits, and the skill starts at t+\tau with the launch delay \tau=\ell+\delta . The robot holds a ready pose until launch. We define the time-indexed skill success model as G_{k}(z,\tau)=\Pr\big(\text{success}\,\big|\,z_{t}=z,\ \text{$k$ starts at $t+\tau$}\big), (1) where success means that the whole task, including the follow-up skills, is completed before the deadline. A static competence model corresponds to G_{k}(z,0) , which is the success probability of a launch at the moment of observation. When the latency is known only through its distribution p(\ell) , the planner selects (k^{\star},\delta^{\star})=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{k\in\mathcal{K},\ \delta\in\Delta}\ \mathbb{E}_{\ell\sim p}\big[G_{k}(z_{t},\ell+\delta)\big]. (2) If the latency of the decision is known, ( 2 ) reduces to maximising G_{k}(z_{t},\ell+\delta) . The following identity links the two kinds of model. Proposition 1 If the robot does not act on the scene before launch and z_{t} is a sufficient statistic of the observation history for the future of the task, then for every skill k and delay \tau\geq 0 G_{k}(z,\tau)=\mathbb{E}\big[G_{k}(z_{t+\tau},0)\,\big|\,z_{t}=z\big]. (3) Proof: A launch at t+\tau is an immediate launch from the decision state z_{t+\tau} . By sufficiency, its success probability given the history up to t+\tau is G_{k}(z_{t+\tau},0) . Since the robot idles, the distribution of z_{t+\tau} given z_{t} does not depend on the decision, and the tower property of conditional expectation gives ( 3 ). ∎ Proposition 1 follows directly from the tower property, but it makes the error of each practical predictor explicit and shows when a forecast is sufficient. The static model G_{k}(z_{t},0) ignores the delay. A static model evaluated on a point forecast \hat{z}_{\tau}=\mathbb{E}[z_{t+\tau}\mid z_{t}] moves the expectation inside the function, and with \Sigma_{\tau}=\operatorname{Cov}(z_{t+\tau}\mid z_{t}) its error is, to second order, G_{k}(z_{t},\tau)-G_{k}(\hat{z}_{\tau},0)\approx\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\nabla^{2}_{z}G_{k}(\hat{z}_{\tau},0)\,\Sigma_{\tau}\big). (4) This error is large where success changes sharply with the state, for example at the edge of a capture tolerance, and it grows with the forecast spread, and hence with the delay and the disturbance level. Averaging the static model over sampled forecasts estimates the right-hand side of ( 3 ), but only as well as the forecast distribution is known and the static model, trained on observed states, extrapolates to forecast states. When z_{t} only summarises the history, ( 3 ) is itself approximate. A time-indexed model is instead fitted directly to the left-hand side on exact samples and needs no forecast model at run time.
