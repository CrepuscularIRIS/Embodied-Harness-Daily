# Grounding Object-to-Object Hazard Reasoning into 3D Risk Fields for Task-Conditioned Safety

## Abstract
Safety in robotics does not solely depend on geometric constraints, but also on semantics: the given task, environment properties, and interactions between objects in the scene. We present a framework based on vision-language models (VLMs) to infer task- and scene-dependent safety constraints for robot manipulation. We provide the VLM a structured safety tool based on an RGB-D observation and a natural-language task instruction that first decides whether the task should be rejected since its execution may result in a non-recoverable hazardous outcome. Otherwise, it identifies preventable object-pair hazards and specifies the spatial relations that must be avoided. Open-vocabulary segmentation and depth information are then used to ground the detected objects in 3D and construct continuous risk fields that represent the corresponding hazardous regions. These fields are integrated with an obstacle avoidance module that corrects nominal robot motions during execution while accounting for the risk field. We evaluate the framework on preventable hazards, tasks requiring rejection, and safe variants in simulation and the real-world. Project website: https://vlm-hazard-riskfield.github.io

## I Introduction
Robots that manipulate everyday objects must anticipate if and how their actions can cause harm. Doing so requires connecting what they see with what they are asked to do: an object’s properties, its surroundings, and its intended use together determine which interactions are hazardous. Collision avoidance provides an essential foundation [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] , but geometry alone is far from sufficient for identifying safety constraints. We study the broader challenge of contextual safety [ 5 , 6 , 7 ] in robotic manipulation, with the aim of enabling robots to derive safety constraints from the task and scene and utilize visual context and structured reasoning to guide task rejection and motion correction. These ideas have been explored both in manipulation [ 5 ] and in navigation [ 6 , 8 , 7 ] . In navigation, constraints are defined over the regions the robot itself may occupy. In manipulation, the robot additionally acts on the environment through the object it holds, so safety depends on the relationship between that object and the remaining objects of the scene. For example, placing a metal cup in a microwave for heating can create a hazard regardless of the transport path, whereas carrying coffee over a laptop creates a hazard that can be avoided by routing the cup around it (Figure. 1 ). Hazard reasoning must therefore decide whether a task should be performed at all and, if so, which regions become hazardous for the manipulated object due to the task. In prior work, this knowledge typically comes from a human who specifies the constraints [ 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 ] or from Hamilton-Jacobi reachability [ 11 ] . However, a predefined hazard set could not cover all possible hazards that arise in an open-world environment. There is increasing interest in extracting this contextual knowledge directly from large language models (LLMs). Existing work in manipulation segments visual input into a semantic map, uses a pretrained model to reason about safe and unsafe behavior, and enforces the resulting constraints through control barrier functions (CBFs) [ 5 ] ; however, reducing the scene to a language representation limits the context available for reasoning. Vision-language models (VLMs) jointly process semantic and visual information, allowing reasoning over objects, attributes, and relationships within a scene. This direction has been explored for navigation [ 7 ] , but its application to hazards induced by object interactions in manipulation remains underexplored. Knowing what is unsafe, however, does not by directly prevent it. Safety filters such as CBFs [ 12 , 13 ] provide formal guarantees that corrected nominal trajectories to keep the robot safe with respect to spatially unsafe sets, under their respective modeling and control assumptions. These sets and rules must be specified in advance. Foundation models inherit a high-level semantic understanding to reason about potential hazard interactions, but their predictions need grounding to be applied. A hazard detection pipeline must therefore bridge these two ideas: it must reason about safety based on the environment and task, and ground the predicted hazards in a representation that a classical filter can act on continuously. In this work, we propose a framework centered around a VLM with visual context and a text description of the task to detect hazards arising from interactions between objects and induced by the robot’s actions. A structured schema-based toolset first determines whether the task should be rejected and, otherwise, outputs object-pair hazards with geometric restrictions. Open-vocabulary segmentation and depth ground these restrictions in an automatically generated 3D risk field, which an obstacle modulation filter uses to correct the manipulated object’s motion. In parallel, we present a systematic study of how context representation and structured reasoning affect pretrained models for object-to-object hazard detection. Our contributions are: • A structured VLM safety toolset that separates task rejection from identifying preventable object-pair hazards, and outputs geometric restrictions that can be grounded in the scene. • An automatically generated 3D risk field, built from open-vocabulary segmentation and depth, that represents non-convex hazard geometry without fitting shape primitives, and connects VLM hazard reasoning to obstacle modulation. • A study of context representation and structured hazard reasoning, identifying false-rejection failures and evaluating downstream safe execution in simulation and real-world manipulation. We evaluate our methodology in simulation and real-world experiments, comparing it against task-only reasoning, reasoning over scene descriptions, and geometric obstacle modulation. The evaluation covers tasks requiring rejection, preventable hazards, and safe variants, assessing both hazard avoidance and task completion.

## III Problem Statement
Given a natural-language task instruction T , a robot manipulator operating in a scene, whose end-effector position \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3} evolves as \dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{v} , a nominal controller producing a velocity command \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{nom}}=f(\mathbf{x}) , single RGB-D observation I of the scene taken at the initial state, and a pre-trained VLM model \Psi . We assume the nominal controller has no notion of hazards. The objects in the scene are not known a priori, and no predefined safe or unsafe sets are given. With the above information, our goal is to (1) determining whether T should be executed at all and (2) if T can be performed with appropriate precautions, obtain a hazard-free trajectory that completes the task.

## IV Methodology
In this section, we present the hazard detection framework using visual context and task description. IV-A Hazard Reasoning To solve the questions stated in Section III , we divide the hazard reasoning problem into two problem categories: non-recoverable hazards and recoverable hazards, as shown in Figure 1 . I. Non-recoverable hazards. Some user inputs are inherently unsafe regardless of how the trajectory is planned because the hazard arises from the task itself. For example, ”placing a metal cup in a microwave” (Fig. 1 top) is a fire hazard and should never be executed. Therefore, we first need to evaluate \delta=\Psi(I,T)\in\{0,1\}, (1) where \Psi judges whether executing T in the observed scene leads to a non-recoverable unsafe state, and returns the decision, where \delta=1 means a rejected task. II. Recoverable hazards . For tasks that are not refused, \Psi determines if there are any preventable hazards present (e.g., a spilling hazard can be prevented by not moving liquid containers above electronic devices), and infers them from T and the observation I . Each hazard in the set of inferred hazards \mathcal{H} is represented as a tuple: h_{i}=(a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},r_{i})\in\mathcal{H} (2) where a_{i} and b_{i} are the two objects whose interaction produces the hazard, with b being the anchor hazardous object, c_{i} is a hazard description, and r_{i} is the spatial relation to avoid between them, which can be: “avoid_above”, “avoid_below”, “avoid_inside”, “avoid_between”, “avoid_collision” . For example, the user input “move the cup of coffee next to the laptop”, shown in Figure 1 , should be done carefully to avoid spilling liquid on the laptop; the equation in 2 becomes h_{i}=(\textit{"cup"},~\textit{"laptop"},~\textit{"spill\_hazard"},~\textit{"avoid\_above"}) .
