# CRAFT: A Robot-Agent Harness with Scope-Aware Adaptation

## Abstract
A robot may know how to move an object yet still need to learn where it belongs. When a person rejects a placement, the robot must decide whether the correction applies only to that situation or expresses a broader rule. We study this problem in CRAFT (Contextual Requirement Adaptation with Frozen Tools), a framework for learning local requirements without retraining robot skills. It records physical success separately from whether an action meets the requirement. Our scope ladder uses memory when an action has already been judged in a nearly identical situation. Elsewhere, it combines memory, explicit rules and a learner for requirements beyond those rules, weighting their predictions by how well they predicted earlier feedback. Controlled studies show gains over correction memory alone in utility, which rewards acceptable actions and charges for mistakes and questions. In camera-grounded robot simulations, the ladder asks fewer questions than language-model agents with persistent memory, but some of those agents take fewer wrong actions and succeed more often after requirements change. All execution failures and timeouts remain in the evaluation. These results support combining specific memories with broader rules, while leaving reliable adaptation to changing requirements unresolved. All evidence comes from simulation with generated requirements and simulated feedback.

## 1 Introduction
Consider a robot bringing a glass of water to someone working at a table. It sets the glass beside a laptop, near the table’s edge. The person says, “Not there.” Should drinks be kept away from the laptop, or away from the edge? Both rules explain the objection. Once the laptop is moved to the centre of the table, they disagree about whether the original spot is acceptable ( Figure 1 ). We call this the correction’s reach : the situations to which it should apply. A correction interpreted too narrowly allows the same mistake in a new scene; one interpreted too broadly rules out acceptable actions. This problem arises when a robot has usable physical skills but must learn unstated requirements at the site where it operates. Skill libraries and robot-agent harnesses make it possible to reuse existing controllers ( Hu et al., 2026 ) . Memory and rules offer different ways to use that feedback. Memory can recall what happened in a similar situation. A rule can extend the lesson to unfamiliar objects or layouts. Each has a weakness: similar-looking scenes may require different actions, and a rule may be inferred too broadly or applied incorrectly because perception is noisy. We study this choice in CRAFT (Contextual Requirement Adaptation with Frozen Tools). CRAFT is a robot-agent framework that keeps physical skills fixed while allowing the method that learns local requirements to change. It separates whether a motion succeeded from whether the chosen action was acceptable. A failed movement therefore supplies no verdict about the local requirement. Compared methods receive the same kinds of observations, feedback and opportunities to ask questions; each learns only from the outcomes of its own actions. Within CRAFT, we propose the scope ladder . If an action has already received feedback in a nearly identical situation, memory decides. Otherwise, the ladder combines predictions from memory, an explicit set of possible rules, and an additional learner for requirements those rules cannot express. Each predictor is weighted by how well its past predictions matched the feedback that followed. This gives specific experience priority where it applies and allows broader rules to guide decisions elsewhere. The arbitration rule combines established memory, rule-learning and weighting techniques. Existing systems already learn reusable preferences from corrections or maintain revisable user and scene memories ( Zha et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2026b ) . Our focus is the choice between specific memories and broader predictions when feedback consists of sparse yes/no verdicts. We evaluate the method in controlled studies and in a continuing service that carries experience across robot jobs. Contributions. • A formulation and shared framework ( Section 3 ). We formalise learning the reach of action feedback and provide an engineering framework that keeps physical execution, task acceptability and the cost of asking separate. • A method for combining memory and rules ( Section 4 ). The scope ladder gives memory priority on near-identical situations and combines predictions elsewhere according to their earlier predictive performance. • Evidence of benefits and limits ( Sections 6 and 6.5 ). Pre-registered studies find higher utility than correction memory alone on new and repeated situations. Component comparisons link generalisation to rule inference and repeated-situation performance to the memory gate. Descriptive robot-simulation comparisons with a frontier agent and adaptations of DROC and CarryOn find lower query costs for the ladder, alongside tradeoffs in wrong actions and completion after requirements change. All failures remain in the evaluation. All evidence comes from simulation with generated requirements and simulated feedback. The robot comparisons use a shared frozen skill, camera-derived observations and external scene setup; question counts do not measure human effort. They test adaptation within this setting, and reliable revision after unannounced requirement changes remains unresolved. Unsuccessful studies are retained in the appendices.

## 3 Problem and setting
3.1 Learning a correction’s reach Returning to the household example in Figure 1 , suppose the robot can place a glass of water beside a laptop near the table’s edge, or in a clear space in the middle. The household’s rule is to keep drinks away from the laptop, but this rule has not been stated to the robot. It places the glass beside the laptop, and the person says, “Not there.” The placement succeeds physically but violates the requirement. The robot receives no explanation and no verdict about the unchosen location. Both “keep drinks away from the laptop” and “keep drinks away from the table’s edge” fit this feedback. On the next delivery, the laptop has moved to the middle of the table. The original spot is now away from the laptop but still near the edge. Under the first explanation, that spot is acceptable; under the second, it remains unacceptable. The household rule has not changed, only the layout. The robot must decide whether the earlier rejection still applies. This is the question of a correction’s reach . Each delivery is a job, the observed table arrangement is its context, and the candidate placements form the action menu. We formalise these repeated decisions as follows. A stream is a sequence of T jobs at one site. At job t the robot observes a context x_{t} (an RGB-D image of the scene) and a menu \mathcal{A}_{t} of admissible actions. The actions differ in one role : which object to move, or which target to put it on. Actions are drawn from the declared skill menu, but execution can fail; the requirement determines whether an action is acceptable under a site requirement that nobody stated. A hidden rule r maps each context to its acceptable set r(x_{t})\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{t} , which may be empty. On each job the provider either executes one action a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{t} or holds off. Under the feedback level used throughout (F1), this defines a contextual partial-monitoring problem: the provider observes the bandit label y_{t}=[a_{t}\in r(x_{t})] only for the single action it executed, while holding off and failed executions return no verdict, and nothing is revealed about unexecuted actions a\neq a_{t} . In the decision-level studies, T=40 : the first four jobs supply full-menu commissioning labels and the remaining 36 are scored with net utility u_{t}=\begin{cases}+1&\text{execution completes and }a_{t}\in r(x_{t}),\\ -1&\text{execution completes and }a_{t}\notin r(x_{t}),\\ \phantom{+}0&\text{held off or execution failed,}\end{cases} a decision-quality score rather than physical throughput. A provider with the right to ask may instead spend one of three questions per stream, at a cost of 0.5 each, to receive full-information labels \{y_{t}(a)\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}} for every action in the current context. The continuing-service pilot uses T=24 , reports accepted completion over all 20 scored assignments, and accounts separately for physical failures, wrong dispatches and intervention costs ( Section 5 ). A verdict on (x_{t},a_{t}) is informative only through its reach : the set of later context-action pairs (x_{t^{\prime}},a) whose predicted acceptability p(a\mid x_{t^{\prime}}) it changes.
