===ABSTRACT===
Multimodal agents can now control robot arms by calling tools and writing code. Existing benchmarks fall short in three ways: most test one or two arms on short tasks, many pause the world while the agent thinks, and many give it ground-truth object positions. We present RoboTeam, a simulated benchmark where agents control 4 to 16 robot arms in real time from camera images alone. Its 36 tasks are long jobs that the arms must do together, such as passing timbers or building a picture from hundreds of cubes. We start from the most flexible agent system, the adaptive system, where one agent decides when to fork delegates. With it, gpt-6.1-sol and opus-5.5 complete 28 and 27 tasks, and four other models at most 3. But at a high price: a typical task takes gpt-6.1-sol 3.5 hours and 218 million input tokens, and a third of its steps fail at least once and must be retried. We then compare it with three fixed systems: single-agent, hierarchical, and decentralized. All four complete a similar number of tasks but differ in time and tokens. The hierarchical and decentralized systems are faster but use up to 1.9 times the tokens, and the single agent uses fewer tokens but takes 1.6 times as long, so the adaptive system lies between them. Even so, no model or system is yet fast, cheap, and reliable at once. RoboTeam measures these limits where earlier benchmarks cannot: long multi-arm jobs, camera images only, and a world that never pauses.

In the Spider-Man stories, Doctor Octopus controls four extra arms as easily as his own hands. Real robots are far from this, but multimodal agents are getting closer: frontier models can increasingly control robots through tools and generated code [ 16 , 25 , 39 , 11 , 7 , 24 , 46 , 42 ] . Vision-language-action (VLA) policies [ 54 , 19 , 2 ] typically map camera images directly to robot actions. Agents can do more: they reason about a task [ 47 ] , write tools [ 41 ] , recover from failure [ 26 ] , and communicate [ 49 ] . Existing benchmarks capture these strengths only in part, and they fall short in three ways ( Tab. 1 ). First, most test one or two arms on short tasks, whether the controller is a trained policy [ 17 , 28 , 33 ] or a language-model agent [ 45 , 15 , 46 ] . Multi-robot studies compare agent systems but simplify the physical task [ 6 , 37 , 4 ] . Second, many simulated benchmarks pause the world while an agent thinks [ 46 , 32 ] , which a real robot cannot do. Third, many give agents the ground-truth positions of objects [ 27 , 6 , 37 , 11 ] , which a real robot must estimate from its sensors. We therefore still do not know how far agents are from controlling many arms at once, or which agent system suits them. To study this question, we present RoboTeam, a simulated benchmark where agents control 4 to 16 arms in real time from camera images alone ( Fig. 1 ). Its 36 tasks are long multi-arm tasks of four types modeled on real multi-robot work: sharing parts in an assembly cell, laying bricks, carrying long parts, and turning valves ( Sec. 3 ). Each comes at three team sizes and three difficulty levels. They do not demand fast reactions, because language models take seconds to respond [ 32 , 24 ] . Agents move the arms through one robot tool with simple commands, and a sandbox keeps them away from the simulator and the evaluator, which prevents cheating [ 52 , 12 , 40 ] . In this adaptive system, one controller decides for itself when to fork delegates and hand them arms, and it takes the arms back when their jobs end. With this system we test six models, and we then compare it with three fixed systems: single-agent, hierarchical, and decentralized. Further conditions change the harness, what the agent sees, or the tools it may use, which gives 14 conditions ( Fig. 2 ). The best models complete most tasks, but at a high price. With the adaptive system, gpt-6.1-sol and opus-5.5 complete 28 and 27 of the 36 tasks, while the other four models complete at most 3. A typical task takes gpt-6.1-sol 3.5 hours and 218 million input tokens. An atomic step is the smallest unit of progress, such as placing one cube, and 34% of these steps fail at least once and must be retried. Other agent systems trade time for tokens. The four systems complete a similar number of tasks. The hierarchical and decentralized systems are faster than the adaptive system but use 1.9 and 1.7 times its tokens, and the single agent uses fewer tokens but takes 1.6 times as long. The adaptive system lies between them in both time and tokens. What limits the agents is recovering positions from images. Given the ground-truth object positions instead of camera images, gpt-6.1-sol completes all 36 tasks. From images alone it needs two things, and losing either one costs about half of the completed tasks. With low-resolution images it completes 14 tasks, and without the code tools that it uses to measure positions in images, 13. The two best models also work differently. gpt-6.1-sol divides its work among delegates, and opus-5.5 works mostly as one agent that computes more, in either harness. No model or agent system is yet fast, cheap, and reliable at once, and RoboTeam measures all three. We will release the benchmark, the runtime, and all run records.

## 3 The RoboTeam benchmark
3.1 Tasks RoboTeam has 36 tasks: four task types, each at three team sizes and three difficulty levels ( Fig. 3 ). Team size adds arms and work to divide. Difficulty makes the same job physically or visually harder. Transport. As in an assembly cell where robots share parts and must not collide [ 23 , 5 , 43 ] , the arms deliver every item to its target board. Items rest on flat targets (Level 1), plug into sockets (Level 2), or plug into sockets on a sprung board (Level 3). Mosaic. As in robotic bricklaying [ 22 , 14 , 9 ] , the arms build a reference picture from colored cubes, with one cube in each cell. The cells are pockets (Level 1), printed lines (Level 2), or unmarked (Level 3). Relay. As in a team that carries long parts and passes them along [ 21 , 10 , 14 ] , the arms move long timbers to a common goal. Every handover between arms must happen in the air, and a dropped timber must be picked up again. The timber is straight (Level 1), curved with a hanging weight (Level 2), or hinged in the middle with a hanging weight (Level 3). Dial. As in turning valves [ 44 ] that spring back unless held [ 29 ] , the arms find a hidden code by turning spring-loaded knobs and holding them. Their only guide is a display of the best number of correct dials so far. At Level 1 the knobs sit on the table. At Level 2 one arm must hold each module in the air while another turns its knob, and at Level 3 the knob becomes a crank. Two principles guided these tasks. First, teamwork must have value: arms depend on each other through shared space, shared objects, and mutual support, and we exclude tasks that each arm could finish alone. This teamwork is between arms, and one agent may control all of them. Second, tasks must be hard but feasible: every action is physically possible for the Franka Emika Panda arm and its parallel gripper [ 48 ] . 3.2 Environment Three choices define the environment: real time, what agents perceive, and how they act. The first choice is real time: the world advances with the clock and never waits for an agent. A real robot would not wait for a model, and pausing for one agent would also stop its teammates. The world is simulated in MuJoCo [ 38 ] with a 2 ms physics step. The second choice is what agents perceive. Some benchmarks give agents ground-truth object positions [ 27 , 6 , 37 ] or offer them as a separate setting [ 11 ] , and agents do well with this access [ 39 ] . A real robot must estimate positions from its sensors, so our agents get only two kinds of live data. The first is RGB images from fixed cameras, which agents request by name and which each camera renders at 1280\times 800 pixels ( Fig. 4 ). MuJoCo’s OpenGL renderer draws an image in about 30 ms on a laptop GPU, in a separate process, so looking does not slow the world. A task has 14 to 69 cameras: two at every arm, four overviews of the scene, and close-ups of the task objects. The task file also gives the position and the field of view of every camera, so agents can relate several views. The second is the state of the arms, such as the pose and width of each gripper. Agents never receive object positions, depth, or contact information. The third choice is how agents act. Earlier interfaces have two problems. When agent code drives the simulator directly [ 39 , 11 ] , it can also read hidden state. When agents call fixed high-level skills [ 4 , 11 ] , the skills hide low-level failures. We instead give every model one robot tool, and arms move only through it. Its commands are simple: move an arm to a pose, or open, close, or grasp with a gripper. This keeps agents away from the simulator and makes every failed motion or grasp visible. Unless a condition removes them, agents also have code tools: a shell, file editing, and their own Python code ( Fig. 1 ). They use these tools, for example, to measure object positions in an image. A sandbox keeps agents away from the evaluator, hidden task data, and our code.
