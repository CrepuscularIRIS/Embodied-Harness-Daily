# Know When to Escalate: Critic-Guided Adaptive Execution for Cloud-Local Embodied Agents

## Abstract
Recent advances in foundation-model agents have enabled increasingly capable embodied systems for long-horizon planning and interaction. However, embodied agents require fast, closed-loop decisions as they continuously perceive and act on a changing environment. Deploying strong cloud agents leaves the platform idle for seconds between consecutive actions, while lightweight local models respond quickly but are more vulnerable to errors. Existing hierarchical architectures typically assign models fixed roles, whereas conventional model routers make stateless per-request decisions. We present CARE, a critic-guided adaptive execution framework that dynamically determines when to escalate control from a local executor to a stronger cloud model. A lightweight critic model monitors execution reliability and triggers escalation when local execution becomes unreliable; once escalated, control persists until the agent recovers, avoiding step-wise switching. To support such control transfer, a shared handoff memory maintains execution context and task-level progress across executors, enabling the model to intervene without restarting from scratch. Across diverse embodied benchmarks, CARE improves task success by 6.3-18.3 percentage points over the strongest evaluated collaboration baseline. CARE further approaches or exceeds Cloud-only success while reducing median time-between-action latency by 28.8-82.6%.

## I Introduction
Executing long-horizon robotic tasks requires embodied agents to repeatedly interpret observations, select grounded actions, and update their decisions based on action outcomes. Recent advances in generative foundation models have strengthened these capabilities [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] , enabling such models to coordinate perception, decision making, and embodied skills as general-purpose reasoning modules [ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] . Because model inference lies directly within the perception–action loop, high inference latency can leave the agent acting on stale observations, potentially causing failure in time-sensitive interactions. To balance strong reasoning with responsive closed-loop interaction, recent hierarchical and slow–fast architectures pair powerful foundation models with lightweight local executors or specialized controllers [ 9 , 10 ] . Despite these advances, long-horizon embodied execution remains challenging because each action affects what the agent observes and must do next. Consider an agent midway through a rearrangement task. An earlier action has displaced the target object, causing the next grasp to fail even though the resulting observation appears similar to the previous one. If the agent overlooks this feedback, it may repeat the same action, lose track of task progress, and compound earlier errors [ 9 , 11 ] . Execution difficulty also changes along the trajectory. A lightweight executor may handle routine steps well but struggle after ambiguous observations, failed actions, or deviations from the expected state. The appropriate executor may therefore change as interaction unfolds. This challenge is particularly pronounced in cloud–local embodied systems, where execution must continuously balance model capability against interaction latency. As shown in Figure 1 , local models provide responsive interaction but may struggle in difficult states, whereas cloud models offer stronger reasoning at the cost of seconds per decision [ 2 , 10 , 1 ] . Existing hierarchical systems typically assign these models fixed planner–executor roles, implicitly assuming that the designated executor remains appropriate throughout the task. Replanning methods can revise plans or actions in response to feedback, but generally do so without reconsidering which model should execute the subsequent trajectory [ 7 , 12 , 13 ] . Per-query model routing likewise selects an executor independently for each request, without explicitly modeling how executor suitability evolves within an embodied trajectory [ 14 ] . We present CARE , a critic-guided adaptive execution framework for cloud–local embodied agents. Rather than routing individual actions independently, CARE uses a critic model to assess whether local execution should continue under the current interaction state and selectively escalates execution to the cloud when local execution becomes unreliable. Importantly, escalation is treated as a stateful control transfer rather than a one-step model selection: cloud intervention persists through the current recovery, avoiding premature hand-backs. Once the critic supports local execution again, control returns to the local executor to recover low-latency interaction. Meanwhile, a shared handoff memory preserves observations, completed progress, and failure context across model handoffs, allowing both executors to maintain a consistent execution state. By combining critic-guided control transfer with stateful handoff memory, CARE avoids both persistent cloud invocation and myopic per-action routing while adapting execution authority to the evolving difficulty of the task. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce a new formulation of cloud–local embodied collaboration as a stateful adaptive execution problem and propose a novel critic-guided execution framework that dynamically determines when execution should be escalated and when local control can be safely resumed. • We develop CARE, a novel stateful execution mechanism that combines persistent cloud intervention with shared execution memory, enabling reliable control transfer while preserving execution continuity across model handoffs. • We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of CARE across three long-horizon embodied benchmarks spanning diverse observation modalities and execution interfaces. CARE improves task success by 6.3 – 18.3 percentage points over the strongest evaluated collaboration baseline while reducing median time-between-action latency by 28.8 – 82.6\% .

## III Method
Figure 2 illustrates CARE along a single execution trajectory. The agent begins under local control. When a pick action repeatedly fails and progress stalls, CARE changes which executor proposes the next action while leaving the perception–action loop unchanged. A critic assesses whether local execution should continue based on the current observation, local proposal, and execution history. Control transfers to the cloud when needed and remains there until the critic supports returning to local execution. Throughout these switches, shared handoff memory preserves task progress, allowing the incoming executor to continue from the current state rather than restart. We first formalize stateful cloud–local execution (§ III-A ), then describe the critic-guided transfer mechanism (§ III-B ) and shared handoff memory (§ III-C ). III-A Problem Formulation We consider cloud–local collaboration at the embodied decision layer, where models select grounded actions while low-level motor control is handled by the underlying platform. The agent separates one-time task planning from closed-loop execution. Given a task instruction G and initial observation o_{0} , a planner \pi_{P} first produces a high-level plan P=\pi_{P}(G,o_{0}) , which provides task-level guidance throughout the episode. The resulting plan is carried out by two executors: a lightweight local model \pi_{L} and a high-capability cloud model \pi_{C} . Both operate at the same level of abstraction and output navigation or manipulation actions, but exhibit a capability–latency asymmetry: the cloud executor generally provides stronger reasoning at substantially higher inference latency. At interaction step t , both executors have access to a shared execution context: x_{t}=\bigl(G,P,o_{t},M_{t}\bigr), (1) where G denotes the task instruction, o_{t} the current observation, and M_{t} the shared execution memory accumulated along the trajectory. Let z_{t}\in\{L,C\} denote the executor in control at step t . The executed action is then denoted as a_{t}=\pi_{z_{t}}(x_{t}) . Executing a_{t} yields the next observation o_{t+1} together with an execution outcome e_{t} , which captures environment feedback from the interaction. The control state z_{t} evolves over time according to the critic-guided transfer mechanism introduced in § III-B . Our objective is to approach Cloud-only task success while reducing closed-loop response time, particularly the delay between consecutive embodied actions. Because each executed action changes the subsequent observation and task state, executor selection is coupled across the trajectory rather than made independently for each request.
