===ABSTRACT===
Can AI agents design robots that work in the real world? We introduce RoboBuilder, a benchmark in which coding agents co-design a robot's hardware and program from standard parts, motors and a custom 3D-printed part, and every design that passes in simulation is built and tested on a physical course. Simulation alone is not enough: under public part data, none of 15 initial passing designs crossed the real course, so an agent learns the simulator from measurements it requests and videos of real runs. Across four frontier coding agents, ten obstacles at three difficulty levels, 16,427 simulations of 12,774 distinct robots and 135 physical robots built in an estimated 240 hours, agents solve nearly all easy obstacles and invent mechanisms our search baselines do not reach, but falter at harder levels, when one robot must cross every obstacle, and in the real world, where 66% of simulated passes hold across all levels. Closing the loop helps: shown a video of its real failure, an agent redesigns the robot, sometimes correcting its simulator, and removes 72% of level-1 and level-2 failures. RoboBuilder measures progress toward agents that engineer real machines. Project Page: https://ghadinehme.com/robobuilder.github.io/

===INTRO===
Engineering design is a loop: build a prototype, test it, learn why it failed, and iterate. Coding agents have mastered this loop for software, where a test runs in seconds and its verdict is exact. For a machine, the test is a physical build, and the simulator that stands in for it is wrong in ways only real runs reveal. We ask whether agents can run the full loop for functional robots: designing assemblies that achieve a task under constraints and objectives, and closing the sim-to-real gap the way engineers do, by building and testing. Agents became capable engineers through executable feedback: writing code, running the tests and reading the failure [ 32 , 90 , 75 ] . The same recipe now produces CAD models [ 50 , 33 , 54 , 55 ] , robot code [ 40 ] and machines in physics sandboxes [ 93 , 88 ] . These works judge geometry, or judge function in a simulator whose verdict is taken as truth. A functional assembly is held to more: it must achieve its task within the parts on hand, with joints that hold, gears that mesh and motors that can drive it, often as light or fast as possible, and only the physical world can confirm it. Simulation alone does not suffice. With a simulator built from public data for our parts, none of 15 initial robots that passed in simulation crossed the real course: tyres slipped, joints pulled apart, gears walked out of mesh and loads shifted. Sim-to-real methods calibrate a model of a fixed robot from real data [ 80 , 78 , 9 ] ; in co-design the robot changes with every iteration, so the simulator must hold for designs nobody has built yet. A simulator never checked against reality rewards agents for exploiting it. We introduce RoboBuilder, a benchmark for agentic robot co-design under constraints and objectives whose verdict is grounded in the real world ( ). Agents design a robot and its open-loop program from an inventory of real parts, motors and a custom 3D-printed part (up to four copies), under building rules, to cross ten obstacles at three levels. They must pass each obstacle, pass with a printed part if needed, then make the design lighter or faster, and build one robot that crosses every obstacle ( Figure 3 ). The design space is vast. With 132 part types and about 3,000 parts in stock, a rough count gives about 10^{68} assemblies of only 25 pieces, while passing robots use a median of 114; each motor program adds about 10^{16} timings, and the printed part can be any shape an agent can write as CAD code ( Sec. H.4 ). Agents also close the sim-to-real gap themselves. An agent (Opus 5.5) first learns the simulator, requesting measurements and adjusting the physics until simulation matches training and validation videos of real runs. Every design that passes a task is then built and tested, and each real failure returns to its agent as video, which redesigns the robot, correcting its simulator where the video shows a modelling error, before the redesign is built again. Four frontier coding agents pass 98% of level-1 tasks where search over the full inventory passes none, and invent whegs, printed hoops, cable cars and walkers that our search baselines, with the same budget but without prior knowledge, do not find ( Figure 2 ; more in Appendix A ). Their success rate drops sharply at harder levels and when one robot must cross every obstacle. Iterating in simulation is essential, since a single call without it passes almost nothing, but more exploration does not predict better designs: the strongest agent passes with its first idea in over half its runs, suggesting that the agent’s built-in engineering knowledge is its main edge. In the real world, 73% of level-1 and level-2 simulated passes cross with the learned simulator, and 93% after agents redesign from their failures. Contributions. • RoboBuilder-Bench . Functional robot co-design under constraints and objectives: ten obstacles at three levels, four tasks plus integration, and a real-part inventory. • RoboBuilder-Sim and RoboBuilder-Loop . Simulator learning from requested measurements and real videos, and a protocol that turns each real failure into simulator and design adjustments that are built and tested again. At levels 1 and 2, 73% of simulated passes cross for real, and 93% after the loop. • Analysis of Agentic Design. Performance and optimization, iteration in simulation, the distribution, novelty and diversity of designs (two to three times as diverse as search), how agents explore ideas, and how easily the designed robots can be assembled by hand. • RoboBuilder-Lab and Dataset. 135 robots built by hand in an estimated 240 hours, 16,427 simulations of 12,774 robots, and 461 paired real videos.
