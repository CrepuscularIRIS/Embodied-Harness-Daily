# ACE-CaP-0: Real-World Code-as-Policies in the Era of Experience

## Abstract
Recent advances in frontier models have pushed the boundaries of agentic robot control, enabling agents to perform complex robotic tasks through reasoning and interaction. However, repeated calls to these models during execution can substantially increase task completion time. Code as Policies (CaP) offers a route for coding agents to acquire physical skills through experience and retain them in executable programs, allowing the reasoning invested in development to be reused across runs. Developing reliable code policies while limiting the cost of physical experimentation remains a central challenge. We investigate human guidance as a source of priors for feasible actions and simulation as an auxiliary environment for exploring and testing candidate policies. Our findings favor robot demonstrations available on request and simulation used selectively during real-world policy refinement. These findings inform ACE-CaP-0, an agentic recipe that couples policy revision with experience acquisition through Demonstrations on Request and Simulation on Demand. Across eight real-world manipulation tasks spanning alignment, multi-object handling, and bimanual coordination, the resulting frozen programs achieve 92.1\% success (221/240 trials). Mean execution times across tasks range from 0.97 to 2.27 minutes. The programs execute with runtime perception and control, without a human or coding agent in the loop. These results highlight CaP's potential to turn experience into reliable, reusable physical skills.

## 1 Introduction
Foundation models are changing how robots acquire and execute skills. Learned action models, including vision-language-action (VLA) and world action models (WAMs), combine pretrained knowledge with large-scale data and interaction experience to support generalist robot control [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . Advances in frontier models ( e.g. , GPT-6 Astra [ 4 ] and Claude Fable 5.2 [ 5 ] ) are expanding the role of agents in both robot control and policy development. In agentic robot control, agents interpret observations, choose actions, and revise their decisions in response to physical feedback [ 6 ] . When execution relies on repeated model calls, however, inference latency can account for a substantial part of task completion time [ 7 , 8 ] . Agents can also apply these capabilities during policy development, organizing experiments, diagnosing failures, and revising the policies a robot executes [ 9 , 10 ] . Code as Policies (CaP) represents robot behavior as executable programs, allowing decisions made during development to be retained and reused across runs [ 11 , 12 ] . Through demonstrations and experimentation, a coding agent can discover manipulation strategies and encode their action structure, geometric relationships, and motion parameters in code [ 13 ] . The resulting program can execute with runtime perception and control, without requiring the coding agent to select each action. Its explicit task logic can be inspected and revised, supporting the accumulation of executable skills in embodied Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) [ 14 , 10 ] . Developing reliable code policies requires physical knowledge that a task instruction often leaves unspecified. The agent must resolve choices about grasping, alignment, and coordination, yet a failed attempt may not reveal how to revise the program [ 13 , 10 ] . Repeated physical trials also consume robot time and can require human scene resets [ 9 , 15 ] . Policy development therefore couples two decisions: how to revise the program, and what experience to acquire next. We ask: how should agents acquire experience to develop reliable code policies when physical experimentation is costly? Human guidance can supply feasible strategies and motion references, while simulation provides an auxiliary environment for exploring and testing candidate implementations [ 13 , 15 ] . We examine their use through six development conditions on charger insertion, with one development run per condition (Section 2 ). • What form of human guidance helps an agent develop a physically realizable policy, and when should it be provided? Our results favor robot demonstrations paired with textual guidance and available on request during development. Robot demonstrations supply motion references in the target embodiment, while on-request access lets the agent seek guidance for its current manipulation problem. • When and how should simulated experience support real-world program development? Our results favor using simulation on demand to investigate current development questions within a real-world feedback loop. Simulated experiments examine candidate revisions, while physical execution checks whether the sensing and action assumptions they rely on hold on the robot. Building on these findings, we introduce ACE-CaP-0 , a recipe that couples policy revision with experience acquisition through Demonstrations on Request and Simulation on Demand (Section 3 ). During development, the coding agent chooses whether to request a robot demonstration, examine a candidate revision in simulation, or test the program on the robot. The resulting program is frozen for evaluation and executes with runtime perception and control, without a human or coding agent in the loop. We evaluate ACE-CaP-0 on eight real-world manipulation tasks requiring alignment, object reorientation, multi-object handling, and bimanual coordination (Section 4 ). Development allows at most ten human-assisted scene resets per task, excluding resets for demonstration collection. Each frozen program is then evaluated in 30 trials without further updates. As summarized in Figure 1 , the programs achieve 92.1% overall success (221/240 trials). Mean physical execution times range from 0.97 to 2.27 minutes across tasks. These results support CaP as a practical route from targeted experience to reliable, reusable physical skills.

## 2 Investigation
We study charger insertion to examine how human guidance and simulation support code-policy development under limited physical interaction. Six experimental conditions address three questions: what form of guidance to provide, when to provide it, and how to incorporate simulation into exploration. Here, exploration involves executing candidate programs, inspecting feedback, and revising code. All three comparisons share a real-only reference with on-request textual guidance and robot demonstrations. Shared setting and development budget. All conditions use the same foundation model, task instruction, and real-world perception and control interfaces. We conduct one independent development run per condition, allowing at most ten human-assisted scene resets for the agent’s physical trials. Resets for demonstration collection are excluded; autonomous executions and demonstrations do not count against the budget. Evaluation and cost accounting. After development, we freeze each selected program and evaluate it in 30 real-world trials under common success criteria and initial-state conditions, without further updates [ 16 ] . Final success rate is the fraction of these trials in which the program completes charger insertion. Development time is the elapsed time in hours, including model calls, simulated experiments, and real-world interaction. Output token usage, reported in millions (M), sums the tokens generated across all model calls during development, including guidance interpretation, simulation construction, execution analysis, and code revision. Both costs exclude frozen-policy evaluation. 2.1 How Do Human Priors Shape Real-World CaP? Human guidance supplies prior knowledge about manipulation strategies and their implementation [ 13 , 17 ] . For charger insertion, the task goal leaves choices about grasping, plug orientation, and approach motion unresolved. Demonstrations can supply a workable reference when execution feedback alone does not indicate how to revise the program [ 13 , 18 ] . In addition to textual guidance, the agent can request videos of a person performing the task with their hands, recorded from the robot’s execution viewpoint. • Robot Demonstrations. In addition to textual guidance, the agent can request kinesthetic demonstrations in which a person physically guides the robot arms through the task.
