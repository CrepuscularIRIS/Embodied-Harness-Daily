===ABSTRACT===
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) are capable low-level controllers, but they tie where objects go to the layouts they were trained on: when objects are moved or obstacles block the way, they carry them to the wrong place or into collisions. Fine-tuning on new layouts needs robot data for each one and erodes skills the model already has. We introduce Cairn, which keeps the VLA frozen and steers it at test time along learned 3D trajectories of the object in its gripper. A small prior reads point clouds of the scene and the held object, along with its pose when grasped and the instruction, and samples SE(3) trajectories up to its release that lead it around obstacles and turn it to fit through gaps. The prior and the VLA form the two levels of a hierarchical planner in which feedback runs both ways: the VLA's intended motion chooses and reshapes the trajectories, and the chosen one guides the VLA's denoising. Its predicted actions, mapped to the held object's poses through the grasp, are pulled toward the next waypoint, while the VLA's weights stay untouched. Across 1,941 held-out LIBERO scenes with displaced objects and obstacles, Cairn raises the success rate of a frozen π0.5 from 24.5% to 95.1%, above that of a π0.5 fine-tuned on our training layouts, without hurting standard LIBERO. On the third-party LIBERO-PRO benchmark, it raises success from 27.3% to 38.0%, and from 54.4% to 81.1% on the episodes it steers. On a real Franka, with a prior trained purely in simulation, it guides the frozen DROID π0.5 around an obstacle and to a placement at the limit of the arm's reach, where it otherwise fails. All code, models and data will be made public.

===INTRO===
Moving into a new home calls on two abilities: lifting and setting down a bookshelf are skills one brings along, while where it should stand and how to get it there, around the sofa and tilted through the doorway, depend on the new home. Vision-language-action models (VLAs) [ 6 , 39 , 4 , 3 , 59 ] have the first: trained by imitation on large robot datasets, they grasp and place many objects from images and an instruction. They largely lack the second. Deciding one short action chunk at a time, mostly from 2D images, a VLA absorbs where objects belong and how to reach them from its training layouts; moving objects collapses its success [ 93 , 20 , 73 , 19 ] , and obstacles cause collisions [ 67 , 51 ] . An obstacle calls for a detour chosen before it is reached, and a long object may need to turn to fit through a gap. Both depend on the scene’s 3D geometry and the object’s shape. The motor skill is in place; the route is missing. Fine-tuning a VLA can teach it those layouts, but it needs robot demonstrations of every new scene and can make it forget skills it already has [ 95 ] . Guidance instead changes what a generative model produces without changing the model: each denoising step is nudged along the gradient of an objective. It has spread from image generation [ 15 ] to inverse problems [ 13 , 66 ] , trajectory planning [ 35 ] and robot policies [ 75 , 17 , 51 ] . Since current VLAs generate actions with flow-matching experts [ 4 , 3 , 59 ] , the same recipe applies to them with no robot data or training: OmniGuide [ 67 ] steers frozen \pi_{0.5} and GR00T by back-propagating 3D energies through the robot’s kinematics. Its targets are local: a collision field does not know the goal, an attractor pulls straight through obstacles and can get stuck in local minima, and both act on the gripper, not the object it holds. The open question is not how to steer a frozen VLA, but where to. A route that helps a frozen VLA must meet several requirements, and existing planners each meet only some of them. It must be metric and three-dimensional, unlike image paths, which lack depth [ 25 , 45 ] . It must describe the held object and how it turns, not only the gripper positions of 3D path predictors [ 34 , 33 ] . Models of 6-DoF object motion [ 84 , 87 , 64 ] plan the object, but learn from human data, without its shape or the robot’s reach. It must cover the whole carry and find the goal from the instruction, whereas motion planners [ 40 , 70 ] must be given the goal. It must reach the VLA without retraining it, whereas hierarchical VLAs [ 45 , 34 , 7 ] train their low-level policy to follow plans. Above all, the route and the VLA must agree. Together they form a hierarchy, a planner of the route above a planner of actions, and in hierarchies the high level usually commands while the low level is trained to obey. A frozen VLA has its own preferred motion, and a route chosen without it has to be forced on it. A high level that offers a distribution of routes can instead couple with the VLA at test time, with feedback in both directions: the VLA’s intended motion chooses and reshapes the route, and the route steers the VLA. We present Cairn, a framework that steers frozen VLAs with 3D trajectories of the held object that account for the scene, its obstacles and the object’s own shape. The name comes from the stacked stones that mark a hiking trail: a cairn shows the way but carries no one along it. In the same way, Cairn marks the route of the held object and leaves the moving to the VLA. To meet the requirements above, it builds a two-level planner from a small 3D specialist, which plans the route, and the frozen 2D generalist, which plans the actions ( Fig. 2 ). The specialist is a motion prior built like 3D point-cloud policies [ 23 , 24 ] , but it predicts where the held object travels rather than how the robot moves. From point clouds of the scene and the held object, along with its pose at the grasp and the instruction, it samples with rectified flow [ 52 ] a route to the release as \mathrm{SE}(3) waypoints. It is trained without robot actions, on simulated demonstrations generated with moved objects, obstacles and several routes per scene. At test time the prior and the VLA steer each other. The VLA’s intended motion weights and refines the sampled routes that clear the scene. In turn, at every denoising step, the object motion that the VLA’s action chunk would produce is pulled toward the next waypoint of the chosen route. The VLA’s weights are never changed. We evaluate Cairn in simulation and on a real robot. On LIBERO-Shift, 1,941 held-out LIBERO scenes with moved objects and obstacles, it raises the success of the frozen \pi_{0.5} from 24.5% to 95.1%, more than fine-tuning \pi_{0.5} on the same layouts, which also degrades \pi_{0.5} on standard LIBERO, where Cairn leaves performance unchanged.
