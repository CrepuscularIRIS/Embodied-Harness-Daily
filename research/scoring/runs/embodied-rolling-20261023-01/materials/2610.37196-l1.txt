# How Much Can Best-of-N Help a World Action Model? Measuring Selectable Headroom and Realizing It in Closed Loop

## Abstract
World action models (WAMs) predict the visual consequences of the actions they generate, which makes test-time selection a natural way to improve a fixed policy: imagine the futures of candidate actions and execute the best one. The benefit of selection is bounded by the selectable headroom, how much better the best candidate truly is, but the standard estimate, which executes each candidate once and keeps the best outcome, conflates candidate quality with the randomness of everything that follows. We introduce a denoised counterfactual protocol, based on bitwise-reproducible branching, repeated continuations, held-out oracles, and common random numbers, that measures the headroom a selector can realize, and use its measurements to build a fail-gated selector. Across four WAMs, two benchmarks, over 100,000 branched rollouts, and pre-registered tests, we find that (i) the standard estimate overstates the selectable headroom of native samples: bounds from 17 continuations per candidate place its selectable share between 24% and 66% for three WAMs; (ii) structured action perturbations create up to 16 points of selectable headroom; and (iii) in both WAMs with action-conditioned imagination, imagined futures carry candidate-outcome information with no detectable loss relative to real futures, information that the value head of Cosmos Policy leaves unused. Deviating from the policy chunk only when a readout of the WAM predicts failure, the selector raises closed-loop task success from 76.9% to 81.6% on held-out episodes of training tasks, with the largest gain where the policy fails most often, and from 62.6% to 69.8% on unseen tasks.

## 1 Introduction
World action models, which extend video-prediction policies and joint video-action models [ 11 , 50 , 21 , 29 , 58 ] , jointly produce an action chunk, a prediction of the scene after the chunk is executed, and an estimate of its value [ 25 ] . This structure makes a simple form of test-time compute available to any WAM: sample several candidate chunks, imagine where each of them leads, and execute the chunk whose imagined future looks best. Sampling-and-verification schemes of this kind, established for reasoning with language models [ 9 , 33 , 45 ] , have improved vision-language-action (VLA [ 24 , 5 ] ) policies [ 27 , 36 , 23 , 28 ] , and verifiers specific to WAMs have been proposed [ 56 , 55 ] . How much can such selection gain? The answer is fixed before any scorer is chosen: the gain is bounded by how much better the best candidate in the pool truly is. We call this quantity the selectable headroom . Yuan et al. [53] measure it directly with counterfactual branching. They restore the same simulator state, execute each of 16 sampled candidates, continue each candidate with the policy until the episode ends, and find that choosing the candidate with the best outcome would raise success from 68.9% to 79.2%, whereas learned values, image similarity, and VLM judgments recover little of this gain. This estimate executes each candidate once . Everything that happens after the candidate, including every later policy query, is random, so the candidate with the best realized outcome is partly the candidate whose continuation happened to go well. Selecting and scoring on the same random draw is the optimizer’s curse [ 44 ] , the same bias that double estimators remove in reinforcement learning [ 48 ] . The question that matters for selection is therefore how much of the oracle gain a selector could ever realize. We answer this question with a denoised counterfactual protocol ( Fig. 2 a). Every candidate is executed from a bitwise-reproducible simulator snapshot and continued several times. The realizable part of the headroom is measured by a held-out oracle that selects on some continuations and is scored on others; a variance decomposition separates the effect of the candidate from its interaction with the continuation; and every evaluator is run with common random numbers, so that all candidates are compared under the same evaluation randomness. Using more than 100,000 branched rollouts of four WAMs, namely Cosmos Policy [ 25 ] , Flex- \pi [ 51 ] , Fast-WAM [ 54 ] , and Streaming-WAM [ 22 ] , on RoboCasa [ 37 ] and LIBERO-Plus [ 14 ] , we establish the following results, each supported by pre-registered tests. • The single-continuation oracle is dominated by continuation randomness ( Sec. 5.1 ). Under the protocol of Yuan et al. [53] , we reproduce a 12.3-point oracle gain, yet a selector that observes three independent outcomes per candidate realizes only 9% of it on a fresh continuation, and 98% of the candidate-specific outcome variation is re-drawn when the continuation seeds change. The same holds for all four WAMs on LIBERO-Plus. With 17 continuations per candidate, the selectable share lies between 24% and 52% of the single-continuation oracle for Cosmos Policy and between a third and two thirds for Flex- \pi and Fast-WAM ( Fig. 1 ). • Structured proposals create selectable headroom ( Sec. 5.2 ). Native samples are nearly identical and offer almost no selectable headroom over the policy chunk, whereas displacing the policy chunk along the robot axes creates headroom of which 67–95% survives held-out evaluation for all four WAMs. This headroom lies where the policy chunk fails, and it exceeds the chunk by 4.5–16 points on both benchmarks and for all four WAMs. • The imagination carries the signal that the value head misses ( Sec. 5.3 ). On both WAMs with action-conditioned imagination, a linear readout of the imagined future ranks candidates with no detectable loss relative to the same readout of the real future; for Cosmos Policy, the signal resides in the predicted consequence rather than in the encoding of the action. The value head, although accurate at the state level (AUROC 0.90), recovers almost none of this signal. • A selector built on the findings improves on the policy ( Sec. 5.4 ). A learned failure gate decides whether to deviate from the policy chunk, and a learned ranker decides where to go, so the selector deviates only where the policy is likely to fail, which is where the headroom lies, and executes the policy chunk unchanged everywhere else. Frozen and pre-registered, it raises closed-loop task success from 76.9% to 81.6% on reserved episodes of the training tasks, by 10.7 points where the policy fails most often, and, when re-trained without the test tasks, from 62.6% to 69.8% on unseen tasks. Selectable headroom. At a decision point s_{t_{b}} , the policy outputs an action chunk and executes its first part (16 steps for Cosmos Policy) before it is queried again. For a candidate c , let p_{c} denote the probability of task success when the executed part of c is applied from s_{t_{b}} and the policy acts thereafter; the randomness is that of all later policy queries. The selectable headroom over a uniform choice among K candidates is O^{\star}=\max_{c}p_{c}-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{c}p_{c}. (1) When the pool contains the policy chunk c=0 , we also report \max_{c}p_{c}-p_{0} , the gain over executing that chunk. Bitwise-reproducible branching. We snapshot and restore the full MuJoCo [ 47 ] state, the global random-number state, and the fixture states that RoboCasa keeps outside the joint positions ( e.g . , stove flames). Branching from a snapshot reproduces seeded replay from reset byte for byte (actions, proprioception, camera frames, and success flags), and every rollout passes online and offline rendering checks ( Appendix B ).
