# Streaming Failure Forecasting: VLA Failure Monitors That Keep Learning After Deployment

## Abstract
Failure monitors for vision-language-action (VLA) policies are trained offline and then frozen. After deployment, lighting, objects and cameras drift, and the policy itself is updated or moved to new tasks. On recorded real-robot runs, after a switch of task or policy version, a frozen monitor fit on the old task ranks failures little better than chance. We present Streaming Failure Forecasting (SFF), a monitor that keeps learning from the one label every episode provides: whether it succeeded. SFF keeps the offline monitor's network fixed and refits only its last linear layer after each episode, at 2.18 ms per policy call on a Jetson CPU core. Its alarm threshold moves only after successes, which in simulation kept false alarms near target except when two drifts came at once. With every setting locked before testing, SFF ranks failures better than the same monitor kept frozen on 4 of 5 simulated test suites under unannounced drift, and above resetting on detected drift on every suite. Carried with its locked learner settings to 434 recorded runs of four VLAs on real SO-101 arms, replayed across unannounced switches of task or policy version, it ranks failures better than the frozen monitor for every VLA and, in an exploratory comparison, above a full reset at the true switch. A deployed failure monitor need not stay frozen: refitting its readout from outcomes is cheap and helps after the changes we tested.

## 1 Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) policies carry out manipulation tasks from a language instruction [ 2 , 3 , 4 ] , but they still fail often, so a deployed robot needs a failure monitor : a model that watches each episode through onboard signals and raises an alarm before a failing episode ends. SAFE [ 5 ] learns such a score from the policy’s internal features; other monitors measure uncertainty, action consistency or feature distances [ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 ] . All of them are trained and calibrated offline, then frozen. A deployed robot does not stand still. The light changes, objects are swapped, a camera gets bumped and gripper pads wear, and the policy itself is updated or moved to a new task as the fleet improves. Nobody tells the monitor. Each change can break the monitor’s ranking , so failing episodes no longer score above successful ones: on recorded real-robot runs of four VLAs, a frozen monitor’s within-task AUROC falls from first/frozen on the task it was fit on to pool/auroc/frozen after a switch, close to chance (Fig. Streaming Failure Forecasting: VLA Failure Monitors That Keep Learning After Deployment ). Recalibrating the alarm threshold cannot repair ranking, because the order of the scores does not depend on the threshold. Yet the deployed system learns how every episode ended. We ask whether this one label is enough to keep a monitor useful, if it updates the right part. SFF keeps the offline monitor’s network fixed and refits only its last, linear layer after each episode, in closed form, pulled toward the offline layer so that it starts out ranking exactly as the offline monitor does (Fig. 2 ). Its alarm threshold moves only after successful episodes, the only ones on which an alarm is false, which bounds the long-run false-alarm rate for any bounded score, even one that is still learning. An update is one 130\times 130 solve, no episode is stored, and the monitor is never told when or how conditions changed. We designed SFF on one LIBERO suite, chose each suite’s features and two settings on its own development streams, locked them, and tested each suite once; we then carried the locked learner settings to total/runs recorded real-robot runs of four VLAs. We find: • Refitting the readout improves ranking under drift. SFF ranks failures better than the frozen monitor on 4 of 5 simulated test suites, including the primary test specified in advance, and above a monitor that resets on detected drift on every suite (Sec. 6.1 ). • The locked settings carry to recorded real-robot runs. After unannounced switches of task or policy version, SFF ranks failures better than the frozen monitor for every VLA, and above a full reset at the true switch (exploratory; Sec. 6.2 ). • In our tests, the readout was the part worth adapting. Our whole-network streaming learner barely moved its hidden weights, and resetting SFF’s head, even at the true change, ranked lower on every suite (exploratory; Sec. 6.3 ). • It is cheap, and calibrated in simulation. 2.18 ms per call on a Jetson CPU core, a 1.0 MB state that never grows, and a false-alarm rate near target except under compound drift (Sec. 6.4 ). Every monitor we ran, and where SFF does not help, is reported in Secs. 6.3 and 7 and App. G .

## 3 Problem: monitoring a deployed policy as a stream
What a monitor sees and when. A frozen policy runs one episode after another while conditions change without notice. At every policy call c , once per action chunk, a monitor reads the policy’s internal features and the elapsed fraction t_{c}/T of the task’s step limit T , and returns a risk score s_{c} . It alarms once the episode’s running maximum of s_{c} exceeds a threshold \tau , fixed for that episode. After the episode it receives the outcome y_{e} (1 for failure), assumed correct. It is never told when or how conditions changed, keeps a state of fixed size, and every score is logged before any update that uses it. Two goals: ranking and calibration. Ranking is the chance that a failing episode reaches a higher peak score than a successful episode of the same task, over all such pairs under one condition, with ties counting half. We call it within-task AUROC ; 0.5 is chance. Peaks are taken only up to the task’s shortest recorded episode, as in SAFE [ 5 ] : in simulation every failure runs to the step limit, so this is the shortest success; on real runs a failure can end early. Without this cut, elapsed time alone would flag 1.000 of LIBERO-Spatial’s failures at a false-alarm rate of 0.101; with it, its within-task AUROC is 0.500. Comparing within a task stops a score from gaining by recognizing harder tasks. Calibration is the false-alarm rate on successful episodes (FAR), against a target \alpha chosen by the operator; we use \alpha=0.1 . A threshold rule can change FAR but not ranking, so the two goals need two mechanisms.

## 4 Streaming Failure Forecasting
SFF has three parts: fixed features from an offline monitor, an online readout that learns ranking, and a success-only threshold that keeps calibration. Fig. 2 shows what happens at every policy call and after each episode; Algorithm 1 in App. C lists it step by step. Fixed features. Before deployment, a small network with two hidden layers is trained on nominal warm-up episodes to predict, at every call, whether the episode will fail; a separate set of warm-up episodes sets the first threshold. Its input x_{c} is built from the VLA’s internal activations, projected to 128 dimensions and normalized by running statistics, plus t_{c}/T . Its weights then stay fixed, and its hidden layers \phi give z_{c}=[1,\phi(x_{c}),t_{c}/T]\in\mathbb{R}^{130} . Its output layer, with a weight of 0 for the appended t_{c}/T , is the offline head \bar{\theta} . The frozen monitor scores \bar{\theta}^{\top}z_{c} throughout. Online readout. SFF scores the calls of episode e with s_{c}=\theta_{e-1}^{\top}z_{c} . After each episode, \theta_{e} fits each call of the episodes so far to its episode’s outcome by discounted least squares, pulled toward the offline head: \textstyle\theta_{e}=\arg\min\limits_{\theta}\sum\limits_{k\leq e}\frac{\rho^{\,e-k}}{n_{k}}\sum\limits_{c\in k}\bigl(y_{k}-\theta^{\top}z_{c}\bigr)^{2}+a\lVert\theta-\bar{\theta}\rVert^{2}+\delta\lVert\theta\rVert^{2}=\bigl(A_{e}+(a+\delta)I\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(b_{e}+a\,\bar{\theta}\bigr), (1) where episode k has n_{k} calls, A_{e}=\rho A_{e-1}+\sum_{c}z_{c}z_{c}^{\top}/n_{e} and b_{e}=\rho b_{e-1}+y_{e}\sum_{c}z_{c}/n_{e} . Each choice has one reason. The weight 1/n_{k} gives every episode the same total weight, since failing episodes run longer. The anchor a is a Gaussian prior centred on the offline head: while outcomes are few, SFF ranks as the offline monitor does, and \theta_{0}\approx\bar{\theta} . The discount \rho\leq 1 lets old conditions fade; tuning chose the slower of two values on every suite. The ridge \delta is small. The extra coordinate t_{c}/T lets the head learn a direct time weight that the offline head lacks. An update is one 130\times 130 solve, no episode is stored, and the state never grows. We also report a warm-up start , which fits A and b on the warm-up episodes with a=0 . Why only the readout. Our first learner, SFF-TD, trained the whole network online by streaming TD( \lambda ) with an overshooting-bounded optimizer [ 45 ] . Over a development stream its hidden-layer weights moved by only 0.2–2.
