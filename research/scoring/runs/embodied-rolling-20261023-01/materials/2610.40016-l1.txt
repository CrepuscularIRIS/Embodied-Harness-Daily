# LOGIV: Graph-Verified Execution with Causal Local Repair for Long-Horizon Robot Tasks

## Abstract
Agentic robots must turn a high-level goal into actions while keeping the physical conditions needed for future steps valid. A completed action does not ensure that its effects will persist: a later disturbance can invalidate support that the remaining plan still assumes. We present LOGIV, a graph engineering framework for agentic robot execution that uses an explicit task graph to connect symbolic planning, observation-based verification, and embodied action. LOGIV compiles a validated plan into condition-support and resource-ordering edges, checks their physical validity during execution, and uses dependency tracing to determine what must be restored after a failure. Repair is constrained by retained unfinished actions, and the combined continuation is symbolically validated before execution resumes. The framework operates above existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) and World Action Model (WAM) policies without adding trainable components. Across 20 dual-arm tasks from RoboTwin~V2 and BiCoord with three execution backbones, LOGIV improves mean task success without injected disturbances by 10.67 and 15.33 percentage points, respectively. Under predefined perturbations, it achieves higher task completion and condition restoration than three adapted recovery baselines for every backbone on both benchmarks. On three real-robot tasks, successful trials increase from 12/30 to 17/30. These results support explicit, continuously checked task dependencies as a practical interface for reliable agentic robot execution.

## I Introduction
Agentic robots combine goal-directed planning, perception, and physical action in a feedback loop Qin et al. (2026) . Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models Octo Model Team et al. (2024) ; Kim et al. (2024) ; Physical Intelligence et al. (2025) and World Action Models (WAMs) Yuan et al. (2026) ; Cai et al. (2026) provide increasingly capable execution policies, while an agent-level controller must decide how their actions contribute to a longer task. A distinctive difficulty in physical execution is that an intermediate result can cease to hold after the action that produced it has succeeded. A support placed during bridge assembly may be displaced before the beam is installed (Fig. 1 ). The placement remains a completed action, yet the support condition needed by the remaining plan is no longer valid. Reliable long-horizon behavior therefore requires tracking both action completion and the continuing validity of its consequences. Existing work provides important components for this execution loop. LLM- and VLM-based systems select skills, interpret feedback, and organize multistep behavior Ouyang et al. (2025) ; Huang et al. (2024) ; Feng et al. (2025) ; Chen et al. (2025c) ; Ji et al. (2026) ; Peng et al. (2026a) ; Zhang et al. (2026a) ; Huang et al. (2026) . Symbolic and program-based methods make action constraints explicit Yang et al. (2025) ; Kwon et al. (2025) ; Chen et al. (2025b) ; Chu et al. (2025) , building on established work in plan monitoring and repair Pollack and McCarthy (1999) ; Van Der Krogt and De Weerdt (2005) . Graph-based agent frameworks expose subtask dependencies for reuse and repair Zhang et al. (2026b) ; in robotics, AgentChord augments task graphs with anticipatory recovery branches before execution Xu et al. (2026) . These advances raise a question: when observations invalidate an earlier condition, which support should the robot restore while preserving a usable unfinished plan? We approach this question through graph engineering : constructing, checking, and revising an explicit dependency graph as the interface between planning and robot execution. We present LOGIV ( L ong-Horizon O nline G raph-based I nference and V erification for Plan Repair). Its task graph records which conditions each action supplies to later actions, together with resource-ordering constraints. The key design is to separate a node’s completion status from the current validity of the support it provides. When that support fails, unfinished consumers can remain in the plan while repair re-establishes their prerequisites. The retained continuation then constrains the repair goal. This gives graph structure an operational role in deciding what to check, what to preserve, and what to change. LOGIV implements this interface as a closed-loop execution and repair layer above an existing VLA or WAM policy. Before execution, it validates a high-level plan and compiles its dependencies into a task graph. During execution, visual observations and robot feedback check the selected action and the support required by pending actions. After a confirmed failure, dependency tracing identifies affected actions; repair synthesizes a continuation constrained by retained operators and expands the replacement scope when validation fails. The combined plan is checked under the symbolic model before the graph is rebuilt and execution resumes. In the bridge example, the robot restores the displaced support while retaining the remaining beam operations. This design adds no trainable components and focuses on online repair from the observed state, with the validity of the repaired continuation checked explicitly. We evaluate LOGIV on 20 simulated dual-arm tasks from RoboTwin V2 and BiCoord using \pi_{0.5} , AHA-WAM, and Light-WAM. Without injected perturbations, mean success across the three backbones increases from 71.67% to 82.33% on RoboTwin V2 and from 42.33% to 57.67% on BiCoord. Under predefined perturbations, LOGIV achieves higher task completion and condition restoration than three adapted recovery baselines for every backbone on both benchmarks. On three real-robot tasks, successful trials increase from 12/30 to 17/30. Ablations support the role of graph structure: removing dependency checks reduces perturbed-task success from 60% to 48%, and replacing local repair with replanning all remaining actions increases repeated valid actions from 0.2 to 1.6. These results support graph engineering for the evaluated tasks. ## III Method
LOGIV separates high-level task management from low-level control: it constructs, checks, and repairs plans, while the execution policy carries out individual subtasks. We use bridge assembly as a running example: the robot places supports, grasps a beam, and lays it across them. III-A Problem Formulation At round t , O_{t} is the visual observation and S_{t} is the symbolic state estimated from visual interpretation, robot status, and actuator feedback. Given S_{0} and goal g , the Operator Planner generates a reference plan P=(o_{1},\ldots,o_{n}) of grounded PDDL operators with specified preconditions and effects McDermott et al. (1998) . VAL checks the plan Howey et al. (2004) , which is then compiled into a directed acyclic graph G=(V,E) . \displaystyle P \displaystyle=\operatorname{PreparePlan}(S_{0},g)=(o_{1},\ldots,o_{n}), \displaystyle G \displaystyle=(V,E)=\operatorname{BuildGraph}(S_{0},P,g). (1) The nodes in V are subtasks, start , and goal . Causal edges record which action supplies another’s required condition; resource edges encode ordering constraints from resource use or modeled interference. Here, causal means PDDL condition support, not a learned model. The reference plan orders the graph and breaks scheduling ties. The selected operator is passed to the low-level policy as a natural-language instruction with the current visual observation. After execution, LOGIV updates the state and checks the outcome and dependencies. A confirmed execution failure produces an error record e_{t} and triggers plan repair: (P^{\prime},G^{\prime})=\operatorname{TopologyGuidedRepair}(S_{t+1},e_{t},P,G). (2) The updated plan combines a repair fragment with retained unfinished operators. Validation from S_{t+1} establishes consistency with the symbolic model and supplied state, without guaranteeing perception accuracy or physical success. Completed actions’ valid effects remain in the current state; the completed prefix is not replayed. III-B Pre-Execution Planning Plan generation and validation.
