# Pretraining Knows What, Simulation Teaches How: Compositional Generalization in a Sim-Trained Robot Agent

## Abstract
A robot policy needs to understand what a task is asking for in a scene, and how to move the robot to accomplish that task. To achieve these two skills, we introduce Zero-real Agent Policy (ZAP), a recipe that teaches a small off-the-shelf vision-language model to control a robot via a simple tool harness. We use a pretrained 27B parameter model to bootstrap world understanding and reasoning, followed by supervised fine-tuning and GRPO on simple simulated tasks to teach it how to move the robot. Without any real-world robot data during training, ZAP reaches 96.7% success on real-world tasks, matching GPT-6 Astra in our harness and outperforming the MolmoAct2 VLA (33.3%), while running 3x faster and 17x cheaper than GPT-6 Astra. We demonstrate that ZAP also generalizes to completely new objects, goals, strategies, and language by seating a test tube in a rack (and choosing a different hole in the rack when prompted), handing a test tube between arms mid-air, drawing an X on a tic-tac-toe board, and playing chess. These goals and types of manipulation don't appear in our training data. Furthermore, ZAP also transfers to an unseen embodiment. On a single-arm Franka, ZAP reaches 55.9% on RoboLab-120, 4.9 percentage points ahead of the leading VLA/WAM baseline.

## 1 Introduction
A robot policy has to do two difficult things at once: it needs semantic reasoning to understand instructions in a potentially unfamiliar scene, and motion control to translate that understanding into precise physical actions. We introduce Zero-real Agent Policy (ZAP), which adds motion to a small pretrained vision-language model through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL), entirely in simulation. We evaluate how these skills carry over to new tasks, real hardware, and a new embodiment. Existing methods tend to trade off performance in one of these areas for the others, and broadly fall into three groups (Table 1 ). Models that directly output robot actions. Vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world-action models (WAMs) learn semantic reasoning & motion control from robot action data [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] . Because diverse action data is scarce, both skills end up tied to the training distribution: the policy often generalizes poorly to new prompts, objects, scenes and goals, and a new task usually means training again. Code that directly drives the robot. In Code as Policies [ 5 , 6 ] , a language model writes a program that composes perception and control APIs. This is quick and reliable when the situations it will face can be anticipated when the program is written, but struggles outside that script, for example with an unexpected failure, an unfamiliar object or an ambiguous instruction. Adapting to a new task or scene means rewriting the program. Large frontier models controlling the robot through tools. Frontier language models interacting with the robot via an agentic harness bring world understanding, reasoning and generalization [ 7 , 8 , 9 ] . But they are not explicitly trained to move a robot, and a trillion-parameter model is slow and costs dollars for a task that smaller policy models, when they can do them, do in seconds and cost pennies. Approach Speed Cost Generalization VLAs / WAMs ✓ ✓ – Code as Policies ✓ ✓ – Large agent as policy (frontier LLM + tools) – – ✓ ZAP (ours) ✓ ✓ ✓ Table 1: Comparing robot policy paradigms. VLAs/WAMs and Code as Policies offer fast, inexpensive execution, while frontier agents provide broader generalization at higher latency and cost. ZAP combines fast, low-cost execution with generalization to new instructions, objects, scenes, and goals. ZAP combines semantic reasoning from vision-language pretraining with motion control learned entirely in simulation. We start with Qwen 3.6 27B [ 10 ] , which contributes world understanding and reasoning. We then teach the model motion control in simulation - first SFT on its own mined successful traces, followed by RL across 40 pick-and-place, stacking, and reorientation tasks. Neither stage uses real-world robot demonstrations. At test time, the policy combines pretrained semantic reasoning with learned motion control to perform real-world tasks it was never trained to execute, generalizing to new objects, goals, and robot embodiments. Our contributions are as follows: • Teacher-free training of an agent policy in simulation. We teach a pretrained vision-language model to control a robot with no frontier teacher and no real robot data. The model commands end-effector poses and gripper openings through a harness that is identical in simulation and on hardware. We seed it with successful traces mined from its own rollouts, then refine it with RL using a dense reward ladder that credits partial progress (§ 3 ). • Transfer to hardware and new embodiment. With no real-world robot training data, ZAP matches GPT-6 Astra on 10 real tasks at 3 \times the speed and 1/17 the cost. The same checkpoint transfers without retraining to a completely new embodiment (single-arm Franka) exceeding the performance of leading VLA/WAM baselines on RoboLab-120 (§ 4 ). • Generalization to new goals, language, and reasoning. ZAP combines pretrained semantic reasoning with learned motion control to execute real-world tasks unseen during robot training. The policy handles new objects and goals, follows instructions for manipulation strategies it never practiced, and applies complex reasoning to tasks such as chess and drawing (§ 5 ).

## 3 Method
We first describe the harness, the tool interface through which the model perceives and acts, identical in simulation and on hardware (§ 3.1 ). We then describe how we train the model to move entirely in simulation using GRPO and a dense reward ladder (§ 3.2 ), and how we initialize that run from successful traces gathered by a short GRPO warm-up and prefix-tree search (§ 3.3 ). 3.1 One interface across simulation and hardware ZAP controls two 6-DoF (degrees-of-freedom) YAM arms with parallel-jaw grippers [ 26 ] . It commands absolute end-effector poses and gripper openings, rather than joint angles, trajectories, or action chunks. We frame tasks as a conversation between the model and its environment through the harness: the model first writes a plan and names the objects to locate based on camera views of the environment, then repeatedly issues perception and movement tool calls to complete its task. The harness uses SAM 3.1 [ 27 , 28 ] to segment the objects named in the model’s perception queries in each camera stream. These masks are combined with calibrated depth cameras to yield each object’s 3D centroid, height, and principal axes. This process runs continuously and asynchronously in the background: the model steers perception by changing its queries, and receives object measurements and annotated camera images. The model controls the robot and manages the episode through five tools, two of which move the robot: move_to sets a complete end-effector pose for one or both arms, and set_gripper sets each jaw opening. change_query sets what perception looks for, update_todo keeps a short plan in view across turns, and done ends the episode. There are no grasp macros or motion primitives, so every approach, grasp, lift and release is a sequence of these two motion tools that the model plans itself. Appendix A describes the rest of the harness: the pose and orientation parameterization, the observations, validation, the motion controller and its outcome reports, and how the same harness runs in simulation and on hardware. Appendix B steps through a complete real-robot episode turn by turn. 3.2 Learning to move in simulation We build our simulated tasks in Isaac Sim [ 29 ] by adding simulated YAM arms with calibrated cameras and arm dynamics against real YAM arms. Learning motion control on simulated tasks. We design 40 simulated tasks, none of which appear in RoboLab-120, that exercise the same families of everyday object manipulation skills: grasping and lifting, single- and multi-object pick and place, stacking, and reorientation. The goals are deliberately simple, and none requires heavy reasoning. This lets us test whether motion control learned on simple tasks can combine with pretrained semantic reasoning to solve unseen tasks with new goals and strategies. GRPO with dense rewards. The GRPO run is initialized from the checkpoint detailed in § 3.3 and trains on all 40 tasks (hyperparameters in Appendix C ). Binary reward based on task success is inefficient since some tasks start with zero successes, and therefore a pass/fail reward would provide zero advantage and contribute no gradient.
