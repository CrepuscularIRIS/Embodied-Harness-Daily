# Is Your Robot's Memory Just a Clock? Temporal Shortcuts in Memory-Augmented VLA Policies

## Abstract
Memory-augmented vision-language-action (VLA) policies are meant to remember what happened. Many of them, however, fill memory by time: they sample frames uniformly across the episode and stamp each frame with its time step. Because time passes even when nothing happens, such a memory may not tell waiting from working. We show that it does not. On the counting tasks of RoboMME, we make the robot briefly pause or fidget its wrist. Such a null event changes nothing the task depends on, yet a released memory VLA counts the interval as work done and stops early. We call this failure phantom progress. It cuts success from 47 to 16 of 50 on one task and from 44 to 10 on the other. When the same frames are withheld from memory, success returns, so the cause lies in memory rather than in the interruption itself. We trace the failure to a temporal shortcut: because demonstrations proceed at a steady pace, elapsed time predicts progress. Indeed, rescaling the time stamps alone makes the policy stop early when the clock runs fast and repeat when it runs slow. Surprisingly, removing the time encoding does not cure the failure, because the frames of a null event still occupy slots of the time-sampled memory. Across RoboMME's released designs, vulnerability follows how memory is indexed. Of the fixes the diagnosis suggests, training with injected pauses does not remove the failure and impairs counting, whereas indexing memory by events recovers half or more of the loss without retraining. A memory meant to remember what happened should count events rather than clock ticks.

## 1 Introduction
Many manipulation tasks cannot be solved from the current observation alone. A robot asked to pick up a cube three times and then press a button sees much the same scene before its second pick as before its third, so it has to remember how many picks it has made. Memory-augmented vision-language-action (VLA) policies address this by conditioning each action on a memory of the episode so far [ 41 , 44 , 48 , 11 ] . A simple and widely used way to fill such a memory is by time: the policy samples past frames uniformly over the episode and stamps each frame with its time step. This design covers the whole episode at a fixed cost, and on RoboMME [ 11 ] , a benchmark built to test memory, the strongest released policy uses exactly this design. However, time passes even when nothing happens. Real robots often wait for a slow planner, for an operator who has paused them, or for a safety monitor to clear. They also hesitate, or reach for an object and come back empty-handed. We call such an interval a null event . It takes time, and the robot may even move, yet nothing the task depends on changes. A memory that records what happened should therefore pass over a null event as if it had not occurred. A memory filled by time cannot do so, because the interval receives its share of sampled frames and time stamps like any other stretch of the episode. This raises a simple question: Can a memory filled by time tell waiting from working? We find that it cannot ( Fig. 1 ). On the two counting tasks of RoboMME, we make the released policy pause twice for 5 seconds, or roll its wrist back and forth for as long. Success falls from 47 to 16 of 50 on PickXtimes and from 44 to 10 on SwingXtimes. Almost every failure is an early stop, in which the policy presses the button before it has made the required number of repetitions. The robot thus perceives progress that never happened. We call this failure phantom progress . One might suspect that the interruption itself disturbs the robot, for instance by leaving its arm in an unusual pose. We therefore repeat each event with its frames withheld from memory. The robot pauses or fidgets exactly as before, but its memory never sees the interval, and success returns to within three episodes of the baseline. The failure therefore lies in what the policy remembers. Why would a policy mistake waiting for working? We trace the failure to a temporal shortcut . Demonstrations proceed at a steady pace, so elapsed time alone predicts how many repetitions are done 89% and 80% of the time on the two tasks, and over 92% on regular demonstrations from two real teleoperation datasets. Since demonstrations contain almost no idle stretches, imitation learning never penalises a policy for reading progress from time. If the policy indeed reads progress from time, rescaling the time stamps alone should shift its failures in a predictable direction. It does: with no null event and the frames unchanged, a faster clock makes the policy stop early, and a slower one makes it repeat ( Sec. 5.2 ). The obvious remedy is then to remove the time encoding. Surprisingly, a policy trained from scratch without it still fails under pauses (21 and 14 of 50), because the frames of a null event still occupy slots of a memory sampled by time ( Sec. 5.3 ). The shortcut thus lies in how memory is indexed, and the time stamps are only one of its carriers. Consistently, among RoboMME’s released designs, every memory indexed by time collapses under null events, a memory that selects tokens by pixel change is hurt less by pauses than by fidgets, and memories indexed by sub-goals withstand both null events on PickXtimes ( Sec. 5.4 ). The diagnosis also suggests where to fix the failure. Training with injected pauses changes the data but keeps the index, so at best it can teach the policy to discount the null events that we know how to synthesise. It does not even achieve this: pause augmentation does not remove the failure, and it impairs counting when no null event occurs. Changing the index should work instead. Indexing memory by events collapses every stretch in which the world returns to a state it was already in. Applied at test time without retraining, it recovers half or more of the lost success under pauses, fidgets and air-grasps alike ( Sec. 6 ). We summarize our contributions as follows: • Phantom progress. Brief null events make memory VLAs that fill memory by time stop early, and the damage passes through memory alone. Comparing visible with hidden null events, together with rescaling the time stamps, gives a protocol for testing whether any memory policy tracks events or elapsed time ( Sec. 4 ). • A shortcut in the index. We trace the failure to a temporal shortcut that lives in how memory is indexed, so that removing the time stamps does not remove it, and we show that vulnerability follows the index across RoboMME’s released designs ( Sec. 5 ). • Fixes suggested by the diagnosis. ## 3 Setup
Tasks and policies. RoboMME [ 11 ] provides memory-dependent manipulation tasks in simulation. We focus on its two counting tasks, where memory is needed to decide when the task is done: in PickXtimes the robot picks and places a cube K\in\{1,\dots,5\} times and then presses a button; in SwingXtimes it carries a cube back and forth between two targets K times. We study the released MME-VLA policies [ 11 ] , built on \pi_{0.5} [ 36 ] . Their memory, FrameSamp, keeps 32 frames sampled uniformly in time from the whole episode and stamps each with a sinusoidal encoding of its absolute time step. The three variants read this memory through cross-attention in the action expert (“Modul”), through a separate memory expert (“Expert”), or in context (“Context”). TokenDrop instead keeps tokens selected by pixel change, with the same time encoding. For comparison across memory designs ( Sec. 5.4 ) we also evaluate the released MemER-style keyframe memory [ 44 ] , a text sub-goal memory, a recurrent test-time-training memory, and \pi_{0.5} without memory. Unless stated otherwise, results use FrameSamp-Modul, the strongest released policy. Models trained from scratch. To test whether the failure needs the time encoding, we train two models that differ only in it. Both start from the \pi_{0.5} base checkpoint and follow the released recipe on the RoboMME training data for 30k steps, but Clock keeps the time encoding while NoClock removes it. Without null events both come close to the released model ( Appendix F ). Null events. At action-chunk boundaries after policy steps 48 and 160 we inject one of three events that leave unchanged what the task depends on ( Fig. 2 a): a pause , in which the robot holds still for 100 environment steps (5 s; 30 and 300 steps in dose studies); a wrist fidget , in which the wrist rolls \pm 0.3 rad around its held target for 100 steps; and an air-grasp , in which the motion planner executes a grasp above a resting object and returns to the pre-event pose.
