# Do VLAs Know They Will Fail? Interpretable Success and Failure Patterns in Physical AI Systems

## Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as powerful general-purpose robot policies, but their successes and failures remain difficult to diagnose and understand. In this work, we show that sparse autoencoder (SAE) representations of OpenVLA activations contain outcome-predictive information and can be used to predict task failure before the robot reaches an unrecoverable state. Moreover, to provide insights into what common behaviors may contribute to these outcomes, we identify individual SAE features whose strong activations are associated with specific stages of success or failure, and uncover recurring behavioral patterns by localizing temporal intervals where these features exhibit significant activation changes. We further develop a VLM-based interpretation pipeline to generate behavioral descriptions of these patterns and validate their correspondence to feature activations on held-out intervals.

## 1 Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models have achieved strong generalist performance by scaling model capacity and training data [ 15 , 9 ] , but their internal representations make it difficult to diagnose why a rollout succeeds or fails. Recent work shows that these representations anticipate failure. SAFE learns a multitask failure detector from internal VLA features and uses conformal prediction to trade detection accuracy against warning time [ 5 ] . Mahato and Ren show that near-term failure is linearly decodable from OpenVLA activations under visual distribution shift [ 11 ] . FPC-VLA instead predicts failures with an external VLM supervisor [ 14 ] . These works report warning time relative to the end of the episode. The problem is that every action an agent takes changes how easy the remaining task is to complete; thus, these approaches may trigger an alarm after the task has become unrecoverable. We therefore build on this line of research by measuring lead time against a point of no return , a timestep after which the only reachable states by branched policies are failures. More importantly, we aim to move beyond predicting outcomes toward identifying the behavioral patterns strongly associated with success or failure. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) provide a promising approach by decomposing dense, polysemantic activations into sparse, potentially interpretable features [ 3 , 4 ] . Recent work has applied this technique to VLAs. Haon et al. [ 7 ] identify interpretable, steerable directions in OpenVLA. Swann et al. [ 13 ] find SAE features for motion and semantics, some of which causally steer behavior. Event-Grounded SAEs tie features to behaviorally salient events in closed-loop rollouts [ 8 ] . These works characterize features by what they represent or control. We ask a complementary question: which sparse features track whether a rollout will succeed, and what behavior do they correspond to? We show that SAE features preserve the outcome-predictive information in OpenVLA activations, and that, evaluated against the point of no return, the predictor separates eventual failures from successes while the failures are still recoverable. We then select individual features by their association with trajectory outcomes and automatically localize the critical intervals where they emerge. Finally, we describe these intervals with a VLM and validate the descriptions on held-out clips via detection scoring [ 1 , 12 ] . Together, these steps connect early failure warning to interpretable, failure-relevant behavior in VLA policies. An extended discussion of related work is in Appendix A .

## 2 Experimental Results
2.1 Problem Setup We run OpenVLA-7B [ 9 ] fine-tuned on LIBERO-10 [ 10 ] greedily from the 50 benchmark initial states of each of the ten tasks, giving 500 rollouts that end either in success or in failure at a 520-step timeout (51.6% success; 95 trajectories held out for evaluation). Let \mathbf{h}_{i,t} denote the layer-0 residual-stream activation at timestep t of trajectory i , averaged over image tokens. While dense activations may contain outcome-predictive information, their polysemanticity makes it difficult to identify the specific behavioral patterns associated with success or failure [ 3 , 4 ] . We therefore use a TopK sparse autoencoder (2,048 features, K=64 ) to decompose them into sparse feature activations \mathbf{a}_{i,t} . We ask whether \mathbf{h}_{i,t} and \mathbf{a}_{i,t} predict the eventual outcome, and whether individual SAE features reveal interpretable success- and failure-related behavior (task, splits, and SAE details are in Appendix B ). 2.2 Online Outcome Prediction We first test whether the raw activations and SAE features contain information predictive of the eventual trajectory outcome. Specifically, at each timestep t , we train a logistic regressor f_{t} using either the raw activations or SAE features to predict the final outcome of an ongoing trajectory, taking its predicted failure probability as p_{\mathrm{fail}}(t) . To reduce transient activation noise, we consider activations within a rolling window of T=40 steps rather than single-step activations. The predictor input is the rolling mean raw activation m^{h}_{i,t}=1/T\sum_{s=t-T+1}^{t}\mathbf{h}_{i,s} or the rolling mean SAE representation m^{a}_{i,t}=1/T\sum_{s=t-T+1}^{t}\mathbf{a}_{i,s} . As successful trajectories tend to terminate earlier, the activations at large t predominantly come from failed trajectories, causing severe label imbalance and making high accuracy easy to achieve. We therefore compare our predictor with a majority baseline that takes no input and always predicts the majority outcome label at each timestep t . Figure 1 (a) shows that raw activations and SAE features achieve similar accuracy and outperform the majority baseline at early-to-mid timesteps. Their AUROCs are also nearly identical (0.80 and 0.81, respectively, over all held-out frames), remaining above chance from the first 80 steps (0.68) through steps 240-319 (0.75). These results indicate that SAE features preserve most of the outcome-predictive information in the original OpenVLA activations. Recoverability-grounded early warning. Predicting failure before an episode ends does not necessarily provide an actionable warning, as the robot may have already reached an unrecoverable state. To evaluate whether our predictor identifies failures while recovery is still possible, we estimate state recoverability by restoring saved simulator states and sampling policy continuations. We define the point of no return (PNR) as the earliest probe after which all subsequent probes have estimated recoverability \hat{R}\leq 0.1 . These results highlight the trade-off between reducing false alarms and providing sufficiently early warnings. 2.3 Discovering Outcome-Associated SAE Features Next, we seek SAE features whose activations may associate with successful or failed outcomes, potentially revealing specific success- or failure-related behavioral patterns. For feature j , we define the rolling mean activation as m_{i,t,j}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=t-T+1}^{t}a_{i,s,j} , and the trajectory score as the maximum rolling mean activation, s_{i,j}=\max_{t\geq T}m_{i,t,j} . We hypothesize that some features exhibit strong indications of success or failure when highly activated. We therefore select the top- k trajectories by trajectory score for each feature j and compute their success rate among these top- k trajectories. We use k=30 in our experiments.
