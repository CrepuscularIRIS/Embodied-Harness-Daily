# Learning the Task or Learning the Verifier? Rethinking Criteria Exposure in Embodied Self-Improvement

## Abstract
Self-improving embodied agents are increasingly being developed and compared on robot simulation benchmarks. Several recent works expose the benchmark's success criteria to the learning agent, a practice we call Criteria Exposure (CE). These criteria, originally written to verify policies trained by imitation learning, often demand more than the corresponding language instructions, so a score from such a verifier may not faithfully reflect whether the instructed task was completed, especially for robot-control programs written by an agent. As embodied coding agents report ever higher benchmark scores, a natural question arises: when a coding agent is exposed to the benchmark's criteria, does it generate code that better performs the instructed task, or code that simply overfits to the verification criteria? We conduct controlled experiments with a self-improving agent with and without CE, and find that with CE the agent quickly produces code with a higher success rate under the given criteria, but this advantage disappears when the criteria are changed slightly without departing from the original instructions. More importantly, the skills the agent develops under CE transfer poorly across tasks. We further propose instruction-faithful criteria and show that they better reflect the performance of agent-generated programs.

## 1 Introduction
Agents that write code to control robots with multimodal language models complete many manipulation tasks zero-shot [ 19 , 15 , 12 , 29 ] , but preserve nothing from one episode to the next, which makes self-improvement across episodes a natural next step [ 28 , 31 , 36 ] . Such agents are developed and compared on embodied simulation benchmarks [ 9 , 20 , 22 ] originally built to evaluate end-to-end policies trained by imitating demonstrations, such as vision-language-action (VLA) models [ 6 , 17 , 4 , 25 ] and world action models (WAMs) [ 26 ] . The actual success criteria of these benchmarks often ask for more than the instruction; in the press_by_number task of RoboDojo, for instance, the criteria require the blue button to be pressed exactly twice, between two groups of red presses, which the instruction never states ( figure 1 a). For policies trained by imitation, this gap is harmless, since their demonstrations already contain every detail the criteria verify: order, counts, end states, and the return of both arms. An agent that receives only the instruction, however, lacks this information. Existing self-improving methods therefore expose the criteria to the agent to different degrees, from rule descriptions [ 10 ] and published scoring tables [ 18 , 13 ] to numeric thresholds [ 14 ] , formal goal predicates [ 32 ] , and the source code of the verifier [ 3 ] . A robot in the real world, by contrast, receives only its instruction and, at most, a success judged by a human or a VLM. Our research question is: when a self-improving embodied agent is exposed to the success criteria, does the code it iteratively produces better perform the instructed task, or simply overfit to the verification criteria? Studies of benchmark validity and of specification gaming show that criteria can demand more than the instruction states and that agents exploit a verifier they can inspect [ 16 , 40 , 37 ] , but we are not aware of work that measures how much of a reported gain in embodied self-improvement comes from reading the criteria, or whether it survives when the criteria change. We therefore compare two settings of one fixed self-improvement loop, in which a frozen coding agent acts and a learner agent turns its episodes into a library of skills and memory: with Criteria Exposure (w/ CE), the learner reads the criteria; without it (w/o CE), the learner sees only whether each episode succeeded. We ask three questions: on which tasks exposure raises the score; whether its lead survives a change of criteria, both of the criteria that judge the episodes and of the criteria the learner reads; and whether what it adds carries over to new tasks and to two further benchmarks, LIBERO-PRO and RoboCasa365. To change the criteria, we rewrite them for the RoboDojo tasks whose criteria ask for more than the instruction, so that they verify only what the instruction asks. On these tasks every episode is judged by both versions, and in a separate run the learner reads the rewritten criteria instead of the original ones. Our experiments reveal three findings. First, under the original criteria, exposure helps only on tasks whose criteria ask for more than the instruction; when criteria and instruction agree, learning from success alone does just as well. Second, the score of exposure follows the criteria it read: when the same episodes are judged by instruction-faithful criteria, its advantage disappears, as the exposed libraries lose successes while the success-only libraries gain them ( figure 1 b); conversely, a library learned from the rewritten criteria scores far lower when judged by the original ones. Third, the lead of exposure does not reappear on new RoboDojo tasks, and across benchmarks neither the exposed nor the success-only library gives a clear gain over the empty library; only experience gathered within the target benchmark does. Taken together, these results suggest a practical principle for evaluating embodied self-improvement: the learner should receive only the feedback a robot would receive in the real world, and the benchmark’s criteria should verify what the instruction says. A gain that holds when the criteria are changed but still agree with the instruction reflects what the agent has learned about the task. Seen this way, whether the learner reads the criteria is not an implementation detail of a self-improving agent but a choice about measurement, and it determines what a reported gain means.

Third, among criteria-limited tasks the lead depends on whether an unstated rule shows in failed episodes: in build_tower the leftover blocks reveal it and both settings reach 20/20, whereas the button protocol of press_by_number, blue pressed twice and between the two red groups, never shows, and the success-only learner never finds it. 4.1.2 Synthesis • Exposure pays off only where the criteria ask for more than the instruction. Richer feedback is often assumed to make self-improvement better. Our results show a condition: when the criteria verify what the instruction says, success alone tells the learner what the task requires, and what remains, a reliable procedure, is learned equally well in both settings. Exposure helps only with rules that the instruction omits and that failures do not reveal. • The advantage of exposure measures the benchmark as much as the agent. Because it comes from what the instruction leaves out, the gain of exposure grows with the gap between a benchmark’s instructions and its criteria.
