# Envisioning Agentic Robotics: A New Era of Robotic Systems

## Abstract
The release of GPT-6 Astra marks the emergence of a new paradigm in robotics: agentic robotics. Within weeks, frontier models with agentic harnesses matched or exceeded task-specific policies on many embodied benchmarks. Instead of training a dedicated policy for each family of tasks, an agentic robotic system applies a foundation model to open-world embodied tasks. It operates in a closed loop: observing the environment, interpreting user intent, reasoning about the task, invoking skills and tools, verifying outcomes, and adapting its strategy. Despite these advances, the community has yet to converge on a shared definition, a reliable evaluation framework, or a research roadmap. We argue that Embodied System 2, realized by a foundation model together with an embodied harness, forms the decision-making core of agentic robotics. Its central challenge is to translate general-purpose reasoning into effective and verifiable actions under physical constraints such as limited reaction time, mechanical limits, unpredictable environment changes, and irreversible consequences. Motivated by this perspective, we propose an evaluation framework that measures both model capabilities and closed-loop system performance. Furthermore, we identify the principles that carry over from digital to physical agents and the challenges that must be addressed to achieve reliable physical autonomy. On top of the Embodied System 2 and the evaluation framework, we envision a research roadmap toward sustained autonomy: the ability to perform work safely and reliably with progressively broader responsibility, from executing individual primitives to fulfilling standing roles.

## 1 Introduction
A central goal of robotics is to enable robots to translate human intent into desired outcomes in the physical world. Early autonomous robots often integrated hand-engineered components for perception, planning, and control within modular or layered architectures ( Nilsson, 1984 ; Gat et al., 1998 ) . Since then, advances in deep learning have allowed robots to learn increasingly complex behaviors. Policies learned in simulation, mostly with deep reinforcement learning, have produced agile locomotion ( Hwangbo et al., 2019 ) and versatile humanoid whole-body control by tracking human motion data ( Liao et al., 2025 ; Luo et al., 2026c ; Liao et al., 2026 ) . However, these simulation-trained policies remain specialized to specific tasks and embodiments, limiting their ability to generalize across diverse and open-ended environments. To move beyond task-specific controllers and enable robots to follow human instructions, the field has increasingly turned toward models that unify perception, language understanding, and action generation within a single framework. Among them, vision-language-action (VLA) models, exemplified by the \pi series, combine vision-language pretraining with diverse robot data to learn general policies that map visual observations and language instructions to actions ( Black et al., 2025a ; Black et al., 2025b ) . More recently, world-action models (WAM) integrate video-based modeling of physical dynamics with robot policy learning ( Ye et al., 2026a ; Yuan et al., 2026b ; Li et al., 2026c ) . Together, these advances have broadened robot capabilities. Yet reliable generalization remains a central challenge, as policy performance can still deteriorate when scenes or instructions change ( Zhou et al., 2025c ) . To address this generalization challenge, a major line of research focuses on scaling and diversifying training data for VLA and WAM policies ( Zheng et al., 2026 ; Dyna Robotics, 2026a ) . These efforts draw on diverse data sources, including real-robot data ( Black et al., 2025a ) , UMI demonstrations ( Chi et al., 2024 ) , egocentric human data ( Zheng et al., 2026 ) , and multimodal web data ( Brohan et al., 2023 ) . The release of GPT-6 Astra ( OpenAI, 2026a ; OpenAI, 2026b ) and its early applications to robotics offered a different perspective on this challenge. In an early evaluation, the model outperformed trained policies on many robotic benchmarks without task-specific fine-tuning ( Zhang et al., 2026c ) . Subsequent work explored its use with learned policies and robotic tools ( Galbot Team et al., 2026 ; Gao et al., 2026b ) . These results suggest that generalization can also be achieved by relying on a general-purpose model’s knowledge and reasoning to organize and adapt behavior during execution. This perspective motivates a paradigm shift from policy-centric robotics to agentic robotics , by shifting the focus from learned action policies (Figure 2 a) to an agent that pursues goals by coordinating skills and tools, evaluating outcomes, and adapting its decisions (Figure 2 b). Despite this progress, the community still lacks a shared understanding of agentic robotics or a reliable evaluation framework. The scope of the paradigm, its relationship to existing forms of robot autonomy, and the organization of its constituent functions remain insufficiently clarified ( Qin et al., 2026a ) . Furthermore, differences in experimental conditions and system configurations also make it difficult to compare results across studies. For example, RoboDojo evaluations of Astra vary in the task-knowledge sources they use and the action budgets they permit ( Zhang et al., 2026c ; SenseNova et al., 2026 ) ; separately, Harness VLA shows substantial performance gains from execution memory when the planner and VLA are held fixed ( Zhang et al., 2026f ) . All of these results underscore the need to establish what constitutes the agentic system, which capabilities are being assessed, and under what conditions comparisons are meaningful. These conceptual and methodological challenges call for a unified framework that clarifies the agentic workflow by specifying the functional roles of its components and how they interact. Such a framework can provide a shared conceptual foundation for characterizing agentic robotics, developing valid and comparable evaluations at the model and system levels, and formulating a research roadmap. ## 2 Overview of Agentic Robotics
We adopt a system-level view of agentic robotics: an agent interprets the user’s intent, decides how to proceed, orchestrates skills and tools at run time, and verifies their physical effects against the intended goal. Feedback from verification guides subsequent decisions, enabling the system to adapt its actions, recover from failures, and seek clarification or assistance when needed. We first present a reference architecture that defines the responsibilities and interfaces of S2, S1, and S0. We then go through the agentic loop and examine three system-level properties: operation across multiple timescales, responsiveness during deliberation, and state persistence across tasks. 2.1 Reference Architecture In general, an agentic robot requires three functional roles: S2 interprets intent, reasons about goals and constraints, orchestrates capabilities, and verifies outcomes; S1 provides skills and callable modules; and S0 provides the body interface and local real-time control. Note that this decomposition is functional: one model may serve several roles, and one role may span several models or modules. Figure 3 shows the three systems and their interfaces, together with the user, the robot body, the physical world, and a real-time event monitor. The systems typically run on different timescales, but timing alone does not distinguish them. We describe them from S2 downward, focusing on their responsibilities and interactions. System 2: Intent, Reasoning, Orchestration, and Verification. S2 is the functional layer that interprets intent, reasons about goals and constraints to make decisions, orchestrates skills and tools accordingly, and verifies outcomes to adapt subsequent decisions toward the intended goals. It decides whether to act, gather information, ask for clarification, continue, retry, replan, or stop. When acting, it chooses a capability and specifies the call, which may invoke an S1 skill or a tool, or send an action target directly to S0.
