# RoboJev: An Open Vision-Language Judge for Robot Success Detection

## Abstract
Many robot manipulation videos do not indicate whether the task was successfully completed. RoboJev-27B is an open-weight vision-language model that takes a task instruction, 10 frames of a manipulation attempt, and (if available) final frames from auxiliary cameras and predicts whether the robot accomplished the task. RoboJev-27B is a variant of JEV-27B-VL, trained with a rank-32 LoRA adapter on the language model only (keeping the vision encoder frozen) using 52,598 episode records. It also uses a source-specific KL penalty that applies only to research data, anchoring predictions closer to the original model on those sources. It uses a calibrated two-token readout that outputs success/failure probabilities in a single forward pass. On 268 held-out episodes of the same robot model (SO-101), RoboJev-27B attains 95.2% balanced accuracy and 0.993 AUROC. It also generalises to episodes collected by unseen labs/robots (AUROC = 0.890 over 459 episodes) as well as to clearly labelled community-collected episodes (accuracy = 93.3% over 135 episodes), where the base model attains an AUROC = 0.900 on the unseen-lab/robot subset with the same inputs. It takes 0.26 seconds per episode (median, serial) to infer on a single H100, which is about \$0.19 per 1,000 success predictions at \$2.69 per GPU-hour (not including media processing and serving costs). RoboJev-27B is released together with its inference code, along with a transparent evaluation: 20,628 predictions organized into 29 model/protocol entries are made available to facilitate browsing and to enable reproducible benchmarking of success detection. We also compare against strong API-based frontier VLMs, which take in fewer input frames (4 images from the primary camera only); note that this comparison is not strictly like-for-like because RoboJev-27B is given more frames at inference.

## 1 Introduction
An important ingredient for review queues, learned terminal rewards, or aggregate evaluation is knowing which episodes succeeded. Such labels are often not machine readable and cannot be easily derived from low-level heuristics (e.g. trajectory length, data origin, or smoothness). We therefore fine-tune an episode judge from JEV-27B-VL [ 1 , 2 ] into RoboJev, an open-weight success detector for robot manipulation videos. Given a task instruction, ten evenly spaced frames from the primary camera, and the final frame from any auxiliary cameras, RoboJev outputs a calibrated success probability. Rather than building a different classifier for every task and environment, RoboJev can be used in a plug-and-play fashion. Rather than generating an explanation, RoboJev scores true / false tokens at a designated position. RoboJev is reusable across tasks and environments thanks to its well-defined inputs, consistent prompting protocol, and verifiable error logs. We test RoboJev on held-out SO-101 rollouts, on lab/robot domains not included in the robot-specific fine-tuning, and on community videos that have clear labels. Community videos with ambiguous labels can still be inspected, but are not used for binary metrics. While the SO-101 split contains rollouts from the same physical environment as some of the training data, we leave out two entire tasks, as well as individual recording sessions. On this split, RoboJev-27B achieves 95.2% balanced accuracy. On the unseen-domain data, it achieves 0.890 AUROC, just under the 0.900 AUROC of the unadapted JEV-27B-VL run with the same input protocol. Alongside our released predictions, protocols, and a pinned checkpoint, this indicates that RoboJev is a useful and inspectable tool for policy evaluation, rather than indicating that fine-tuning yields generalizable improvements.

## 3 A task-conditioned episode judge
3.1 Input and decision readout Let u be a task instruction, and V=\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{N-1}\} a video captured by the primary camera. Our release samples K=10 evenly spaced frames from the beginning to the end of the attempt, and includes the last image from each available auxiliary camera. The prompt specifies the task instruction, chronological order, and all other available views. Success requires completing the instruction by the end of the attempt; a dropped object, incorrect placement, unfinished attempt, or wrong task is negative. The vision-language model then consumes all this information in one pass, generating the logits z_{0},z_{1} at the answer location for the tokens false and true . We employ the following calibrated readout p_{\theta}(y=1\mid u,V)=\frac{\exp((z_{1}+b_{1})/T)}{\sum_{c\in\{0,1\}}\exp((z_{c}+b_{c})/T)}, (1) where the release preserves the token IDs, additive biases b_{c} , and the temperature T in decision.json . In the release, we set T=0.9 . This output provides a measure of the likelihood that the task was successfully completed at the end of the episode; it is not a continuous quantity reflecting the progress made by the agent toward completion of the task, nor does it describe the result in natural language. 3.2 Training data Our model is fit on 52,598 episodes. The data used to fit our model is a mixture of policy failures (from real robots), demonstrations, and simulation interaction data. Specifically, there are 28,170 episodes from RBM-1M sources, 1,388 episodes from ArmnetBench, 10,216 teacher labelled community episodes, 3,718 community demonstration episodes, 4,962 episodes from ViFailback, and 4,144 successful episodes from DROID. These are the number of records used for fitting after the train/valid/test split, and do not necessarily correspond to the number of records in each dataset before the split. We do not claim that these counts are independently de-duplicated. We hold out entire validation datasets, and hold out community data from users that we evaluate on from our training mix. We leave a description of the exact composition of our training set in Appendix C . There is a small number of records that do not match up with the number returned by the community loader, the cause of which we could not determine. In total, the community loader reports 11,415 records were accepted, although a subset of these are discarded during validation. We start with annotations that have high-confidence agreement between GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol (probability \geq 0.9 or \leq 0.1 ) on the four primary-camera frames. If a Claude Opus 5.5 annotation exists, we discard all annotations it disagrees with on the binary outcome. We then average over all teacher-model annotations to obtain a soft target and discard any examples where the mean is not \geq 0.9 or \leq 0.1 . This process yields a consensus annotation file with 11,387 unique targets. After removing validation examples and those involving held-out robots, 10,216 examples remain for training. To avoid information leakage between training and test labels, we also discard training examples from all 106 owners that appear in the human-labeled community check. The student model still depends on teacher models, which we also use as baselines. We employ inverse-frequency class balancing for the success/failure examples for each source, clamping weights to [0.25,4] , and if a source contains only one type of example, we assign weight 1. For data augmentation we use truncated positive episodes and episodes with mismatched instructions as heuristic negatives. This may introduce label noise because a truncated episode might fulfill a shorter instruction. We also resize, recompress using JPEG, and perturb the colour of images. Eligible episodes with ten-frame evidence use the ten-frame protocol with probability 0.6. The other branches of augmentation preserve the four-frame and final-view examples. In total, we obtain 102,172 training examples in the epoch through data augmentation and repetition. 3.3 Adaptation and calibration We start with JEV-27B-VL, merging in the decision adapter weights before doing any robot-specific adaptation.
