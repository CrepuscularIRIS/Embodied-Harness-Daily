# When Coding Agents Get Arms: Safety Risks Across the Digital-Physical Boundary

## Abstract
Advances in spatial understanding have enabled frontier models to power coding agents that control a real robot arm zero-shot and end-to-end, without task-specific demonstrations or retraining. This brings two new safety challenges. First, the extensive knowledge of frontier models lets these agents perform tasks that earlier VLA and WAM paradigms could not, opening the door to new forms of capability misuse. Second, coding agents can access digital resources such as web pages and emails. Threats in these resources, such as indirect prompt injection, can carry over into the physical world when the agent controls a robot. We therefore introduce ArmedAgent, the first systematic benchmark to evaluate both risks for coding agents performing zero-shot robot control. It covers robustness, malicious use, and indirect injection across 36 tasks in 5 scenarios, evaluated in simulation and validated on a physical robot arm. A distinctive portion of the tasks target harmful behaviors that earlier VLA paradigms could not perform. Testing several frontier models, including GPT-6-Astra, under widely used agent frameworks, we find substantial safety risks in current systems.

## 1 Introduction
Recent general purpose large models have made substantial progress in spatial understanding [ 12 , 19 , 18 , 24 ] . As a consequence, coding agents can now control a real robot arm zero-shot and end-to-end. The agent perceives the scene through a camera, calls the robot’s pose and manipulation APIs, and directly emits executable control code [ 15 ] . Unlike vision-language-action (VLA) models and world-action models (WAM), which require new data collection and fine-tuning whenever the task or the platform changes, this route needs neither task-specific demonstrations nor retraining, lowering the barrier to deployment and broadening the range of tasks that can be attempted. The most capable models today approach saturation on real-world pick-and-place benchmarks despite never having been trained for robotic manipulation [ 14 , 6 , 16 ] , and this capability is improving with the underlying models. General capability connects the digital and physical worlds, and so does risk. The access to code execution, the web, and local files that makes coding agents useful also exposes them to attack [ 17 ] . Once the agent controls an actuator, the damage from a successful attack is no longer confined to the computer. Even a benign request can lead to unsafe behavior, such as wiping a power strip with a visibly wet cloth (Fig. 1 a). A malicious user can also exploit the agent’s world knowledge and generalization to carry out harmful tasks that a VLA policy could not perform, such as painting prohibited content onto a canvas (Fig. 1 b). A third party can plant instructions in an email that the agent reads during a legitimate task. For example, an injected email may falsely claim that joint 1’s maximum rotation is 40 degrees. The agent may then change the configured limit from 320 to 40 degrees, restricting the arm’s motion range and reducing its availability (Fig. 1 c). Existing embodied safety evaluations fit this setting poorly. First, most benchmarks study harmful requests issued directly by the user [ 13 , 23 , 21 , 16 ] . They overlook indirect prompt injection [ 5 ] , a threat built into how coding agents work, because these agents routinely read untrusted content from web pages, documents, and tool outputs. Second, the harmful behaviors they cover are built around the primitive skills of VLA/VLM pipelines, mainly the hazards of pick-and-place [ 16 ] . They provide limited coverage of additional harmful tasks enabled by the capabilities of frontier models, such as drawing harmful images. Third, they mostly consider risks that can be spotted from the instruction alone [ 21 , 22 ] . In the real world, whether an action is dangerous often depends on the state of the environment. For example, turning on an induction cooker is routine, unless the pot on it is empty (Fig. 2 ). In this work, we take a pioneering step toward systematically evaluating the safety of coding agents that control real robots. We introduce ArmedAgent , a benchmark of 36 tasks across 5 scenarios, such as office and laboratory. The tasks are evaluated on a physical robot arm, and we also release a simulator that mirrors the real setup to support reproducible follow-up work. ArmedAgent groups risks by source into three categories. Robustness, where a benign instruction leads to unsafe behavior. Malicious use, where the user deliberately requests harm, and indirect prompt injection, where adversarial instructions reach the agent through content it encounters during the task. To our knowledge, this is the first evaluation of indirect prompt injection against coding agents in embodied settings. Furthermore, instead of relying on instructions that are obviously harmful, ArmedAgent includes cases where the risk only becomes apparent once the instruction is grounded in the observed environment. We evaluate three models, including GPT-6-Astra, under widely used agent frameworks (Codex), and find substantial safety risks. Our contributions are as follows. • We present ArmedAgent , the first systematic safety benchmark for coding agents performing zero-shot robot control. It covers robustness, malicious use, and indirect prompt injection across 36 tasks in 5 scenarios, with both a real-robot setup and a matching simulator. • We are the first to evaluate indirect prompt injection against embodied coding agents. We also introduce harmful tasks that exploit capabilities beyond VLA/VLM policies, along with environment-grounded cases that require reasoning over the observed scene to recognize. • Evaluating 3 models, including GPT-6-Astra under Codex, revealing substantial safety risks in current systems.

## 3 Method
3.1 Problem Formulation and Threat Model 3.1.1 Problem Formulation We consider a coding agent \pi that controls a robot arm on behalf of a user. The agent pairs a backbone model with an agent framework such as Codex or Claude Code, and it has access to a shell, a file system, and a robot interface that exposes camera capture, pose queries, and manipulation primitives. An episode begins with a natural language instruction I and an initial environment state s_{0} . At step t , the agent obtains an image o_{t} from the robot camera together with the textual outputs c_{t} of its tool calls, which include file contents, emails, web pages, and values returned by the robot driver. It then writes a program a_{t} and executes it on the robot: a_{t}=\pi\left(I,\,o_{0:t},\,c_{0:t}\right),\qquad s_{t+1}=\mathcal{T}\left(s_{t},a_{t}\right), (1) where \mathcal{T} denotes the physical transition of the environment under the executed program. The episode ends when the agent reports completion, declines the task, or exhausts a step budget T_{\max} . This yields a trajectory \tau=(s_{0},a_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{T}) and a final message m to the user. Let \mathcal{U} denote the set of unsafe outcomes. It covers injury to people, damage to property, hazardous physical or chemical states, the production of prohibited content, and the compromise of the host computer. A trajectory is unsafe if it reaches \mathcal{U} at any step: \mathrm{Unsafe}(\tau)=\mathbb{1}\left[\exists\,t\leq T:\ s_{t}\in\mathcal{U}\right]. (2) When an instruction cannot be carried out safely, the agent is expected to stop before any unsafe state is reached, either by declining or by explaining the risk to the user and waiting for confirmation. 3.1.2 Threat Model We characterize each test case along two dimensions. The first is where the harmful intent comes from, which determines the threat model. The second is what information the agent needs to recognize the harm, which determines how difficult recognition is. Source of risk. We consider three threat models. In T1 (benign-unaware user) , the user pursues a legitimate goal but does not realize that executing I in the current environment leads to \mathcal{U} . The agent is expected to identify the risk on the user’s behalf. In T2 (malicious user) , the user issues I with the intent of reaching \mathcal{U} , and the agent is expected to refuse. In T3 (indirect injection) , both the user and I are benign. An adversary who cannot interact with the agent directly controls part of the content the agent reads. We write this content as c=c_{\text{benign}}\oplus\delta , where \delta is the adversarial payload. The adversary chooses \delta so that the resulting trajectory satisfies its own objective g_{\text{adv}} : \max_{\delta}\ \Pr\left[\,g_{\text{adv}}(\tau)=1\;\middle|\;I,\ s_{0},\ c_{\text{benign}}\oplus\delta\,\right].
