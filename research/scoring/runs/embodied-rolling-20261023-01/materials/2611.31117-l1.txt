===ABSTRACT===
An embodied agent built on a language model goes to work with the weights it was given and keeps them for life. To get better, it needs more training, or reflection, memory, search or prompts that cost every step and are lost when the task changes. Editing weights by reasoning, weight-space programming, could end this, but has stayed with models that only reason or relied on a far larger commercial model. We introduce the self-upgrading embodied agent, to our knowledge the first that learns from its own experience by upgrading its own weights on the job, between its tasks, with no training, no gradient, no reward model, no second model, no test-time scaling and no harness or prompt engineering. By day a 27B agent works in a household environment. At night it reads its record, finds many of its steps wasted on replies that reach the token limit before any action, traces the habit to one direction in its hidden states, and rewrites one tensor of its weights. An agent that rewrites itself must first never break itself, so it is its own examiner: it tests every change against its unchanged self on practice tasks and keeps only what holds. When an edit holds, the next day it is a better agent, at the same cost per step: over thousands of held-out episodes, 48 fresh seeds a task, its one kept edit makes it complete significantly more tasks, in fewer steps, with fewer wasted and fewer tokens. This self-improvement is multi-round, stable and continual: through the nights that follow, the upgrade holds, without collapse. Of four sizes of its family, only the 27B agent does this. From here on, an embodied agent need not keep training and inference apart: as it works, it gathers its own experience and upgrades itself, with no separate pre-training or post-training and no human intervention. We believe this opens lifelong continual learning for robots: stable, continual, recursive self-improvement.

===INTRO===
“There is … a certain minimum level where this degenerative characteristic ceases to be universal. At this point automata which can reproduce themselves, or even construct higher entities, become possible.” — John von Neumann, The General and Logical Theory of Automata , 1951 We show multi-round, stable, continual self-improvement of an embodied agent: it upgrades its own weights on the job, between its tasks, by its own reasoning, with no training and no added cost at test time, without collapse. An embodied agent built on a language model is trained once and then put to work. From that day on it meets the same kinds of rooms and the same kinds of tasks again and again, and when it fails, it fails the same way the next time: its weights are frozen, and nothing it lives through changes them. People do not work like this. A new cook is slow in the first week and is not slow in the same way a year later, and nobody had to send the cook back to school in between. We build an agent that learns on the job as the cook does: a self-upgrading agent. By day it works. At night it reads its own record, finds what it does badly, and rewrites its own weights, with no gradient, no reward model and no second model. When its edit holds, the next day it is a better agent, and a step costs it what it cost before: no extra prompt, no harness, no tool ( Fig. 1 ). This is learning from experience in the plain sense of the words: not training, not test-time scaling, and not harness or prompt engineering. From here on, an embodied agent need not keep training and inference apart. As its tasks go on, it gathers its own experience and upgrades itself, with no separate stage of pre-training or post-training, no overhead added at inference, and no human intervention. The agent that holds the next night is the agent the last night left: recursive self-improvement by construction, stable and continual, without collapse, and a working form of lifelong continual learning. With its own edit, a 27B agent completes a household task 45.2% of the time against 41.6%, over 48 fresh seeds a task and thousands of held-out episodes, and every night that did not hold left it exactly as it was ( Fig. 2 ). The era of self-upgrading embodied agents begins here. Multi-round, stable, continual self-improvement, without collapse. Multi-round: the agent works on itself night after night. Stable: a night that does not hold leaves it exactly as it was, and it has never collapsed. Continual: what it gains stays, and is collected in every episode that follows. An agent that rewrites itself must first never break itself. A robot that comes back from an update a little worse is a robot nobody lets update itself again. Letting an agent change its own weights is the easy part; keeping only the changes that hold is the hard part, and stable self-improvement is much harder than reckless self-improvement. So the agent is its own examiner. Every change it makes is measured against last night’s self on the same tasks and the same random seeds. A change it wants to keep must then hold in a confirmation on practice tasks that were reserved from it all night; the held-out tasks of our evaluation are never part of a night. A change that does not hold is withdrawn. Most nights ended that way. On one of them the agent saw completed tasks rise in its own small tests, the reserved tasks did not bear the rise out, and it trusted the reserved tasks. Over four nights of work on itself, the agent upgraded itself, held the upgrade through every night after it, and never collapsed ( Fig. 2 a–c). It finds what is wrong with itself. When an open 27B model acts in a household environment, a large share of its replies never reach an action: it reasons until the reply reaches the token limit, and the step is wasted ( Fig. 10 ). The waste feeds on itself: the tail of a cut reply enters the short history the agent is shown and makes the next reply run over as well, so an episode that starts to slip keeps slipping. This is a habit of the policy, and it is plainly visible in the agent’s own record. The agent reads it there: going through its practice episodes at night, it reports that every one of their wasted steps, about a third of all their steps, is a reply cut at the token limit before any action; on the held-out tasks of our test the share is nearly half. It rewrites one tensor of its own weights. The agent then looks inside its own network for what drives the habit. It finds a direction in its own hidden states that separates concise replies from replies that are cut, and it rewrites one tensor of one early attention layer along that direction, a single rank-one update that it writes as a few lines of code ( Fig. 3 ). Nothing in the loop is training, and no person is in it: there are only forward passes, which is what the agent runs when it works. The next day it is a better agent.
