# RoboScope Kit: Unified Interfaces for Fast and Scalable Embodied-AI Evaluation

## Abstract
Simulation benchmarks are the standard way to compare robot policies, but each benchmark brings its own evaluator, observation layout, and action convention, and evaluation is slow: 500 RoboTwin Easy episodes with one simulator and one model on one GPU take 359.7 minutes. Profiling the official StarVLA evaluator shows that simulation occupies CPU cores while the GPU averages 14.3% utilization. RoboScope Kit wraps each model once in a Python class called a Policy, and each benchmark once in an adapter around the benchmark's own evaluator, so the benchmark's rules and scoring stay unchanged. Scheduling runs simulator workers and Policy replicas as separate pools, sizes both from measured probes, and shards work deterministically across GPU instances; runtime optimization renders only the observations an action-chunk Policy reads. Adapters cover six benchmark families. On LIBERO-Long with a StarVLA checkpoint on one 48 GiB RTX 4090, the concurrency search selects twelve simulator workers sharing three Policy replicas, which evaluate 500 episodes 4.45$\times$ faster than the official StarVLA evaluation with equivalent success rates, 2.78$\times$ faster than RLinf, and 1.52$\times$ faster than vla-eval at its tuned configuration. With CUDA MPS, which lets the replicas run their kernels concurrently, the search selects sixteen workers sharing four replicas, 6.16$\times$ faster than StarVLA, 3.85$\times$ faster than RLinf, and 2.10$\times$ faster than vla-eval. On RoboTwin Easy, four GPUs with chunk-boundary observation complete the 500 episodes in 14.4 minutes, 25.04$\times$ faster than the single-GPU baseline, with identical success outcomes and terminal steps.

## 1 Introduction
Simulation benchmarks evaluate complementary aspects of robot behavior: knowledge transfer in LIBERO ( Liu et al., 2023 ) , bimanual manipulation in RoboTwin ( Chen et al., 2025 ) , language-conditioned task sequences in CALVIN ( Mees et al., 2022 ) , and household manipulation in RoboCasa ( Nasiriany et al., 2024 ) . Each benchmark brings its own evaluator, observation layout, action convention, and episode bookkeeping, and each policy family brings its own preprocessing and inference loop. Connecting several policies to several benchmarks repeats this integration for every pair. Execution cost compounds the problem: with a StarVLA checkpoint, the official evaluation of 500 LIBERO-Long episodes takes about 50 minutes on one GPU, and running 500 RoboTwin Easy episodes with one simulator and one model on one GPU takes 359.7 minutes. Comparing checkpoints, seeds, or training stages multiplies these costs, and a shared evaluation layer can remove much of this repeated work. Profiling the official evaluators shows where this time goes (Section 2.2 ). Simulation occupies CPU cores while one loaded model leaves the GPU mostly idle, so simulators and models are best scaled separately. On RoboTwin, an action-chunk Policy reads one of every 16 rendered observations, so the others need not be rendered. RoboScope Kit addresses the repeated integration with one interface and the execution cost with two acceleration mechanisms, scheduling and runtime optimization. RoboScope Kit wraps each model once as a Policy , a class that loads the checkpoint and keeps the model’s own preprocessing, and each benchmark once as an adapter around the benchmark’s own evaluator. The Policy also declares what RoboScope Kit may rely on: the observations it reads, the chunk length it returns, and how often it needs a new observation. It keeps each episode’s state separate, so one loaded model can serve many simulators at once. Simulator workers, Policy replicas, scheduling, and result collection run as separate components, so the number of simulators K and the number of loaded models g vary independently. The concurrency search chooses K and g for each workload and host from short probes. Runtime optimization in the Simulation Runtime removes simulation work that the Policy never consumes, such as rendering observations between action-chunk boundaries. Beyond one host, RoboScope Kit partitions the work items deterministically across independent GPU instances, each running its own workers and replicas, and merges their results. This report makes three contributions: 1. A measurement of where evaluation time goes. On one RTX 4090, the official StarVLA LIBERO-Long evaluator spends 55% of its time on simulation and per-step handling on CPU cores while the GPU averages 14.3% utilization. Adding simulators to one model saturates it at 45.7% utilization, and a second Policy replica raises utilization to 87.7%. On RoboTwin, the official evaluator renders three cameras at every control step, yet the StarVLA Policy reads one observation every 16 steps. 2. A shared evaluation interface. A model is wrapped once as a Policy class and then runs on any benchmark that provides the inputs it needs, without changes to RoboScope Kit. Adapters cover six benchmark families. With a StarVLA checkpoint, a fixed RoboScope Kit configuration reproduces all 500 LIBERO-Long success outcomes of the official StarVLA evaluation. 3. Fast evaluation through scheduling and runtime optimization. Scheduling runs simulator workers and Policy replicas as separate pools, so simulators and loaded models scale independently and several simulators share each model; a time-bounded concurrency search sizes both pools for each workload and host, and deterministic sharding extends an evaluation across GPU instances. For runtime optimization, a Policy can declare that it needs a new observation only once per action chunk; RoboScope Kit then renders once per chunk, and each worker reuses one SAPIEN engine and renderer across its episodes. On one RTX 4090, the configuration chosen by the concurrency search evaluates 500 LIBERO-Long episodes in 485 s, against 2,990 s for the official StarVLA evaluation ( 6.16 \times ), with equivalent outcomes, and is 3.85 \times faster than RLinf and 2.10 \times faster than vla-eval. On RoboTwin Easy, scheduling and runtime optimization together cut the time for 500 episodes from 359.7 minutes, with one simulator and one model on one GPU, to 14.4 minutes on four GPUs ( 25.04 \times ).

## 3 Design
3.1 Overview RoboScope Kit has four parts (Figure 1 ). A Policy is a class, installed in its model’s own environment, that loads a checkpoint and keeps the model’s preprocessing. A benchmark adapter wraps the benchmark’s native evaluator , which keeps task definitions, initial states, termination, and scoring (G2, faithful). Two runtimes connect the two. The Control Runtime schedules the evaluation. It expands a benchmark suite into a manifest , the ordered list of episode keys (task, episode, seed), and groups the keys into work items . It starts K simulator workers , processes that each own one simulator, and g Policy replicas , processes that each hold one loaded Policy, and collects one episode record per episode key. The Simulation Runtime runs inside each simulator worker: the native evaluator, instrumented by the adapter, steps the simulator, renders observations, and sends them to a Policy replica. Acceleration has two parts. Runtime optimization in the Simulation Runtime removes simulation work the Policy never consumes (§ 3.6 ). Scheduling in the Control Runtime separates simulation from inference, chooses K and g , and shards work across instances (§ 3.3 , § 3.4 , § 3.5 ). An evaluation proceeds in four stages. The Control Runtime first validates that the Policy’s declared capabilities match the benchmark’s contract. It then runs the concurrency search , short probes that choose K and g , unless they are fixed. Next it runs the manifest as work items across K workers that send observations to g replicas. Finally it aggregates completed items into benchmark scores and execution metrics. 3.2 Evaluation Interface Observation and action contracts. A model is integrated once as a Policy and a benchmark once as an adapter, and each is reused with every counterpart (G1, unified). Each benchmark specification names its observation features and its action. A feature has a canonical name such as images.head , state.joints , or language.instruction , a kind, a dtype, and an optional shape; the action has a name, a dimension, and a dtype. A Policy declares the features it requires, the suites it supports, the chunk lengths it returns, its action representation, and an observation stride per benchmark. Checkpoint-specific work, including image resizing and cropping, normalization, tokenization, and the transform from model outputs to benchmark actions, stays inside the Policy. Adapters apply returned actions in the benchmark’s native convention and execute action chunks one step at a time at the native control frequency. Policy lifecycle and episode state.
