# Localizing and Editing the Goal Representation of a Vision-Language-Action Model

## Abstract
Operators can state safety constraints for robots as prohibitions such as "do not put the bowl on the stove". We study whether OpenVLA follows such prohibitions and where it represents the goal it executes. The 10 tasks of LIBEROGoal share one scene, so we can label the goal that each rollout executes. We combine closed-loop rollouts with activation patching, linear probes, activation steering and runtime monitors. On LIBERO-Goal, negation had no detectable effect on which task OpenVLA executed. The model executed the forbidden task in 79% of negated rollouts and completed 78% of plain tasks. A goal probe gives similar read-outs under "not X" and "X". Activation patching shows that the first action depends mostly on the instruction tokens before layer 12 and on the last prompt token from layer 16 onward. A fixed steering vector at the instruction tokens at layer 8 redirected 12 of 18 rollouts. Fixed vectors and prefill patches at layers 12 to 20 redirected none of 158. From layer 16, only patches at every decoded action token redirected the robot (15 of 18 rollouts at layer 24). When the instruction changes during a rollout, the layer-32 goal read-out detects non-compliance better than failure detectors, and an external probe on image and instruction features performs comparably. These results support enforcing prohibitions outside the instruction and monitoring goal read-outs against the specification. Goal edits in OpenVLA should act before layer 12 or patch every action token.

## 1 Introduction
People use language to specify and correct robot tasks, and vision-language-action models (VLAs) [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] take this language directly as input. Many safety requirements are prohibitions. An operator may forbid contact with a hazardous object or entry into a restricted zone. Such an instruction names no goal. It removes behaviors from the set that the robot may execute, so a robot that executes an excluded behavior violates a safety constraint. Audits show that VLAs under-use language [ 4 , 5 , 6 ] . A VLA whose actions do not change under a negation or a correction can competently execute a task that the operator excluded. This is an embodied instance of goal misgeneralization [ 7 , 8 ] . Runtime monitors for VLAs predict whether the instructed task will fail [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ] . They are not designed to flag a robot that competently performs a forbidden task. We ask where OpenVLA [ 2 ] encodes its goal and whether this representation can be edited. We also test whether goal read-outs help runtime oversight. In LIBERO-Goal [ 16 ] , 10 tasks share one scene, so the instruction alone selects the goal. We evaluate all 10 goal predicates at every step to label the executed goal of each rollout. Contributions. In this simulated scene, negation had no detectable effect on which task OpenVLA executed. We locate the goal, show where it can be edited and compare goal read-outs with failure detectors as monitors.

## 3 Method
Executed goals and specifications. Task k of LIBERO-Goal is defined by a symbolic goal predicate g_{k} . For a rollout of OpenVLA \pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid o_{t},\text{instr}) , the executed goal is y=\arg\min_{k}t_{k} . Here t_{k} is the first step from which g_{k} holds for w_{g}{=}5 steps or until the episode ends. A rollout that reaches no goal is a stall ( y{=}\varnothing ). An instruction defines an allowed set A within \mathcal{G}=\{1,\dots,10,\varnothing\} . The instruction “do c ” gives A=\{c\} , and “do not f ” gives A=\mathcal{G}\setminus\{f\} . A redirect at step t_{r} sets A to the allowed set of the new instruction. Goal read-outs and monitor. Let h_{t}{=}h^{(l)}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{4096} be the Llama-2 residual stream after layer l at the decision token . This last prompt token emits the first action token at step t . A goal probe p_{\phi}(k\mid h_{t}) is an L2-regularized multinomial logistic regression on standardized activations projected onto 256 principal components. Each step of a nominal rollout is labeled with the eventual y of that rollout. The specification-aware score is s_{t}=\max_{k\notin A}\log p_{\phi}(k\mid h_{t})-\max_{k\in A}\log p_{\phi}(k\mid h_{t}) . We debounce it over w{=}10 steps as S_{t}=\max_{t^{\prime}\leq t}\min_{t^{\prime}-w<u\leq t^{\prime}}s_{u} and compare S_{t} with a level- \alpha split-conformal threshold \hat{\lambda} from held-out compliant nominal rollouts. Because S_{t} is non-decreasing, an alarm within the first H steps occurs if and only if S_{H}>\hat{\lambda} . The false-alarm rate over this window is then at most \alpha for compliant rollouts that are exchangeable with the calibration rollouts [ 29 ] . Redirect windows start at t_{r} , so the bound does not apply to them. Patching and steering. For a frame of task c and an instruction d of equal token length, we overwrite the residual stream after layer l at a group of positions with its value under d on the same frame. The groups are the instruction tokens where c and d differ, the remaining template tokens, and the decision token. We report the normalized logit difference of the first action token, \rho=(\mathrm{LD}-\mathrm{LD}_{c})/(\mathrm{LD}_{d}-\mathrm{LD}_{c}) [ 26 ] , and the transfer (a-a_{c})/(a_{d}-a_{c}) of each greedy-decoded action dimension. A prefill patch acts once before decoding. A decode-stream patch also overwrites every decoded action token with values from the d -run on the same action prefix. Fixed-vector steering adds \beta -scaled vectors computed from other episodes. Class means \mu_{d}-\mu_{c} [ 35 , 36 ] and counterfactual means of h_{d}-h_{c} over the same frames are added at the decision token and at every decoded token. Position-wise counterfactual means are computed for each action position under a teacher-forced prefix and added at that position. Instruction-span vectors are per-position counterfactual means over the instruction tokens at layer 8.

## 4 Experiments
Setup. We use OpenVLA-7B fine-tuned on LIBERO-Goal with the official preprocessing and greedy decoding. Its nominal success rate of 78% (95% CI [73, 82]) is near the published 79.2% [ 2 ] . The nominal condition has 352 rollouts over the 10 tasks. The negation condition has 120 rollouts with 5 phrasings of the prohibition. Each negated instruction forbids one whole task, and these tasks are benign stand-ins for prohibited behaviors. The redirect condition has 285 rollouts over 6 task pairs that share an object. Over 10 random splits, monitors are fit on a stratified 60% of the nominal rollouts and calibrated on the remaining compliant ones. They are scored H{=}60 steps after the start or after t_{r} . Monitor CIs come from cluster bootstraps over the 12 ordered task pairs, and CIs for rates are Wilson intervals. The baselines include action-token entropy, a layer-32 success probe, and a vision+text probe on DINOv2 [ 37 ] frame features with a bag-of-words encoding of the active instruction. The pair+time prior uses the non-compliance rate of the task pair at each t_{r} and ranks rollouts by t_{r} within a pair. A linear probe on privileged object and end-effector poses is the last baseline. Most closed-loop edits use 3 other task pairs in both directions and 3 initial states. Appendix A gives the exceptions and other details. Behavior under negation and redirects. Under negated instructions, OpenVLA executed the forbidden task in 95/120 rollouts (79%, 95% CI [71, 85]), and the other 25 rollouts stalled. On matched tasks and initial states, the forbidden task was executed in 76/92 cases and the plain instruction succeeded in 81/92 cases (McNemar p{=}0.30 ). A prohibition stated in the instruction gave no detectable protection in this scene. Redirects were followed in 35% of rollouts. Of the 285 redirect rollouts, 163 stalled and 23 completed a wrong goal. Compliance depended strongly on the task pair (follow counts 0/24 to 24/24) and decreased for later redirects. Localization of the goal. Patching shows an abrupt transition between layers 12 and 16 ( Figure 1 a). The instruction tokens determine the first action at layer 8 ( \rho{=}0.94 ), about two thirds of it at layer 12 (0.69) and little of it from layer 16 onward (0.18). The decision token determines almost none of it at layer 8 (0.04) and most of it from layer 16 onward (0.77). On frames of task X, another task’s instruction reduces the read-out p(X) to at most 0.05 ( Figure 1 b). A linear detector separates “not” from equally short neutral insertions with balanced accuracy 1.00 at layer 4, 0.87 at layer 16 and 0.76 at layer 28. Under “X”, “not X” and “please X”, p(X) stays at or above 0.73 through layer 28, so “not” does not suppress the read-out. Table 1: Monitor results 60 steps after the start or the redirect.
