# Embodied Agents Take Control: Minimal-Interface Zero-Shot Agents Rival Industrial-Scale Policies in Vision-and-Language Navigation

## Abstract
Autonomous embodied agents must sustain a long decision-making loop of perceiving, acting, verifying, and self-correcting. In trained end-to-end policies and hand-designed workflows, the interaction structure remains externally specified. We study how much of this loop a general-purpose model can direct through a minimal interface. We term this organization agentic embodied control: the reasoning model directly steers every action, keeping reasoning and control aligned. We evaluate fourteen frontier multimodal models as provided by their vendors, with monocular RGB and primitive actions across six navigation and embodied question-answering benchmarks. On 100-episode subsets, the best agents exceed published scores and approach or surpass our human tester. Who directs the loop matters: SmartWay and O2C-Nav using the same strong models achieve substantially lower success rates on R2R-CE. The lightweight mini-swe-agent harness performs comparably to vendor harnesses. Stronger models benefit from optional rather than imposed waypoints; adding SLAM raises R2R-CE success to 88%. Physical trials expose limitations in body awareness, execution feedback, and persistent spatial understanding.

## 1 Introduction
Figure 1 : Who directs the interaction loop. Left: policy, workflow, and agentic control (T: trained; ZS: zero-shot). Middle: our minimal interface. Right: our best agent, published trained policies, and human reference ( table 1 ). Consider a household robot expected to operate autonomously day after day. It must find the kitchen, pick up a cup, and recover from mistakes without human rescue. Navigation and manipulation require different low-level controllers, but share the high-level challenge of reasoning over extended interaction. We ultimately seek an Autonomous Embodied Agent that can operate over extended interaction without repeated human rescue while managing its state, errors, and computational resources. Embodied navigation systems differ in who directs the interaction loop ( figure 1 , left). With a trained end-to-end policy , an external rollout loop repeatedly supplies one or more observation frames and the task instruction to the model, executes the predicted action or action chunk, and collects the next observation. NaVid and StreamVLN learn navigation actions from video and language ( Zhang et al., 2024 ; Wei et al., 2025b ) , much as Diffusion Policy and \pi_{0} learn action policies for manipulation ( Chi et al., 2023 ; Black et al., 2024 ) . Industrial-scale models such as Qwen-RobotNav scale this policy-learning approach through large-scale trajectory supervision and vision–language co-training ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) . The policy determines the actions; the rollout loop repeatedly queries the policy and executes its output in a fixed observe–act cycle. Many zero-shot systems instead place a frozen model inside a hand-designed workflow . These workflows run within the same rollout loop, but expand a single policy call into a sequence of perception, memory, planning, and verification operations. MapGPT and DiscussNav build such navigation-specific procedures around model calls ( Chen et al., 2024 ; Long et al., 2024 ) . These operations are usually organized into a predefined graph of nodes and connections ( Zhou et al., 2026 ) . This gives the model more room to reason, while the workflow still prescribes how that reasoning proceeds, limiting its ability to handle situations outside the anticipated paths. Some dual-system models, such as ABot-N1 and InternVLA-N1, also follow predefined workflows, coupling a slow reasoning model to a trained action expert through a fixed handoff ( Gong et al., 2026 ; Wei et al., 2025a ) . With agentic control, the model chooses how the interaction proceeds. NavGPT ( Zhou et al., 2024 ) provides an early example, implementing ReAct ( Yao et al., 2023 ) through a LangChain agent loop. More recently, AgenticNav ( Li et al., 2026a ) adopts a tool-calling harness. From the goal and accumulated feedback, it decides whether to observe again, act, check a result, revise its plan, or stop. The harness executes calls and returns their results, while the sequence of task operations is determined by the model at runtime. For trained end-to-end policies and hand-designed workflows, much of the interaction structure remains externally specified. This organization deserves reconsideration as frontier vision–language models improve in multi-turn, multimodal reasoning and spatial understanding. These capabilities raise the possibility that the model can decide not only what action to take, but also when to gather more information, check progress, or revise its approach. How much of embodied interaction can the model direct through a minimal interface? We study this question through embodied navigation and question answering. We give thirteen frontier multimodal models, as provided by their vendors, a minimal interface—a monocular RGB view and four discrete actions—and let each model decide when to observe, act, recover, and stop. We call this organization agentic embodied control . We evaluate this setup across six benchmarks spanning instruction-following navigation, object-goal navigation, and embodied question answering. This minimal setup yields strong performance across six benchmarks. On the evaluated R2R-CE subset, the best agents achieve 82–83% success, outperforming earlier zero-shot workflows on the same episodes. Model choice matters more than harness complexity: the lightweight mini-SWE-agent harness performs comparably to vendor harnesses, while earlier workflows using the same top-performing models achieve substantially lower success rates. Beyond this minimal interface, tools help when the model can choose how to use them. Offering a trained waypoint predictor and SLAM tools alongside primitive actions improves the strongest agent, whereas imposing the predictor as its action interface does not. Yet navigation failures, latency, context growth, and difficulties in physical deployment still limit sustained autonomy. These results show both the scope and the current limits of model-directed embodied control.
