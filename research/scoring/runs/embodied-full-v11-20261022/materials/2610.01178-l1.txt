# Recova: Agent-Guided Failure Recovery for Autonomous Robotic Manipulation

## Abstract
Manipulation failures can leave scenes in states from which a task policy cannot recover. Learning corrective behaviors requires scalable failure exploration and physical grounding. We present Recova, an agent-guided framework that jointly develops task execution and recovery in a reconstructed digital twin, then verifies and refines both through real-world experience. In the twin, the agent diagnoses failures, tests corrective programs, and collects successful task and recovery rollouts for separate policies. During deployment, it monitors progress, invokes a learned or programmatic recovery, verifies scene restoration, and resumes execution. When no suitable recovery is available, a human demonstration resolves the failure and enters the learning loop, allowing the system to expand its recovery capabilities. Physical rollouts and human demonstrations are routed to the corresponding policy for DAgger training. Across six LIBERO-Pro settings and four MolmoSpaces categories, Recova achieves 78.8% and 64.9% mean success, compared with 71.7% and 38.0% for the strongest baselines. With parallel collection across four real-robot workstations, DAgger fine-tuning raises mean success from 23.8% to 77.5%, and recovery skills further raise it to 87.5%. Over four collection rounds on one task, observed human intervention falls from 87.5% to 0%. Together, these results show how agent-guided recovery turns failures into reusable capabilities, improving robustness while progressively reducing human intervention. ## 1 Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models enable robots to perform diverse manipulation tasks from language instructions ( 6 ; 26 ; 48 ) , from folding laundry and assembling boxes to cleaning unfamiliar homes ( 4 ; 3 ) . However, even a small mistake can halt an otherwise capable policy. A tipped cup must be set upright before pouring can continue; a pencil caught across a box rim must be moved before the lid can close. In both cases, the robot must restore a workable scene before resuming the task. Most VLA training data, however, contain successful trajectories from standard initial states and provide little guidance for restoring a failed scene. Failures create a long tail of unusual configurations, and correcting them may require undoing a previous action, repositioning an object, or temporarily moving away from the nominal task goal. Consequently, policies may struggle when objects appear in unfamiliar arrangements ( 70 ) or when asked to continue after a failed attempt ( 39 ) . Simply retrying the task policy often leaves the underlying obstruction unresolved. Robots therefore need a complementary recovery capability that can expand as new failure modes arise. Learning such recovery behaviors directly on hardware is costly, as exploring failures requires frequent resets and risks damaging objects ( 58 ; 29 ) . Reconstructed digital twins provide a safer and more scalable setting for reproducing failures and testing corrections ( 58 ) , while physical experience remains essential for verifying and refining them under real contacts and observations. Effective recovery therefore requires a loop that discovers behaviors in simulation, validates them on the robot, and feeds physical experience back into learning. Language-model agents are natural candidates to coordinate this loop. Recent models combine multimodal understanding with the ability to use tools, execute code, and revise solutions from feedback ( 2 ; 51 ) . Prior robotics systems show that agents can interpret failures and develop reusable programs ( 40 ; 31 ; 41 ) . The central challenge is to connect these abilities into a complete recovery process: diagnose a failure, select or develop a correction, verify that it worked, retain successful solutions, and use the resulting data to improve the appropriate policy. The agent must also decide whether new experience should become a reusable program, a task-policy demonstration, or a recovery-policy demonstration. When no suitable recovery is available, human demonstrations enter the loop to resolve the failure and expand future recovery capabilities ( 35 ) . We present Recova ( Fig. 1 ), an agent-guided framework that develops task execution and scene recovery in a reconstructed digital twin, then continually improves both through real-world experience. In the twin, the agent explores task execution, diagnoses failures, and develops corrective behaviors. Successful task and recovery rollouts initialize separate task and recovery policies, while programs that compose perception and motion primitives capture reusable recovery strategies. Keeping these data pathways separate allows the task and recovery policies to specialize in their distinct roles. During real-world deployment, the agent monitors task progress, invokes an applicable learned or programmatic recovery, verifies that the scene is workable again, and resumes execution. When no suitable recovery succeeds, it requests human assistance. Successful task rollouts and human task demonstrations improve the task policy, while human recovery demonstrations improve the recovery policy. A parallel collection harness runs this loop across four robot workstations, allowing task and recovery experience to accumulate concurrently. Newly encountered failure modes also guide the development of additional skills. Human intervention therefore resolves the current failure while helping the robot recover more autonomously in the future. Our contributions are: • An agent-guided real-to-sim-to-real framework for jointly developing task execution and failure recovery. It uses a reconstructed digital twin to safely discover corrective behaviors, then verifies and refines them through physical robot experience. • A parallel deployment-and-learning loop that coordinates task and recovery policies, reusable recovery programs, outcome verification, and human demonstrations. Across four concurrently operating robot workstations, collected experience improves mean task success by 53.7 percentage points to 77.5%. On one task, the observed human-intervention rate falls from 87.5% in the first collection round to 0% in the fourth. • A comprehensive evaluation on two simulation benchmarks and four real-robot tasks. Recova exceeds the strongest baseline in our comparison by 7.1 percentage points on LIBERO-Pro and 26.9 points on MolmoSpaces. Recovery skills provide a further 10-point gain on the real-robot tasks, reaching 87.5% mean success.
