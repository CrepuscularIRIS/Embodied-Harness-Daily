# CovNav: Conformal Prediction-Guided Adaptive Inference for Object Goal Navigation

## Abstract
In object goal navigation (ObjectNav), an embodied agent must find a target object (e.g., "find a bed") in a previously unseen environment, relying on spatial commonsense such as object co-occurrence. Large Language Models (LLMs) encode this commonsense, but deploying them on robots exposes a trade-off between reasoning quality and efficiency: cloud-based models incur latency and privacy risks, whereas small on-device LLMs are fast but prone to hallucinated spatial judgments. Hybrid reasoning LLMs offer both a fast non-thinking mode and a slow thinking mode, which raises a key question for embodied agents: when is slow thinking worth its cost? We propose CovNav, a training-free framework that answers this question with Conformal Prediction (CP), turning dual-process (System 1 / System 2) inference into an uncertainty-aware decision. Much like recent decision models such as Jev, CovNav casts each spatial query as a structured binary decision rather than free-form generation: a local LLM in fast mode judges whether the target is likely near an observed object, CP converts its token probabilities into a prediction set, and only ambiguous queries are escalated to slow chain-of-thought reasoning. The object-pair formulation keeps calibration tractable and supports open-set objects through off-the-shelf detectors. Experiments on Gibson and HM3D show that CovNav surpasses the strongest zero-shot baseline by 0.8 and 1.7 percentage points in success rate on HM3D v1 and v2, while invoking slow reasoning for only 12.4% of queries and retaining most of the gain of always-on thinking at a fraction of its latency. Real-robot experiments on a TurtleBot 4 further validate the approach. CovNav shows how calibrated uncertainty can decide when an embodied agent should think slowly, offering a principled form of adaptive test-time reasoning for LLM-based robots.



## I Introduction
Fig. 1: We propose a Conformal Prediction (CP)-guided fast-slow inference paradigm for object goal navigation using Qwen 3 - 8 B as the local LLM. CP monitors the local LLM’s output confidence to adaptively switch Qwen between its fast direct inference mode and slow deliberative reasoning mode. Clear Case: When observing a bathtub, Qwen confidently predicts a toilet will be nearby (logits: 0.9 vs 0.1), so CP accepts the fast direct inference. Ambiguous Case: When observing a sofa and querying about a bed, Qwen shows low confidence (logits: 0.6 vs 0.4), so CP triggers its slow deliberative reasoning mode for more reliable spatial analysis. Recent advances in large foundation models have achieved remarkable success across language understanding, visual reasoning, and knowledge-intensive tasks. A pivotal next step for artificial intelligence is to ground these capabilities in the physical world, building embodied agents that can perceive, reason, and act within complex, unstructured environments [ 1 ] . Among the core competencies required for such agents, the ability to autonomously navigate to semantically specified targets is both fundamental and practically significant, underpinning applications from domestic assistance to search and rescue. Object goal navigation (ObjectNav) serves as a representative testbed for this capability. Given a semantic target (e.g., “find a bed”), an agent must explore and navigate through a previously unseen environment without any prior map [ 2 , 3 ] . A key enabler for efficient exploration is commonsense spatial knowledge, that is, the understanding that functionally related objects tend to co-locate within the same room (e.g., beds and nightstands in bedrooms, sofas and televisions in living rooms). Large Language Models (LLMs), which encode vast commonsense knowledge from pre-training, have recently emerged as a powerful tool for providing such spatial priors without task-specific training [ 4 , 5 , 6 ] , motivating a growing body of work on LLM-based object goal navigation. Existing LLM-based navigation approaches face significant deployment challenges. First, many state-of-the-art methods rely on cloud-based APIs (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude) [ 5 , 7 ] , which introduces network latency, raises privacy concerns, and limits deployment in offline or network-constrained scenarios. Second, directly deploying smaller open-source LLMs on resource-constrained robot platforms often leads to performance degradation, manifesting as hallucination and unreliable spatial judgments when processing complex relationships. Third, existing approaches lack adaptive mechanisms to dynamically adjust inference depth based on problem difficulty, resulting in either consistently invoking large models (incurring high computational costs) or relying exclusively on small models (sacrificing accuracy). This raises a critical question: How can we achieve both efficient and accurate spatial reasoning on resource-constrained robotic platforms? To address this challenge, we propose an adaptive inference framework that dynamically adjusts inference depth based on problem difficulty, utilizing LLM prediction uncertainty as a core metric. A key insight driving our work is that not all spatial reasoning problems require deep deliberation, as illustrated in Figure 1 . For instance, judging the spatial proximity between a television and a sofa is straightforward, given their typical co-occurrence in living rooms. However, determining the relationship between a sofa and a bed requires cross-room reasoning to recognize they belong to distinct functional spaces. Crucially, the output confidence produced by small LLMs, specifically the logits generated during deployment, serves as an effective indicator of problem difficulty, reflecting how well the input aligns with the training corpus. We leverage Conformal Prediction (CP) [ 8 ] , a statistically rigorous uncertainty quantification framework, to assess this confidence without requiring additional model training. CP constructs prediction sets based on model outputs: when the model is highly confident, the prediction set contains a single answer, indicating that the fast direct inference mode is sufficient. Conversely, when uncertain, the prediction set contains multiple possible answers, indicating the model’s inability to identify a unique solution, which triggers a slow deliberative reasoning mode. This mechanism mirrors human cognitive processing: System 1 thinking (fast, intuitive) handles routine decisions, while System 2 thinking (slow, analytical) engages for complex problems [ 9 ] . This perspective also connects CovNav to two recent trends in LLM reasoning. First, hybrid reasoning models such as Qwen3 [ 10 ] expose both a fast non-thinking mode and a slow thinking mode, and recent work trains such models with reinforcement learning to decide when to think [ 11 , 12 ] ; CovNav makes this decision without any training, using a statistically calibrated criterion. Second, decision models such as Jev [ 13 , 14 ] answer queries with structured choices, binary judgments, or scores instead of free-form text, trading open-ended generation for speed and reliability. CovNav’s fast mode follows the same principle by casting each spatial query as a binary object-pair decision with a confidence score, and adds a CP gate that escalates to System 2 reasoning only when this System 1 decision is uncertain. Building on this insight, we design a CP-guided navigation framework (CovNav) where the robot constructs an object-wise semantic map using off-the-shelf detection models. For each newly observed object, it queries the LLM to assess spatial relationships with the target object. CP analyzes the response confidence to determine whether the fast direct inference mode can reliably answer the question. If confidence is sufficient, the system directly utilizes the response; otherwise, it activates the slow deliberative reasoning mode for more thorough analysis. The resulting semantic value map, weighted by these assessments, guides the robot toward high-potential exploration regions. Two key design choices facilitate this framework: (1) formulating queries as object-pair relationships rather than open-ended visual question answering makes the problem space more tractable for CP calibration, and (2) leveraging open-source detection models enables open-set semantic understanding without task-specific training. The main contributions of this work are as follows: • We propose a novel fast-slow inference paradigm for robotic navigation that adaptively switches between fast direct inference and slow deliberative reasoning based on LLM response uncertainty, effectively achieving a balance between computational efficiency and decision accuracy on resource-constrained platforms. • We introduce the first use of Conformal Prediction to gate between the fast and slow reasoning modes of an LLM for object navigation, providing a theoretically grounded and training-free switching mechanism. We further design an object-pair-based query formulation that makes CP calibration tractable while enabling open-set semantic understanding through off-the-shelf vision models. • We validate our approach through extensive simulated and real-world robot experiments, demonstrating significant improvements in both navigation efficiency and success rate compared to existing methods. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work in zero-shot object navigation, LLM-based navigation and uncertainty quantification in Embodied AI. Section III provides the problem formulation and details our proposed methodology, specifically the object-based semantic mapping and the CP-guided fast-slow switching mechanism. Section IV presents extensive experimental results, including simulation comparisons and ablation studies. Section V details real-world validations, demonstrating the effectiveness and efficiency of our approach in physical environments. Section VI concludes the paper and discusses future directions.
