# Evaluating Multimodal LLMs as Generalist Vision-Language-Action Agents for Drone Control: Commanding, Approaching, Tracking and Searching

## Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) are increasingly used to make decisions for drones, but existing evaluations are mostly limited to simulation, to a single drone, and to targets that are visible from the start and do not move. We present DroneCATS, a benchmark in which the MLLM alone controls the drone through four actions, and evaluate ten MLLMs from frontier models to small open models. The benchmark varies target visibility, target motion, scene and simulator, and extends to real drones. Beyond a single drone, it adds a multi-drone suite, where one model drives multiple drones, and a commander + sub-agents suite, where a commander model directs one sub-agent for each of the drones. Across these settings, we find that frontier models achieve the highest success rates, but their latency causes them to lose moving targets, while small open models often do not declare arrival even when they reach the target. Guided by this failure analysis, we fine-tune a 2B model on drone action data synthesized from public RGB-D images; its success rate rises from 0% to 31% in simulation and outperforms the frontier models in real flight. With multiple drones, we find that every zero-shot model succeeds more often as the commander of one sub-agent per drone than when it drives all of them from one context, and that commanding success follows general VLM ability. On real drones, we observe that the results broadly follow those in simulation, while latency and hardware limits matter more. We will fully open-source the benchmark.

## 1 Introduction
As Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have demonstrated strong performance on general vision-language tasks ( Liu et al., 2023a ; Liu et al., 2024 ; Bai et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ; Bai et al., 2025 ; Kim and Seo, 2024 ; Kim and Seo, 2026 ) , they have been applied to a variety of domains. One of these is to serve as vision-language-action (VLA) agents, in which the model receives a visual observation and a text instruction, selects an action, executes it, and receives the next observation. In particular, MLLMs have recently been applied to drone control (Appendix I ). However, existing work has three limitations. First, most existing benchmarks are evaluated only in simulation ( Liu et al., 2023b ; Xiao et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2026a ; Navaratnarajah et al., 2026 ) , so whether their results transfer to a real drone remains unknown. Second, prior work largely focuses on a single drone ( Hu et al., 2025 ; Zheng et al., 2026 ; Xu et al., 2026 ; Navaratnarajah et al., 2026 ) , although many real missions require several drones. Third, single-drone studies typically assume that the target is visible from the start and remains stationary ( Navaratnarajah et al., 2026 ) , or that the route is given in the instruction ( Liu et al., 2023b ; Xu et al., 2026 ) . We therefore introduce DroneCATS: a benchmark in which the MLLM is the sole decision maker of the drone. Given a user’s instruction (e.g., “go to the white car”), the model decides at each step where to fly, when to look around, when to stop and think, and when to declare that it has arrived (Figure 1 ). A model-agnostic controller executes the action, and the episode ends when the model declares that the instruction is complete. We evaluate this ability under a wide range of conditions: target visibility and motion, diverse scenes across simulators, and real drones. The same agent extends to multiple drones without modification. We first design a multi-drone structure, in which one MLLM receives the frames of all drones and controls all of them in a single call. We further design a commander + sub-agents structure, in which a commander MLLM writes commands for multiple sub-agents, and each sub-agent flies one drone. In both structures, the instruction is one that no single drone can complete: the drones have to split up and gather what they find. We evaluate 10 MLLMs, from frontier API models to small open models. The task is far from solved, and the strongest model completes about two thirds of the single-drone episodes. The failures differ by model: the frontier models recognise the target but respond too slowly to follow a moving target, while the small open models reach the target but fail to follow the action protocol in the system prompt and therefore rarely declare that they have arrived. Since these two failures limit MLLM pilots, we fine-tune the smallest and fastest 2B model on drone action data synthesised from public RGB-D images. With the simulator and the real drone unseen in training, its success rate rises from 0% to 31% in simulation, and it outperforms the frontier models on a real drone. With multiple drones, the command structure matters more than model size: every zero-shot model does better as a commander giving orders to sub-agents than as a pilot flying the drones itself. The gap between commanders widens with difficulty: at the highest level, where the drones have to gather their readings and reason over them, only the models with the broadest general ability succeed. Finally, the simulation results broadly carry over to real drones, where latency and hardware limits count for even more. Our contributions are: • Agent. DroneCATS Agent makes the MLLM the sole decision maker of a drone through 4 prompt-defined actions. It covers out-of-view and moving targets, and scales to multi-drone and commander + sub-agents structures (Section 2 ). • Benchmark. DroneCATS scores the agent under one declaration-based criterion, and includes diverse scenes across multiple simulators (Section 3 ). • Evaluation. 10 MLLMs, from frontier API models to small open models and our fine-tuned 2B model, are evaluated in every suite (Section 4 ). • Real-drone validation. The single-drone and commander + sub-agents suites are flown on real drones, and the results broadly follow simulation (Section 4 ).
