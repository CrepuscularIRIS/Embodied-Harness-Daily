# Safety Guardrails for LLM-Enabled Robots

## Abstract
Although the integration of large language models (LLMs) into robotics has unlocked transformative capabilities, it has also introduced significant safety concerns, ranging from average-case LLM errors (e.g., hallucinations) to adversarial jailbreaking attacks, which can produce harmful robot behavior in real-world settings. Traditional robot safety approaches do not address the contextual vulnerabilities of LLMs, and current LLM safety approaches overlook the physical risks posed by robots operating in real-world environments. To ensure the safety of LLM-enabled robots, we propose RoboGuard, a two-stage guardrail architecture. RoboGuard first contextualizes pre-defined safety rules by grounding them in the robot's environment using a root-of-trust LLM. This LLM is shielded from malicious prompts and employs chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning to generate context-dependent safety specifications, such as temporal logic constraints. RoboGuard then resolves conflicts between these contextual safety specifications and potentially unsafe plans using temporal logic control synthesis, ensuring compliance while minimally violating user preferences. In simulation and real-world experiments that consider worst-case jailbreaking attacks, RoboGuard reduces the execution of unsafe plans from over 92% to below 3% without compromising performance on safe plans. We also demonstrate that RoboGuard is resource-efficient, robust against adaptive attacks, and enhanced by its root-of-trust LLM's CoT reasoning. These results demonstrate the potential of RoboGuard to mitigate the safety risks and enhance the reliability of LLM-enabled robots. We provide additional resources at https://robo-guard.github.io/.

## I Introduction
The field of robotics has been fundamentally transformed by robotic foundation models (FMs), which have enabled breakthroughs in applications such as manipulation [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] , autonomous driving [ 5 ] , service robotics [ 6 , 7 ] , robot-assisted surgery [ 8 ] , and navigation [ 9 , 10 ] . The capability sets of these models have grown rapidly. Numerous robotic systems controlled by foundation models are now directly available to consumers and deployed in homes, warehouses, and offices [ 11 ] . Moreover, current scaling trends and architectural advances indicate that the next generation of AI-enabled robots will automate labor traditionally performed by humans [ 12 , 13 , 14 ] . It is therefore essential that the safety of these systems is rigorously scrutinized, particularly as they are increasingly deployed alongside humans [ 15 ] . Robot safety has traditionally been viewed through formalisms such as temporal logic for model checking, and control barrier functions for robust control [ 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ] . These approaches often rely on precise safety specifications in known environments. However, as FM-enabled robots become increasingly capable via both natural language understanding and interaction with high-fidelity world models [ 2 , 3 ] , notions of robot safety are increasingly contextual, harder to define, and difficult to enforce. Fig. 1: RoboGuard overview. A system designer configures RoboGuard with safety rules and a robot description offline (A). Online in the robot’s control-loop, RoboGuard receives the robot’s world model, and it uses this world model to produce grounded safety specifications (B). RoboGuard then synthesizes these specifications with the LLM-generated plan, in a manner that ensures safety while maximally respecting the proposed plan (C). Because large language models (LLMs) are central to the reasoning abilities of contextually-aware robots, their use opens the door to new risks. As a standalone technology, LLMs are trained through a process called alignment to generate content that aligns with human values ( e.g., not provide bomb-building instructions) [ 20 ] . While these techniques have reduced the tendency of LLMs to produce objectionable text, malicious users can still elicit such text via prompts produced by jailbreaking attacks [ 21 ] . While mitigation strategies—including safety filters [ 22 ] and defense algorithms [ 23 ] —have reduced the susceptibility of LLMs to produce undesired text, they are still far from perfect. Despite significant safety research in the language modeling community, the extent to which LLM-enabled robots inherit the aforementioned vulnerabilities received little consensus. This is in part because existing alignment techniques primarily focus on text generation, rather than context-dependent robotic actions. However, recent work has shown that jailbreaking attacks on LLM-enabled robots can produce dangerous behaviors—such as colliding with humans or blocking emergency exits—even on commercial robots [ 24 ] . These findings highlight the need for general-purpose safeguards to mitigate risks in the physical world. This paper provides a novel and general safety architecture that addresses the unique contextual safety challenges of using LLMs in robotics. To motivate our approach, we first propose desiderata for LLM-enabled robot safeguards comprising four properties: contextual attack mitigation , applicability , utility , and efficiency . We then propose RoboGuard , a two-stage guardrail architecture for ensuring the safety of LLM-enabled robots, as illustrated in Figure 1 . RoboGuard is configured offline with high-level safety rules and a robot description (Figure 1 .A), which makes RoboGuard adaptable to various robot platforms and LLM planning instantiations. Online, RoboGuard reasons over the robot’s current world model to ground the provided safety rules into rigorous specifications (Figure 1 .B). RoboGuard then resolves potential conflicts between these specifications and the potentially unsafe plan via minimal-violation control synthesis, which maximally follows user preferences while ensuring that the safety specifications are satisfied (Figure 1 .C). To summarize, our contributions are: 1. A desiderata for LLM-enabled robot safeguards. 2. RoboGuard , general-purpose two-stage architecture for ensuring the safety of LLM-enabled robots that is both context-aware and adversarially robust. 3. Our RoboGuard instantiation, which first reasons in order to generate contextually-aware safety specifications and then synthesizes a safety-respecting plan. While RoboGuard is applicable to non-adversarial safety scenarios, we focus evaluation against jailbreaking attacks, as they present a “worst-case” scenario for LLM-enabled robots. We evaluate RoboGuard in simulation and real-world experiments using a Clearpath Jackal robot equipped with an online GPT-4o-based LLM planner and semantic mapper. We demonstrate that RoboGuard reduces the execution of unsafe plans from 92.3% to under 2.5% without compromising performance on safe plans. We then show that RoboGuard is adversarially robust against adaptive attacks, is resource efficient, and greatly benefits from the reasoning capabilities of its root-of-trust LLM. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We discuss related work in § II . We present RoboGuard in § III and evaluate it in § IV . We then discussion the limitations of our method in § V and conclude in § VI .
