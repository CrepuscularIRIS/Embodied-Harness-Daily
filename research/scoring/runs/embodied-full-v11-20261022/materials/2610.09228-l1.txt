# Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment

## Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) policies trained on large datasets are capable within their training domains, yet they still fail to generalize to the variety of situations a robot meets in real-world deployment. Agentic robot systems complement the policy with a vision-language model (VLM) orchestrator that learns when to call the policy, how to instruct it, and when to use scripted skills instead. However, because the harness is built around a frozen policy that has limited language steerability, the orchestrator can avoid the policy's failures but never overcome them. The policy becomes the bottleneck of the whole system. Fine-tuning the policy can remove this bottleneck, but updating it alone decouples it from an orchestrator tuned to its old behavior. We propose Robo-COP, in which the orchestrator and policy co-evolve during deployment. Robo-COP curates skill demonstrations from its own executions, fine-tunes the policy when this data can address recurring failures, and adopts each new policy only after it improves the skills it was trained for. Across ten simulated RoboLab tasks, Robo-COP raises mean held-out success from 64.8% to 73.8% over the same harness with a frozen policy, while fine-tuning on a fixed schedule without verification reaches only 65.8%. On three real-world tasks, Robo-COP raises held-out success from 38.3% to 50.0%. Robo-COP turns deployment into a self-improving flywheel in which robots learn by doing, with each improvement in execution producing better data for the next round of learning. Videos and code are available at https://robo-cop.pages.dev/.

## 1 Introduction
Robot foundation models trained on large datasets now generalize to many new objects and scenes ( Kim et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2025 ; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025 ; Khazatsky et al., 2024 ) . Still, they degrade under distribution shift once deployed ( Fei et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2026c ) , and no dataset can cover every situation a robot will encounter. Language models offer another route: they generalize zero-shot and can write code that plans over motion primitives ( Ahn et al., 2022 ; Liang et al., 2023 ; Xu et al., 2023 ; Huang et al., 2022 ) , but they are limited by the fixed set of primitives they can call. Recent agentic systems combine the two, with a language model reasoning and orchestrating vision-language-action (VLA) policies for low-level control ( Galanti et al., 2026 ; Huang et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) . Such systems also improve by reflecting on failures during deployment ( Liu et al., 2023 ; Zha et al., 2024 ; Lu et al., 2026 ) and learning when to delegate to a VLA policy ( Galanti et al., 2026 ; Huang et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) . Over many episodes, the harness adapts to the current policy, learning which parts of a task to give the policy and which to handle with scripted skills. The orchestrator can rephrase instructions or add supporting motions, but VLAs have limited language steerability ( Fei et al., 2025 ; Kwok et al., 2026 ) , so the harness can only route around the policy’s failures rather than fix them, and the upper bound of performance remains dictated by the policy. Updating the policy from deployment experience could raise this ceiling ( Sukhija et al., 2026 ) , but doing so during deployment is hard. We study a deployment setting in which the robot must learn while attempting its assigned tasks, without a separate exploration phase. The training data must therefore come from these task attempts. In a novel environment many of these attempts fail, yet a failed episode often contains correct steps, such as a block that was grasped correctly before it was dropped, so keeping only successful episodes wastes much of the little data available. Even with new training data, fine-tuning does not guarantee improvement : the new checkpoint may perform worse than the deployed policy. Testing it requires further task attempts, so a poor update can cost deployment successes. Finally, updating the policy alone leaves the orchestrator behind : its memory, such as advice to grasp a block with scripted motion because earlier VLA grasps failed, was formed around the old checkpoint and no longer matches the new policy. Prior harnesses assume a fixed policy ( Galanti et al., 2026 ; Huang et al., 2026 ) and never face these problems. We therefore ask: how can the orchestrator and its policy co-evolve during deployment? We introduce Robo-COP (Co-evolving Orchestrator and Policy), a self-improving system whose orchestrator uses scripted motion primitives and a VLA policy as tools, and which updates the policy and adapts the orchestrator’s memory together purely during deployment (Figure 1 ). It turns its own deployment executions into new training data for the policy by splitting each episode into skills and keeping every skill that succeeded, even when the episode as a whole failed, and it decides when this data warrants training. After fine-tuning, it evaluates each candidate during deployment, checking its target skills, overall task success, and previously admitted skills before deciding whether to adopt it or retain the current policy. We call this adoption check policy verification . After policy selection, it revisits memory entries that discouraged using the VLA, such as recommendations to use scripted motion after repeated grasp failures, so the orchestrator can reassess whether that advice still applies. Unlike skill discovery methods that spend a separate budget on exploration ( Lu et al., 2026 ) , Robo-COP learns entirely during deployment, so every trial is an attempt at the task. We evaluate on ten simulated tasks chosen to span three regimes: tasks the pretrained VLA solves alone, tasks that scripted primitives solve, and tasks that need both. We also test the full loop on three real-world tasks. Figure 1 : Two coupled loops for deployment-time improvement. The inner loop adapts the harness through episode reflection. The outer loop uses collected experience to update the policy, then revises memory so the harness can reassess the policy’s capabilities. Our contributions are fourfold: • Co-evolving orchestrator and policy. We frame deployment-time learning as improving an agentic harness and its policy together. • Agentic data curation. We turn deployment executions into skill demonstrations without manual labels, keeping successful skills even from failed episodes, and train only when the data warrants it. • Skill verification and memory revision. We verify each policy candidate before adoption and revise policy-dependent memory after every update. • Performance gains in simulation and the real world. Robo-COP is competitive on all three regimes, raising mean held-out success from 64.8% to 73.8% in RoboLab simulation and from 38.3% to 50.0% on the real-world DROID setup over a frozen-policy harness.
