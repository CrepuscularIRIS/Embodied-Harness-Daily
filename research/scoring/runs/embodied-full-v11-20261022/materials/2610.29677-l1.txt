# Toward Recursive Self-Improvement in Physical AI

## Abstract
Recursive self-improvement (RSI) describes a system that improves not only its behavior but also the process by which it improves. Recent digital agents test this idea by rewriting their own code and checking each version with executable tests. Bringing these mechanisms into the physical world requires learning from partial observations through costly trials, while accounting for changing operating conditions and potentially irreversible consequences. We position RSI within robot learning and trace pathways from improving robot capabilities to improving the processes that generate them. We examine how physical experience can strengthen these improvement mechanisms and when the resulting gains can support further self-improvement of the mechanisms themselves. Gains carry forward only when feedback addresses remaining learning needs, the receiving component can use the change, its value survives changes in tasks or hardware, and continued practice remains safe and affordable. Existing studies, concentrated in manipulation, support behavioral improvement, benefits to later learning, and procedure improvement. Some systems also change the improver's knowledge or tools, but the physical studies reviewed here have yet to isolate gains attributable to recursion. We synthesize these findings into evaluation principles and research directions for machines that, trial by trial, safely improve the way they improve.



## 1 Introduction
Recursive self-improvement (RSI) describes a system that improves the process by which it improves, not only its behavior. The idea goes back to I. J. Good’s speculation about machines that design better machines ( Good, 1966 ) and to self-referential learners such as the Gödel machine ( Schmidhuber, 2003 ) , and recent digital agents have begun to approach it ( Chen et al., 2026d ) . STOP ( Zelikman et al., 2024 ) applies a program that improves code to its own source, and the Darwin Gödel Machine ( Zhang et al., 2026c ) lets improved coding agents modify themselves; both judge each candidate with executable tests. Yet a system can get better at a task, and a fixed procedure can keep producing better versions of it, without anything recursive happening. RSI requires two further links (Section 2.2 ): what the system retains from earlier improvements must make its next revision better, and the improver , the component that revises the learning procedure, must in turn use what it has gained to help produce its own next improvement. Physical RSI asks the same question of systems that learn by acting in the physical world, such as robots. Digital RSI supplies the mechanisms (language models that write reward code or controllers, archives of earlier variants, and automated experiment loops); physical RSI tests how these mechanisms use physical experience and whether they hold under physical constraints (Figure 1 ). The physical setting changes the experimental conditions in four ways (Table 1 ). First, physical outcome checks depend on sensors and task-specific verifiers, people, or learned detectors whose reliability must be established; even an automated grasp-success check can miss what it does not sense, and an error in that judgment can be reinforced and then executed on hardware. Second, every candidate costs robot time, resets, supervision, and wear, so a revision must pay off within few trials. Third, practice changes its own conditions: objects move, grippers wear, calibration drifts, and rolling back software does not repair a damaged object. Fourth, what can be improved extends beyond prompts and code to controllers, dynamics models, and the interface to a particular body. Each difference bears on the central question of RSI, whether what one round of revision teaches the improver makes the next revision better. The failure can have several causes, and each calls for a change kept in a different place; these places organize Sections 3.1 and 3.2 . The robot’s policy , the learned controller that turns observations into motor commands, may never produce the recovery motion; an expert correction can teach it, as RECAP ( Physical Intelligence et al., 2026 ) learns from deployment experience and corrections (Section 3.1.1 ). The robot may mistake a light touch for a stable hold because its model of contact is wrong; a world model refitted to contact data can correct the prediction and give the policy a place to practice, as in PlayWorld ( Yin et al., 2026b ) (Section 3.1.2 ). The robot may have recovered from this failure before without keeping what worked; external knowledge , such as a saved recovery or program, can be called the next time, as Zetta ( Ding et al., 2026 ) grows code-based critics and recoveries around a frozen policy in simulation (Section 3.1.3 ). Finally, the robot may keep choosing the wrong kind of repair; then the learning procedure must change: which attempts to collect, how to judge them, and how to turn them into updates (Section 3.2 ). Eureka ( Ma et al., 2024a ) rewrites reward code from simulated training results, and ENPIRE ( Xiao et al., 2026b ) lets coding agents modify the training infrastructure from robot experiments. The first three routes improve what the robot does; the fourth improves how it learns, and it is where physical RSI would begin. Recursion would further require that knowledge of why earlier repairs worked, which we call repair knowledge , improves how the next procedure revision is made. Our central argument is that physical experience can improve task competence, the learning procedure, and the improver that revises this procedure, and that four recurring conditions, each sharpened by the physical setting, decide whether such gains carry forward. Each can fail: the new signal may not address what the learner still lacks, because sensing shows only part of a failure; the receiving component may be unable to absorb it on its body; its value may not survive later changes in behavior or hardware; or further practice may become unsafe or unaffordable. For the grasping robot, this could mean collecting more of the grasps it already makes well, refining a contact model that its controller cannot use, relying on a fix that stops working once the gripper is replaced, or practicing until the gripper wears out. A change can improve execution and still fail to help later learning: added autonomous data often yield modest gains that saturate and can even regress ( Mirchandani et al., 2025 ) . From learning actions to improving how learning happens. Several research traditions supply pieces of this progression. Self-supervised grasping showed that physical outcomes alone can supervise behavior at scale, first by learning from tens of thousands of real grasp attempts ( Pinto and Gupta, 2016 ) and then through large-scale real-robot reinforcement learning (RL) with QT-Opt ( Kalashnikov et al., 2018 ) . Interactive imitation learning, as in DAgger ( Ross et al., 2011 ) and HG-DAgger ( Kelly et al., 2019 ) , showed how an expert can correct the learner in the states that the learner itself reaches. Lifelong robot learning ( Thrun and Mitchell, 1995 ) asks how knowledge gained on earlier tasks can reduce the real-world experimentation needed for later ones; model-based RL, from Dyna ( Sutton, 1990 ) and PILCO ( Deisenroth and Rasmussen, 2011 ) to Dreamer ( Hafner et al., 2020 ) , asks how recorded transitions can support practice inside a learned model; and skill-based approaches, such as options ( Sutton et al., 1999 ) and the shared RoboEarth knowledge base ( Waibel et al., 2011 ) , ask how an extended behavior can be stored and called again. Each tradition widened what one learning episode can pass on to the next. Recent generalist and foundation-model-based systems widen both what can change and what role the change plays. RoboCat ( Bousmalis et al., 2024 ) turns a policy adapted to a new task into a source of training data for the next generalist, and SILVR ( Luo et al., 2026a ) revises a video-based planner using its own executions. In RoboCat, Eureka (in simulation), and ENPIRE, experience reaches the machinery that produces future updates (a data collector, a reward function, the training infrastructure) as well as the policy that receives them. This brings the physical problem close to the long-standing aspiration of learning to learn ( Thrun and Pratt, 1998 ) , and several reviewed systems already contain the components that a demonstration of recursion would need. The open question is how to realize that aspiration with limited trials, imperfect sensing, and a body that changes over time. Scope and related surveys. Our scope is the improvement of policies, world models, memories, skills, and the software procedures that update or use them, whenever their evidence or evaluation is connected to physical action. We discuss hardware change when it affects compatibility, maintenance, or continued experimentation. Autonomous morphology design and physical self-reconstruction are adjacent topics outside our scope. Recent surveys cover neighboring ground (Table 2 ). Some focus on agents and embodiment: recursive self-improvement in AI systems ( Chen et al., 2026d ) , self-evolving agents ( Gao et al., 2025 ) , weights versus executable skills as stores of improvement ( Jena et al., 2026 ) , self-evolving embodied AI ( Feng et al., 2026 ) and humanoids ( Nguyen et al., 2026 ) , and levels of physical recursive self-improvement ( Zhou et al., 2026a ) . Others analyze world models for robot learning ( Hou et al., 2026 ) and manipulation ( Wang et al., 2026a ) , joint world–action models ( Lu et al., 2026b ) , actionable simulators ( Chen and Zhu, 2026 ) , verifiers for robot policies ( Wan et al., 2026 ) , and physical AI broadly ( Xiang et al., 2025 ) . Our organizing question is different: after a change has been acquired, who uses it next, and what does that use improve? Component names alone cannot answer it, because two data collectors with similar task success can yield training data of very different value; a more accurate predictor can still produce less useful training data for a policy; and the same kind of retrieved memory can change either how a program is repaired or how an outcome is judged. First, it positions RSI within robot learning by separating method paradigms and problem settings (from reinforcement learning and meta-learning to AutoML) from a ladder of claims (behavioral improvement, later-learning benefit, procedure improvement, and the two links of recursion), each defined by the comparison that would establish it. It organizes physical self-improvement along that ladder according to where experience leaves its change and which decision of the learning procedure it revises, showing that data volume and component accuracy are unreliable proxies for progress. Second, it separates mechanisms that change an improver’s knowledge or tools from evidence that the improver has improved: existing studies support the first three claims, while recursive gains still need dedicated verification. It reviews digital precedents and physical boundary cases and specifies a two-round experiment that could isolate the recursive relation, together with its controls and an estimate of its hardware cost; Table 13 records where the reviewed studies stand on each claim. Third, it names the four breakdowns above as recurring conditions ( informative feedback , usability , robustness to distribution shift , and continued practice ) and shows how each fails and which capability would restore it. Four research questions organize the review. RQ1 What limits a robot’s next improvement? Is useful experience missing, or is progress limited by supervision, optimization, action generation, interfaces, or retention? RQ2 Which changes address that limitation? How does experience change behavior, reusable models or skills, and the collection, supervision, curriculum, or update procedures used in later learning? RQ3 When can improvements build on one another? When do improvements carry forward into better learning procedures and improvers, and what evidence would establish recursive self-improvement? RQ4 What makes self-improvement different in the physical world? How do sensing, embodiment, reset, safety, cost, and hardware changes constrain the process?
