# Where is Physical RSI capability from? An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy

## Abstract
In this paper, we systematically investigate the mechanisms, scaling behavior, and generalization of physical Recursive self-improvement. We introduce PhysRSI-eval to quantify physical RSI through a five-axis metric: First-Attempt Performance, Efficiency, Success, Budget and Improvement from failure. Different VLMs are evaluated on 18 manipulation tasks under a fixed interaction budget. The analysis of experimental results motivates the Physical Barrel Hypothesis: different capabilities may constrain different parts of improvement. The five-axis metrics also distinguish systems with close public benchmark averages and show that high scores need not come with early or efficient gains. We study observational scaling from two perspectives: predicting the relative performance of VLM agents from their public capabilities, and forecasting later progress from early physical feedback. Together, these analyses ask how quickly and how far an agent can improve. Moreover, we conduct workspace interventions and then analyze what improvement leaves behind. Removing history reduces late-round success, frozen workspaces remain useful on unseen layouts, and three cross-task cases achieve success earlier. The results suggest that scene-specific adaptation and reusable experience can coexist.

## 1 Introduction
Frontier LLM agents demonstrate remarkable capabilities in directly generating actions for robotic manipulation. Through iterative trial and error, they continually improve manipulation ability by updating skills, generating code, and using tools. [ 22 , 9 ] We call this process physical recursive self-improvement, or physical RSI. In this work, rather than further demonstrating the impressive capabilities of frontier LLM agents, we pose three more fundamental questions: What is the origin of physical RSI capabilities? What scaling laws govern this process? Do the resulting improvements reflect overfitting or generalization? Q1: Where is Physical RSI capability from? What allows an agent to turn physical feedback into a better policy? Perception helps it interpret outcomes, spatial reasoning relates observations to actions [ 31 ] , and coding and harness use allow it to implement and test revisions. These capabilities may play different roles. We ask whether strengths behind a good first attempt also explain improvement after feedback. We introduce PhysRSI-eval to study these questions under a common protocol ( Figure 2 ). Six systems revise persistent code, tools and notes across 18 manipulation tasks, with frozen model weights and a fixed physical interaction budget. Gemini 4 is reported separately on ten tasks. An exploratory comparison of four public capability profiles on the 18 tasks associates spatial reasoning most strongly with initial performance, and perception with attained performance and recovery ( Figure 4 ). This pattern motivates the Physical Barrel Hypothesis : the capability that limits an agent may depend on which aspect of improvement we measure. Starting well and learning from failure may require different strengths. These associations suggest candidate bottlenecks, not causal requirements. Q2: How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law? An endpoint score alone cannot quantify physical RSI. It says little about when gains occur or what they cost. Repeated attempts can also increase the chance of success without useful revision. Our five-axis metric separates initial performance, gain timing, attained score, physical budget savings and recovery from failure ( Section 3.4 ). These axes reveal different strengths. Opus 5.5 attains a higher score than GPT-6.1 Sol, but has lower gain-timing and budget-savings scores. Higher scores need not mean improving earlier or using fewer physical interactions. With these outcomes defined, we study observational scaling across model capabilities and physical interaction budgets. Following Ruan et al. [34] , we first ask whether public capability scores can predict the relative order of held-out systems, in attained performance and gain timing ( Section 5.2 ). We then ask whether early physical progress predicts the gains from a larger interaction budget. This connects physical RSI to the study of learning curves [ 19 , 12 ] , with the interaction round as our budget variable. Calibrating the improvement shape of a reference model to a few early rounds forecasts the rest of a related model’s trajectory ( Figure 8 ). The two analyses address complementary questions: what improvement to expect from a model’s capabilities, and how much further it may progress after its first attempts. Both remain exploratory. Q3: Does Physical RSI reflect overfitting or generalization? Does the agent acquire a reusable procedure, or adapt to its practice scene? A rising score alone cannot answer this question. We first remove history to test whether retained experience helps beyond repeated attempts. We then freeze workspaces on unseen layouts and transfer them across tasks. In a six-task Astra ablation, removing history lowers late-round success from 41.7% to 8.3% ( Table 8 ). With further revision disabled, frozen workspace files support success in 25 of 60 trials on unseen layouts ( Table 9 ). Three cross-task cases also reach first success earlier when initialized from other tasks’ workspaces ( Figure 9 ). These results support a role for retained experience and show that some artifacts remain useful beyond the scenes where they were refined. Scene-specific gains and useful transfer can coexist. Explaining which retained skills or memories enable transfer connects this question back to the origins of physical RSI. Our contributions follow this progression: • Five-axis evaluation and capability diagnosis. A fixed-budget protocol separates five physical RSI outcomes and relates them to four capability profiles, motivating the Physical Barrel Hypothesis. • Observational scaling and prediction. Cross-model rank prediction and early-round forecasting probe how model capabilities and interaction budget relate to physical improvement. • Specialization and generalization. Ablations, frozen-workspace tests and cross-task adaptation examine whether gains depend on retained experience and whether that experience remains useful elsewhere.
