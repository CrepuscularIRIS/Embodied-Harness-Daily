# SLoSa: Small, Low-Power, and Safe Foundation Models for Robotic Task Planning

## Abstract
Safe robotic task planning requires planners that can reliably achieve task goals while satisfying safety constraints. Classical planners search over explicit symbolic task representations and can provide formal guarantees with respect to encoded safety constraints, but require rigid input formats. Their computational cost also increases rapidly with the search space. In contrast, large language models (LLMs) offer flexible task interfaces and strong generalization capabilities, but they are large, power-hungry, and pre-trained with little robot-specific safety data and knowledge. Thus, they can be difficult to deploy locally on robotic systems with limited resources. To address these limitations, we present SLoSa, a neuro-symbolic compression and fine-tuning framework for training foundation-model-based robotic task planners with Small size, Low power demand, and Safety awareness. The framework first constructs a safety-aware dataset using classical planners, an advanced teacher LLM, and formal verifiers, followed by two training stages: supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning guided by a verification-based reward machine to enforce safety alignment. We evaluate SLoSa across four Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL) domains, two household simulation environments, local edge platforms, and a desktop robotic arm. Across the PDDL tasks, verifier-guided post-training raises the success rate of compact 4B-14B planners to 70.5-76.0%, while INT4 deployment on a Jetson Orin requires 17.85-26.41 W of average power. Results in household simulators and on the physical robot further demonstrate the applicability of SLoSa across diverse robotic task settings.



## I Introduction
Robotic task planning converts a high-level goal into an executable sequence of actions [ 1 ] . In safety-critical settings, reaching the goal is not enough: every intermediate state must also satisfy requirements such as ordering, mutual exclusion, and resource constraints [ 2 , 3 ] . A plan that eventually places an object correctly can still be unacceptable if it enters a restricted region, manipulates a fragile object in the wrong order, or violates a required inspection step. We therefore study safety-constrained task planning : generating plans that are executable and goal-reaching while satisfying explicit trajectory-level constraints. Classical planners offer a principled solution when a correct symbolic task specification is available. Systems such as Fast Downward and OPTIC search over states defined by action preconditions and effects [ 4 , 5 ] ; PDDL3 expresses trajectory constraints, and tools such as VAL check a candidate plan against the formal specification [ 6 , 7 ] . This formal structure provides an auditable safety criterion, but it comes with two practical costs. First, tasks in complicated environments can induce exponentially growing search spaces as the number of objects and planning horizon increase, making symbolic search difficult to scale [ 8 ] . Second, users must provide rigid symbolic inputs and carefully engineered domain specifications . Imitation and reinforcement learning can amortize planning into compact policies [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] . However, learned policies are commonly tied to particular task distributions, and their outputs do not inherit a formal guarantee without an explicit specification and verifier. LLM planners offer a complementary strength: they can interpret high-level task descriptions and directly generate candidate action sequences [ 13 , 14 ] . Recent systems ground language into symbolic specifications or integrate LLMs with planners and formal verifiers [ 15 , 16 , 17 ] . An inference-time verifier can reject an invalid plan and prompt the model to revise it [ 18 , 19 ] . However, this process leaves the base planner unchanged, so repeated failures increase token usage, latency, and computation. Moreover, general-purpose LLMs can be too large for memory- and power-limited robot computers. These limitations motivate a planner that combines safety-aware plan generation, compact local inference, and flexible task interfaces. We introduce SLoSa , a neuro-symbolic post-training framework for this combination. Rather than keeping the verifier only in the inference loop, SLoSa uses formal feedback to change the compact planner itself. First, SLoSa constructs behavior-changing safety-aware training data by retaining planning instances in which the supplied constraint rejects the baseline plan while a verified alternative remains feasible. Second, compression-aware supervised fine-tuning (SFT) adapts compact, quantized LLMs using the verified plans. Third, verifier-guided Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) refines the planner with a reward hierarchy that separates unparseable outputs, precondition violations, safety violations, goal failures, and successful plans. At inference, SLoSa combines local plan generation with verifier feedback to enforce the supplied safety constraints while reducing the tokens spent on iterative repair. We evaluate SLoSa using three compact open-source LLM backbones across four PDDL domains that cover manipulation, transportation, object transfer, and tool use, together with separately trained planners for safety-adapted VirtualHome and ALFWorld to evaluate the applicability of the same post-training pipeline beyond PDDL-based planning. The results show consistent improvements from safety-aware post-training across model sizes, task domains, and input representations. We further evaluate local inference on an NVIDIA RTX 4080 and a Jetson Orin, and demonstrate the complete vision-grounded planning pipeline on a desktop 6-degree-of-freedom (6-DoF) robotic arm. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce SLoSa, a neuro-symbolic post-training framework that improves compact LLM planners through behavior-changing safety-aware data construction, compression-aware supervised fine-tuning, and verifier-guided GRPO. • We construct safety-constrained evaluation tasks spanning four PDDL planning domains and two adapted household simulation benchmarks, with formally verified plans and task-specific trajectory constraints. • We conduct extensive experiments across multiple model sizes, task domains, and input representations, showing consistent improvements from safety-aware post-training. We further validate local edge deployment, verifier-guided inference, and physical robotic-arm execution.
