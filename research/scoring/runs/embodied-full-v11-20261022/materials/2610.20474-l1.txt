# AdaRSI: On-Demand Recursive Self-Improvement for Embodied Agents

## Abstract
Pretrained vision-language-action models (VLAs) often fail midway through unseen long-horizon tasks. Recursive self-improvement (RSI) offers a remedy, but embodied RSI methods either refine the harness around a frozen executor and so cannot add a missing motion, or train the executor on persistent failures without asking whether a failure lies in how the executor is called or in what it can do. We ask when a self-improving embodied system should change the weights of its executor, and propose AdaRSI, an on-demand RSI framework for embodied agents. A coding agent first improves its skills and harness, then attributes persistent failures by retrying failing sub-steps from saved states, and fine-tunes only the sub-steps that remain unreliable, with demonstrations it collects itself and experience carried over from earlier tasks. On all 50 target tasks of RoboCasa365, with GR00T N1.5 as the executor and no human demonstrations, AdaRSI improves the VLA on all three suites and raises its success on unseen composite tasks from 4.6% to 13.8%. Compared with fine-tuning every sub-step, it reaches slightly higher success while using only 28% of the GPU time and 22% of the self-collected demonstrations, and fine-tuning without a harness trains more yet gains far less.

## 1 Introduction
Pretrained vision-language-action models (VLAs) [ 21 , 11 , 1 ] can perform a wide range of manipulation tasks in diverse environments. Recent models have also made progress on long-horizon tasks [ 27 , 22 ] . Yet on unseen multi-step tasks they still often fail midway. A robot may approach the wrong target, misjudge whether a step has succeeded, or fail to execute a required motion. Some of these failures lie in how the VLA, acting as the executor, is called and others in what it can do, and the failure itself does not tell which. Recursive self-improvement (RSI), in which a system turns its own experience into persistent changes that make it better [ 4 , 37 , 3 ] , offers a way to repair such failures without collecting human demonstrations for every new task. An embodied system, made of a coding agent, its skills and harness, and a low-level executor such as a VLA, can improve itself at two levels. The harness, comprising the agent’s skills, its tools and the procedure that composes them, decides how the executor is called, while the executor’s weights decide what it can do. Harness-level methods refine the agent’s skills and tools while keeping the executor frozen [ 16 , 6 , 18 , 31 , 33 , 38 , 30 , 26 , 17 , 25 ] . Their changes are cheap, readable and easy to revert, but they stall when a task requires a motion the executor cannot perform [ 33 , 29 ] . Policy-level methods instead update the executor’s weights [ 12 , 9 , 34 , 5 , 15 , 10 ] , some of them directing training toward tasks with low recent success [ 5 ] or toward high-risk scenarios [ 9 ] , and systems that act on both levels train on the data that a refined harness produces [ 32 , 7 ] . None of these methods asks whether a persistent failure lies in how the executor is called or in a motion it lacks, although the two call for different remedies. Training on a failure that lies in how the executor is called is not only costly but also risky, as a fine-tuned policy can perform a sub-step worse than the executor it started from. This raises a question that current embodied RSI leaves open: when should a self-improving system change the weights of its executor? We argue that the answer is a matter of attribution. The weights should change only for a sub-step that stays unreliable after the agent has tried other ways of calling the executor, such as other instructions, robot poses or approaches. Refining the harness first therefore does more than fix the failures that lie in how the executor is called. It also sets the states from which the executor is called, so that a policy trained afterwards learns the motion that is actually missing, from the states in which it will be used. Harness-only improvement is bounded by what the executor can do, while fine-tuning every sub-step is costly and can make sub-steps that already worked worse. AdaRSI refines the harness first, which also sets the states from which policies are trained and called, and fine-tunes only the sub-steps that remain unreliable. We answer this question with AdaRSI , an on-demand recursive self-improvement framework for embodied agents. The agent first refines its harness from execution feedback, so that the harness absorbs the failures that better calling can fix without any training. It then attributes the failures that persist, retrying a failing sub-step from a saved simulator state under different instructions and approaches, which separates a poor way of calling the executor from a motion it cannot make. For the sub-steps that no such retry makes reliable, the agent collects demonstrations in simulation and fine-tunes a sub-step policy from the states its harness reaches. Each new policy is evaluated on held-out start states, and the procedure calls it only where these measurements show that it helps, so a weight change enters the system only after it has been both attributed and validated. Improvements accumulate over rounds, each starting from the system that the previous round produced. Experience and a training budget derived from earlier tasks guide later ones, so the system improves not only itself but also the way it improves. No human demonstrations are used, and privileged simulator state serves only to generate and label data, never at test time. We evaluate on all 50 target tasks of RoboCasa365 [ 19 , 20 ] under the official protocol, with GR00T N1.5 [ 21 ] as the executor. AdaRSI improves the VLA on all three suites, most of all on unseen composite tasks, where it raises success from 4.6% to 13.8%, about three times the base rate. The harness provides most of this gain, and fine-tuning, which executes only 15% of the manipulation steps, carries the tasks that the harness alone could not solve, such as one that requires opening a microwave door the VLA never opens on its own. In paired tests from identical states, fine-tuning teaches motions that the executor lacks, but it can degrade sub-steps that the executor already completes. Compared with fine-tuning every sub-step and calling the new policies by default, AdaRSI reaches slightly higher success while using only 28% of the GPU time and 22% of the self-collected demonstrations. Fine-tuning without a harness trains more yet gains far less, and its deployed system calls few of the policies it trained. Training instead the sub-steps on which the executor succeeds least, under the same budget, gains nothing over the harness alone, even though most of the sub-steps it trains are the same, as attribution also tells the agent from which states a missing motion should be learned. Our contributions are as follows: • We formulate on-demand recursive self-improvement for embodied agents, in which the decision to change the executor’s weights rests on attributing each persistent failure to the harness or to the executor, and instantiate it as AdaRSI. • We find that the harness decides whether training pays off, as it absorbs failures that would otherwise be trained on and sets the states from which policies are trained and called, while fine-tuning teaches motions the executor lacks but can degrade sub-steps it already completes. • We show on all 50 target tasks of RoboCasa365, without any human demonstration, that training on demand improves a pretrained VLA on every suite at a fraction of the training cost of fine-tuning every sub-step.
