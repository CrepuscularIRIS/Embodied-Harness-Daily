# MUCO: A Mani-Unit-Structured Manipulation Framework for Compositional Long-Horizon Robot Tasks

## Abstract
Learning and executing long-horizon robot manipulation as complete task trajectories raises data-collection costs, lim?its capability reuse, and causes local failures to cascade into later task stages. We introduce manipulation units (MUs) as reusable operations, each defined by a local goal, an initi?ation condition, and observable success and failure criteria. MUs are learned and verified individually. During execution, consecutive MUs remain coupled through shared environ?ment dynamics. We formulate MU-Structured Manipulation (MUSM) and realize it through MU-Centric Orchestration (MUCO). Given language and vision, MUCO constructs an MU sequence, matches each MU to compatible policy models in a frozen capability registry, and dispatches the model with the highest calibrated success rate. Visual verification checks MU outcomes against success and failure criteria and governs transitions, enabling retry or fallback to another model from the current state without re-executing verified MUs. Canonical keys enable checkpoint reuse for equivalent MUs across tasks without new demonstrations or updates. We evaluate MUCO on RLBench, RoboTwin 2.0, and five real-world tasks. MUCO achieves higher complete-task success and verified progress, stronger failure containment, and lower error propagation than baselines. Results demonstrate cross-task MU reuse and het?erogeneous policy composition without joint training, shared backbones, or common action representations.

## Introduction
A robot that can grasp a container, open its lid, and pour its contents should be able to recombine those capabilities when asked to prepare a different drink. Under the dominant trajectory-centric paradigm, however, a new ordering or combination is still treated as a new end-to-end task, typically requiring another complete demonstration or another task-level policy. The limitation is not simply that current policies are too small or their action horizons too short. The deeper problem is that the trajectory is asked to play too many roles at once. It is the unit of data collection and policy learning, as well as the unit of execution, evaluation, and recovery. For long-horizon manipulation, however, that unit is too coarse-grained. Vision-language-action (VLA) models and related multimodal policies have substantially broadened the behaviors that robots can express by coupling perception, language, and continuous action generation ( Brohan et al. 2023 ; Kim et al. 2024 ; Physical Intelligence et al. 2025 ; Driess et al. 2023 ; Jiang et al. 2023 ; Open X-Embodiment Collaboration 2024 ) . Yet greater policy capacity does not by itself delineate where one operation ends, whether its intended state change was achieved, or which existing controller should handle the next operation. These questions are of particular importance in laboratory procedures, household activities, and assembly tasks, where an early error changes the physical context faced by every later action ( Mees et al. 2022 ; Jiang et al. 2023 ) . Long-horizon competence therefore requires not only a policy that can act, but also an organization of operation that makes intermediate progress reusable and inspectable. The trajectory-centric formulation couples semantically distinct operations into one data collection, learning, and execution problem. At the data level, collecting demonstrations for a lengthy task requires complete successful rollouts, even when most constituent operations have already appeared in other tasks ( Open X-Embodiment Collaboration 2024 ; Khazatsky et al. 2024 ) . A change in task order or final objective can therefore require collecting another full trajectory. At the capability level, the trajectory-level formulation does not expose the operations learned by a policy model as units that can be selected, verified, and recombined. Therefore, knowing how to grasp, pour, and insert does not by itself enable a robot to execute those operations in a new long-horizon ordering ( Mees et al. 2022 ; Jiang et al. 2023 ) . At execution time, an early failure changes the state encountered by all later operations. Without explicit Mani-Unit boundaries, it is difficult to check whether each operation succeeded or to retry only the failed operation ( Hu et al. 2026 ; Zhang and Shah 2026 ) . This problem arises because learning and execution are organized around complete trajectories rather than reusable Mani-Units. To address these problems, we introduce manipulation units (MUs) as reusable operations, each defined by a local goal, an initiation condition, and observable success and failure criteria. An MU specifies what state change should be achieved, while the selected policy model determines how to achieve it through continuous robot actions. Building on MUs, we formulate MU-Structured Manipulation (MUSM), which represents long-horizon tasks as ordered MU compositions. In MUSM, individually learned and verified MUs are recombined into different task sequences. During execution, the outcome of each MU determines the initial state of the next, so consecutive MUs remain coupled through shared environment dynamics. Data are collected and policy models are trained separately for each MU. The same MU can then be reused across different task sequences and executed by different compatible policy models.| Compared with language-guided planners that primarily organize execution through high-level skill selection or program generation ( Ahn et al. 2022 ; Liang et al. 2023 ; Huang et al. 2022 ) , MUSM does more than generate an execution sequence. MUSM represents long-horizon tasks as ordered compositions of reusable and verifiable MUs, and different tasks are formed by recombining the same MUs. Each MU retains its own data provenance and observable success and failure criteria. MUSM therefore changes both how task sequences are constructed and the granularity at which data are collected, policies are trained, outcomes are verified, and learned capabilities are reused across tasks. We realize MUSM through MU-Centric Orchestration (MUCO) , which comprises four components: an MU constructor that converts task instructions and visual observations into ordered MU records; a matcher backed by a frozen capability registry that identifies compatible policy models and ranks them using calibrated success rates; model-specific adapters that translate observations and MU records into each policy model’s native input; and a visual verifier that evaluates success and failure criteria and governs MU transitions and recovery. During execution, MUCO dispatches the highest-ranked compatible model for the active MU. After verified success, MUCO advances to the next MU. After failure, MUCO retries the current model or falls back to another compatible model from the current environment state without re-executing verified MUs. Our experiments test whether this Mani-Unit-centric organization addresses the limitations of trajectory-centric learning described above. We first examine whether a learned Mani-Unit can be reused in a new task. We then test whether assigning different policy models to different Mani-Units improves task success. Finally, we evaluate whether Mani-Unit verification and retry prevent an early error from affecting the rest of a long-horizon task. Our contributions are: • We identify trajectory granularity as a shared bottleneck in data collection, capability reuse, and failure handling, introduce manipulation units (MUs) as reusable and verifiable operations, and formulate MUSM to represent long-horizon tasks as ordered compositions of MUs that can be recombined across task sequences. • We introduce MUCO, a concrete realization of MUSM that integrates an MU constructor, a matcher backed by a frozen capability registry, model-specific adapters, and a visual verifier for transition control and failure recovery, without jointly retraining the underlying policy models. • We establish an evaluation protocol that separates individual policy-model competence from the central claims of cross-task MU reuse without additional demonstrations, heterogeneous policy composition, and robustness to MU-level failures.
