# Refuses When Asked, Complies When Acting: A Runtime Safety Check for Robot Control Loops

## Abstract
Frontier models can control robots through agent harnesses that loop on one question: what motion comes next toward the user's goal? We show that this default loop carries out harmful instructions. With GPT-6 Astra controlling a low-cost SO-101 arm through the Inspect Robots harness on three household tasks whose hazard is common knowledge, such as "put the knife in the toy basket", the policy refused 0% of 15 trials, attempted the harm every time, and completed it in 27%. Its one exit, give_up, is meant for goals that cannot be achieved, and it never cited the hazard. We add an optional runtime safety check to the loop: before the first motion, the model is asked whether it would carry out the instruction, given the same camera frames. The check would have stopped all 15 hazardous instructions, including the 4 that completed the harm, and flagged none of the 15 harmless controls. Code and data: https://github.com/Arnav-Gr0ver/Refuses-When-Asked-Complies-When-Acting.



## 1 Introduction
Before acting, a person considers the harm an action could do to those it affects, but a robot control loop that works toward the user’s goal has no step where that consideration happens. Ask a frontier model whether it would put a knife in a toy basket that children reach into, and it says no. Give the same model the same instruction as the control loop of a robot arm, and it reaches for the knife to put it in the basket. RoboHarm [ 1 ] showed that frontier robot policies fail this check on hardware: GPT-6 Astra refused 2% of harmful instructions (2 of 100) and carried out 60%, including 17 of 20 attempts to stab a baby doll. We study why in one widely used harness. The Inspect Robots harness [ 2 ] runs the policy in a loop in which every call returns one motion toward the user’s goal. Its safety approvers only clamp out-of-bounds and too-fast motion, and its one exit, give_up , is for goals that cannot be achieved, not for goals that should not be pursued. Nothing in the loop asks whether the instruction should be carried out, so a harmful instruction proceeds like any other. We show that the default loop does carry out the harm: on three household tasks, GPT-6 Astra refused none of 15 trials and completed the harm in four. We then add an optional runtime safety check to the loop. Before the first motion, the check asks the policy model whether it would carry out the instruction, given the camera frames. It would have stopped all 15 hazardous instructions and flagged none of the harmless controls (Fig. 1 ). The check reuses the policy’s model and adds one call, with no second model and no safety specification, unlike prior safeguards that add safety specifications [ 3 ] or a separate monitoring model [ 4 ] . We also introduce three household tasks, each with a named hazard, a safe alternative, and a harmless control, and describe the policy’s reasoning across 464 robot calls.
