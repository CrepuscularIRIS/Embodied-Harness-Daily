# Compile Once, Run Cheap: Frozen, Portable Skills for Embodied Agents, and What Recursive Self-Improvement Adds

## Abstract
Embodied AI lacks the data-rich pretraining that gave language models their agents and skills, so new manipulation capability costs demonstrations per task or a frontier model in every episode. We study a third route: a strong general model acts only as an offline author that writes, runs, diagnoses and repairs a manipulation skill in simulation until it passes an exam on layouts drawn after freezing; the frozen skill then runs as code alone or under a cheap caller that reads only its description. We call this skill-level recursive self-improvement Skill-RSI and specify a compile protocol (isolated, audited author; hidden validation layouts; no-feedback and inherit-only controls; tamper-evident ledger) and a reuse protocol that runs the identical files with executors from another model family. On 7 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks with cluttered tables, Claude Code compiled 4 tasks into 7 frozen skills, 5 from a first draft needing only qualification, at USD 4.29-8.22 of author cost each (USD 55 per skill counting the 13 failed campaigns). The other three tasks did not compile in 18 campaigns, including five with GPT-6 and a pre-registered 150-layout run: on the hardest task every campaign scored 78-95 of 100 but had 3-16 safety aborts, failing the gate of 95% success with zero aborts; a 100-episode exam bounds an abort rate only to 3.6%. On hidden layouts, repair that inherits the previous version beat from-scratch rewriting in 8 of 8 controlled campaigns (sign test p = 0.004); an inherit-only lineage attributes most of the gap to inheritance, with development feedback adding a little (3 of 3, p = 0.125). Write-once campaigns found no detectable first-draft effect of practice (minimum detectable effect 14 points). The frozen skills kept 148/150 successes on new layouts with no model and 147/150 with a DeepSeek-flash caller at USD 0.00028-0.00054 per episode; no direct-control baseline was run.



## 1 Introduction
The capabilities that make language models useful as agents were built in a fixed order. Web-scale pretraining produced a strong base model first; agent loops, tool harnesses, reusable skills and protocols for calling them came afterwards, on top of that model. Embodied intelligence is trying to repeat the first step and has no corpus with which to do it. Robot foundation policies consolidate demonstrations, but each new task still asks for new demonstrations or post-training [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . A second route places a frontier multimodal model inside a structured harness and lets it reason per episode; it solves tasks zero-shot but keeps the expensive model in the loop for every execution [ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] . A third route lets a frozen model evolve skills, critics or code through execution and revision [ 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 ] . The third route produces a loop; it does not yet say what kind of object the loop produces or how its claims should be tested. Prior and concurrent systems have begun to evaluate frozen experience on scenes the loop did not see [ 14 , 15 ] , but at execution time each episode still runs a frontier model, at a few dollars per episode where the cost is reported [ 14 ] , and neither of these systems separates learning from feedback from selection among attempts with a control. Three questions follow. First, can the product of the loop run without the model that wrote it, and with what loss when a cheaper model from another family calls it? Second, how much of the improvement across rounds is learning from feedback, how much is inheriting a previous version, and how much is trying several times and keeping the best? Third, what does the skill cost, counted as the full ledger of authoring, failed candidates, simulation and deployment, so that the claim that a skill is cheap has a denominator? We take the position that the order of the language-model history can be inverted for robots: skills can come before the strong embodied base model, be compiled by a strong general model that already exists, and let robots start working, and generating structured data, in specific scenes now. This is a hypothesis, not a result. Its necessary precondition is empirical: a skill must be compiled cheaply enough, qualify on unseen layouts, run without the author, and cost little per episode. This paper tests that precondition. Skill-RSI separates authoring from execution ( Figure 1 ). In Protocol A a strong model, whose weights never change, acts as an offline author: it drafts a skill package from a task card, runs it on fixed development layouts, reads the execution evidence, and revises, until the package meets a target on those layouts and on hidden validation layouts whose results it never sees. A qualification exam on layouts drawn only after freezing decides whether the package becomes a FrozenSkill , an immutable bundle of a skill description, restricted-Python skill code, a perception module and a manifest with hashes, seeds, environment fingerprint and compile cost. In Protocol B the same bundle is executed by a code-only runner and by a caller from another model family that reads only the skill description, on layouts disjoint from every compile-time seed. We ask: Q1 How many repair rounds and how much author cost does it take to compile a task to a 95\% qualification bar with zero unsafe motions, and what stops the tasks that do not compile? Q2 Is the improvement across rounds learning from feedback, or selection among independent attempts; within a repair round, how much comes from inheriting the previous version and how much from reading its execution evidence; and does the author’s own testing within a session contribute? Q3 When the frozen skill is executed by code alone or by a low-cost caller from another model family, how much success is retained? Q4 What does a frozen skill cost per episode, with compilation amortized over deployments? Our contributions are: (i) an author–executor separation in which the unit of adaptation is a verified, portable FrozenSkill that executes with no model at all or with a cheap caller from another family, with two protocols that operationalize compile-once-run-cheap; (ii) a credible measurement of what self-improvement contributes: an isolated and audited author, hidden validation layouts, exam layouts drawn after freezing and run to completion, no-feedback and inherit-only control lineages re-run on hidden layouts under a pre-registered design, and a per-decision cost ledger that includes every failed campaign; and (iii) measurements on RoboTwin 2.0 with two author products that answer Q1–Q4, including three findings we did not anticipate: the frozen skills came mostly from first drafts, so the protocol’s measured contribution on the tasks that compiled is qualification and freezing; the gate binds on rare safety aborts rather than on task completion, and more development layouts did not change that; and most of the per-round advantage of repair over rewriting comes from inheriting the previous version rather than from the runtime’s development-layout feedback on it, while in-session self-testing shows no detectable effect on first drafts. We use the name Skill-RSI because the author recursively refines a persistent external skill from that skill’s own execution evidence; Section 3.3 states precisely what is and is not claimed under that name.
